# Daytime and evening lifestyle behaviours and adolescent sleep: screens/phones, social media, gaming, caffeine and energy drinks, exercise, meals, nicotine/alcohol, late studying

*Target person: 16-year-old male high-school student, fixed weekday wake-up ~06:30–07:30; problems: slow sleep onset, night awakenings, unrefreshing sleep with daytime sleepiness. Scope: behaviour/habits, bedroom set-up and consumer gadgets/apps only (no supplements, medication or clinician-delivered therapy). Light physiology (screen light, blue-light filters), sleep schedule/naps, CBT-I/relaxation, bedroom environment, trackers/apps and medical red flags are covered by other researchers and are touched on here only where needed to explain mechanisms.*

*Evidence grades used below: **Strong** = consistent RCTs / meta-analyses of experiments in adolescents; **Moderate** = consistent adolescent observational meta-analyses plus some experimental or intervention data, or good experimental/RCT evidence from adults with plausible transfer; **Weak** = small or uncontrolled trials, single observational studies, or conflicting findings; **Expert opinion only** = guideline/expert statements without direct trial support for the specific threshold. "ADULT EVIDENCE" marks findings not obtained in 13–19-year-olds. Abbreviations: TST total sleep time; SOL sleep-onset latency; WASO wake after sleep onset; SE sleep efficiency; SWS slow-wave (deep) sleep; OR odds ratio; ARR adjusted risk ratio; MVPA moderate-to-vigorous physical activity.*

## 1. Screens and phones: what do meta-analyses and recent (2020–2026) studies show about bedtime media use and sleep, which mechanism dominates, and what about phones in the bedroom and night-time use ("vamping")?

### Takeaway
Bedtime screen/phone use and even mere bedroom access are consistently associated with shorter, poorer sleep and daytime sleepiness in adolescents (ORs ~1.5–2.7; longitudinal r ≈ −0.06 to −0.33), and the best objective 2024 data show the damage is concentrated in screen use **once in bed** (especially interactive use: ≈ −9 min of sleep per 10 min), pointing to **time displacement of sleep onset** (plus some cognitive arousal from interactive content) as the dominant mechanism; notifications and checking the phone after waking add a separate pathway to night awakenings, while the light mechanism did not reach expert consensus.

### Cited Findings

**Classic systematic reviews / meta-analyses (mostly cross-sectional)**
- Hale & Guan 2015 (Sleep Med Rev) reviewed 67 studies published 1999–early 2014; screen time was adversely associated with sleep (primarily shortened duration and delayed timing) in 90% of studies; by device the share of studies with adverse associations was computer 94%, unspecified screen time 91%, video games 86%, mobile devices 83%, TV 76% — the authors note this ranking is consistent with interactive screen time being more detrimental than passive TV; causality was not confirmed — [Hale & Guan 2015, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4437561/)
- Carter et al. 2016 (JAMA Pediatrics) meta-analysis: 20 (all cross-sectional) studies, 125,198 children aged 6–19 (mean age 14.5); bedtime portable-device **use** → inadequate sleep quantity OR 2.17 (95% CI 1.42–3.32; I² = 90%), poor sleep quality OR 1.46 (1.14–1.88), excessive daytime sleepiness OR 2.72 (1.32–5.61). Children with bedtime **access but no use** also had worse outcomes: OR 1.79 (1.39–2.31), 1.53 (1.11–2.10) and 2.27 (1.54–3.35) respectively. Prevalence of inadequate sleep 45.4% (users) vs 31.5% (no access); excessive sleepiness 21.3% vs 6.7% — [Carter et al. 2016, JAMA Pediatrics](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2571467)
- Hysing et al. 2015 (BMJ Open; youth@hordaland, n = 9,846 aged 16–19, cross-sectional): phone use in the last hour before bed → SOL ≥ 60 min OR 1.48 (1.30–1.68), weekday sleep deficit ≥ 2 h OR 1.35 (1.17–1.55), sleep < 5 h OR 1.85 (1.45–2.35) vs 8–9 h; > 4 h of leisure screen time per day → SOL ≥ 60 min OR 1.49 (1.36–1.64) and sleep < 5 h OR 3.64 (3.06–4.33); dose–response pattern — [Hysing et al. 2015, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4316480/)
- Bartel, Gradisar & Williamson 2015 (Sleep Med Rev) meta-analysis of 41 studies (85,561 adolescents aged 12–18): video gaming, phone, computer and internet use and evening light were related to delayed bedtimes; computer use (with tobacco, evening light, negative family environment and caffeine) was associated with decreased TST — [Bartel et al. 2015](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/protective-and-risk-factors-for-adolescent-sleep-a-meta-analytic-/)

**Longitudinal and 2023–2026 syntheses**
- Pagano, Bacaro & Crocetti 2023 (Computers in Human Behavior) meta-analysis of 23 **longitudinal** adolescent studies (N = 116,431; mean baseline age 13.4): traditional media use (r = −0.33), social media use (r = −0.12), prolonged use (r = −0.06) and dysfunctional/problematic use (r = −0.19) predicted worse later sleep health; in the reverse direction poor sleep predicted later dysfunctional use (r = −0.22) but not later social-media use (r = −0.05) — a "vicious cycle" — [Pagano et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2023.107813)
- Ahmed et al. 2024 (J Affect Disord): 182 studies (n = 1,169,396) in the systematic review and 98 in meta-analyses; problematic social-media use was positively associated with sleep problems; associations were small and heterogeneous — [Ahmed et al. 2024, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39242043/)
- A 2026 scoping review of recent longitudinal studies in youth found ~70% showed a negative impact of social-media use on bedtime and SOL, strongest for problematic use and use at bedtime, with heavy reliance on self-report — [Scoping review 2026, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2451958825003239)
- National Sleep Foundation (NSF) consensus 2024 (Hartstein et al., Sleep Health; 16-member panel, modified Delphi; reviewed 35 experimental/intervention studies and 5 systematic reviews): consensus reached that (1) screen use in general impairs sleep health in children **and adolescents**, (2) the **content** of pre-sleep screen use impairs sleep in children and adolescents, (3) behavioural strategies can reduce negative effects. **No consensus** that light from pre-sleep screens impairs sleep (light effects "typically small or the consequence of laboratory design"), no consensus for adults, and blue-light filtering "consistently showed only minimal improvements". The panel did **not** address timing/duration thresholds; it advised "sensible reduction in overall screen use coupled with avoidance of highly stimulating and interactive content during the presleep wind-down window" — [NSF consensus 2024, Sleep Health](https://www.sleephealthjournal.org/article/S2352-7218(24)00090-1/fulltext); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38806392/)

**Objective night-by-night evidence (best mechanistic data)**
- Brosnan et al. 2024 (JAMA Pediatrics; n = 79 aged 11–14, mean 12.9; video cameras from 2 h before bed until "shut-eye", wrist actigraphy, 4 nights): screen use in the 2 h **before getting into bed** had no association with TST (0 min per extra 10 min; 95% CI −3 to 20) because later sleep onset was matched by later wake-up; screen use **once in bed** was associated with −3 min TST per 10 min of total screen use, **−9 min per 10 min of interactive use**, −4 min per 10 min passive use, **−17 min per 10 min of gaming**, and −35 min on nights with vs without multitasking; social media in bed was associated with 38-min later sleep onset on nights with vs without it. Screen use extended "shut-eye latency" (time in bed before trying to sleep) but was **not** associated with sleep latency once the youth tried to sleep. Authors: blanket advice to stop all screens an hour before bed "seem[s] neither achievable nor appropriate"; focus on limiting interactive in-bed use — [Brosnan et al. 2024, JAMA Pediatrics](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2822859); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39226046/)
- ADULT/young-adult evidence — Hjetland et al. 2025 (Frontiers in Psychiatry; SHoT2022, n = 45,202 Norwegian students aged 18–28, cross-sectional): each additional hour of screen use after going to bed → 59% higher odds of insomnia symptoms (OR 1.59) and 24 min shorter sleep; the association did **not** differ between social media and other screen activities — the authors conclude public messaging should target total in-bed screen time rather than specific activities — [Hjetland et al. 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1548273/full)
- Perrault et al. 2019 (Sleep; Geneva adolescents 12–19): at baseline, screen time after 21:00 correlated with later sleep onset (R² = 0.35) and shorter sleep (R² = 0.23), whereas time spent on **off-screen** activities after 21:00 was unrelated to sleep duration (R² = 0.001); because school wake times were fixed, the screen effect on duration came "primarily" through later sleep onset — [Perrault et al. 2019, Sleep](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsz125); [preprint](https://doi.org/10.1101/259309)

**Mechanism-specific evidence**
- Bauducco et al. 2024 (Sleep Med Rev, theoretical review) re-evaluated the four classic mechanisms (bright light, arousal, night-time sleep disruptions, sleep displacement) and added a **reverse** direction: technology used as a "time filler" while the delayed adolescent clock keeps teens awake, and as an emotion-regulation tool. Daily-diary studies showed day-to-day variation in technology use associated with only "trivial" changes in sleep onset and duration; in 684 adolescents, 62% used technology to distract from negative thoughts, and they reported longer sleep latency; in an older survey, using technology as a sleep aid (except books) was associated with later bedtimes, shorter sleep and more tiredness — [Bauducco et al. 2024 (full text)](https://researchnow-admin.flinders.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/116753136/Bauducco_Bidirectional_P2024.pdf); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.101933)
- Arousal: in lab gaming experiments heart rate did not differ between 150- and 50-min gaming (King et al. 2013) and gaming changed cognitive alertness (EEG alpha power) but not heart rate (Weaver et al. 2010) — i.e., cognitive rather than physiological arousal, with small SOL effects (see §4) — [King et al. 2013, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23137332/); [Weaver et al. 2010, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2854707/)
- Social-media pathway: in adolescents, night-time social-media use was associated with later bedtimes, higher pre-sleep cognitive arousal, longer SOL and shorter sleep; path analysis supported fear of missing out (FoMO) acting (1) behaviourally by driving late-night use that delays bedtime and (2) cognitively by raising pre-sleep arousal — [Scott & Woods 2018, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30031979/)
- Finnish adolescents (n = 1,084, mean age 16.9): using any digital media after 22:00 (vs before 21:00) was associated with shorter school-night sleep; > 10 min of media use after lights-out was associated with more school-day tiredness; effects were most pronounced in evening chronotypes — [Kortesoja et al. 2022, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9842555/)

**Phone in the bedroom, notifications, night-time use ("vamping")**
- ABCD study, cross-sectional (n = 10,280 aged 10–14): 63.2% had a TV/internet device in the bedroom; only 54.9% turned the phone off overnight; 16.9% were woken by calls/texts/e-mails at least once in the past week and 20.0% used a device after waking at night. Device in bedroom → trouble falling/staying asleep ARR 1.27 (1.12–1.44); leaving the ringer on vs turning the phone off → ARR 1.23 (1.03–1.46); being woken by messages → ARR 1.28 (1.19–1.39); using a device after waking at night → ARR 1.34 (1.25–1.43); in-bed activities ranged from ARR 1.08 (laptop for study) to 1.27 (chat rooms), video games 1.22 — [Nagata et al. 2023, Sleep Health (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10823798/)
- ABCD prospective follow-up (n = 9,398 aged 11–12, outcomes 1 year later): ringer left on vs phone off → caregiver-reported sleep disturbance OR 1.25 (1.00–1.56) and shorter self-reported sleep (B = −0.27); silent/vibrate vs off → shorter sleep (B = −0.15); **putting the phone outside the bedroom → no shorter sleep (B = +0.05, n.s.)**; talking/texting, music and social media in bed predicted shorter sleep and more disorders of initiating/maintaining sleep; reading in bed was associated with **longer** sleep (B = +0.07) — [Nagata et al. 2024, J Adolesc Health](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2024.06.006)
- Van den Bulck 2007 (Sleep; prospective, n = 1,656 Flemish pupils, mean ages 13.7 and 16.9): only 38% never used their phone after lights-out; odds of being "very tired" one year later rose with frequency of calling/texting after lights-out: OR 1.8 (< 1×/month), 2.2 (< 1×/week), 3.3 (~weekly), 5.1 (> 1×/week); use between 00:00 and 03:00 → OR 3.9; ~35% of "very tired" cases attributed to after-lights-out phone use; author: "no safe dose and no safe time" — [Van den Bulck 2007, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1978406/)
- Japan national survey (n = 94,777 adolescents): 8.3% called and 17.6% texted after lights-out every day; this was associated with short sleep, poor subjective sleep quality, excessive daytime sleepiness and insomnia symptoms, though effect sizes were small — [Munezawa et al. 2011, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21804663/)
- Common Sense Media 2023 ("Constant Companion"; passive phone tracking of 203 US 11–17-year-olds for one week, Android only): median 237 notifications/day, ~5% arriving in school-night hours (00:00–05:00); 59% used their phone between midnight and 05:00 on school nights (median ~20 min/night; range < 1 min to 5 h); 67% had night-time pickups; school-night use was dominated by YouTube (47%), social media (39%) and gaming (29%); older teens (16–17) used phones overnight more; youth advisers described TikTok as overstimulating at bedtime — [Common Sense Media 2023 report (PDF)](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/sites/default/files/research/report/2023-cs-smartphone-research-report_final-for-web.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Dominant mechanism for this teen: displacement of sleep onset ("one more video/match/chat"), amplified by cognitive arousal from interactive content.** With a fixed 06:30–07:30 school wake-up, every minute that sleep onset is pushed later is a minute of sleep lost (Perrault: duration effect ran through later onset; Brosnan: in-bed screen time was not compensated by later wake-up). Interactive content (gaming, messaging, multitasking, social feeds) costs roughly 2× more sleep per minute than passive viewing.
- **Night awakenings pathway:** notifications/ringer-on and checking the phone after a spontaneous awakening are associated with ~20–35% higher risk of trouble falling or staying asleep (ABCD). For a teen whose complaint includes night awakenings, overnight phone availability is a plausible, modifiable contributor even if total screen time is moderate.
- **"Access without use" matters** (Carter: OR 1.79 for insufficient sleep) — consistent with temptation/notification effects, which supports removing the phone from reach rather than relying on willpower.
- **Light is not the main lever** for this age group according to the 2024 NSF consensus; changing content/timing/location of use is more important than filters (light details belong to the light-physiology notes).
- **Bidirectionality:** a teen who struggles to fall asleep may use the phone to fill time or calm rumination; the phone then delays sleep further. Rules should therefore pair removal of the phone with a non-screen replacement activity (see §3).
- **Magnitude:** cross-sectional ORs (1.5–2.7) look large, but longitudinal and within-person effects are small (r ≈ −0.06 to −0.12 for social media/time; "trivial" day-to-day effects in diaries). Practically, the important exposures are *in-bed* use, *interactive* use and *overnight* availability — not total daily screen time per se.
- Evidence grade for "bedtime/in-bed screen use and overnight phone access worsen adolescent sleep": **Moderate** (large consistent observational base + objective repeated-measures data + expert consensus; few RCTs).

### Gaps
- No adolescent RCT isolates notifications or "phone outside the bedroom" as a single manipulated factor; evidence is observational.
- Short-form video (TikTok/Reels/Shorts) specifically: I found no adolescent meta-analysis isolating it; evidence is qualitative (Common Sense advisers) or bundled under "social media".
- Light vs content vs displacement have not been separated experimentally in 13–19-year-olds (NSF).
- Most outcome data are self-reported; objective data (Brosnan) are from 11–14-year-olds, not 16-year-olds.
- Whether the social-media-specific harm is larger than other screen activities is contested (Nagata/Scott & Woods suggest social/chat activities are worse; Hjetland in young adults found no difference; Pagano found traditional media r = −0.33 vs social media r = −0.12).

## 2. Intervention trials: what happened when teenagers restricted phone/screen use before bed?

### Takeaway
Uncontrolled pre–post trials in adolescents show that an evening screen curfew (no screens after 21:00 on school nights, or phone off 1 h before bed) moves lights-out/sleep onset ~17–21 min earlier and adds ~17–21 min of sleep per school night without changing wake time or SOL; randomized evidence is thin — a family RCT in mostly pre-teen children found no EEG-sleep benefit, and a 2026 pilot RCT of total smartphone/social-media abstinence in 13–18-year-olds found only tentative, non-durable gains — so the effect is real but modest and depends on compliance.

### Cited Findings
- **Perrault et al. 2019 (Sleep; Switzerland; non-randomized, no control group):** 569 adolescents (12–19) recruited; 2-week baseline → 40-min sleep-education workshop → 2 weeks asked to stop screen use after 21:00 on school nights; 183 "active" participants with actigraphy + diaries. School-night bedtime moved from 23:28 to 23:07 and sleep duration rose from 7 h 33 min to 7 h 50 min (**+17 ± 2 min**); wake-up/out-of-bed times did not change; the increase was significant mainly in 14–19-year-olds; better compliance (larger reduction in screen time) correlated with earlier sleep onset (R² = 0.133) and longer sleep (R² = 0.112); daytime vigilance improved (faster slowest reaction times) — [Perrault et al. 2019, Sleep](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsz125); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31260534/); [preprint with n = 569](https://doi.org/10.1101/259309)
- **Bartel, Scheeren & Gradisar 2019 (Health Communication; Australia; single-arm pre–post, n = 63 adolescents):** instructed to stop phone use 1 h before bed on school nights for one week; participants stopped phone use ~80 min earlier, turned lights off earlier and slept **21 min longer**, but did not go to bed earlier and did not fall asleep faster (no SOL change) — [Dibben et al. 2023 systematic review, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10909457/); lights-off ~17 min earlier as summarized by [He et al. 2020, PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0228756)
- Dibben et al. 2023 (J Sleep Res, systematic review of prospective studies): only three interventional studies targeted pre-bedtime device use in adolescents, none randomized; "modest but promising" results — [Dibben et al. 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10909457/)
- **SCREENS cluster RCT (Pedersen et al. 2022, JAMA Pediatrics; Denmark):** 89 families, 181 children (mean age 8.6–9.5; eligible ages 4–14) and 164 adults; leisure screen use limited to ≤ 3 h/week for 2 weeks with smartphones/tablets handed in. Children's leisure non-sedentary activity rose by 45.8 min/day vs control, but there were **no significant effects on EEG-measured TST, SOL, WASO or sleep stages in children** (adults showed increased sleep duration only in post hoc analyses) — [Pedersen et al. 2022, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9127712/). A secondary analysis found improved behavioural difficulties (SDQ total, Cohen's d = 0.53) — [Schmidt-Persson et al. 2024, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11245724/)
- **Sullivan, Cairney & Henderson 2026 (Child and Adolescent Mental Health; preregistered pilot RCT, UK):** 82 adolescents aged 13–18 randomized to 21-day total smartphone/social-media detox (n = 26), detox with a basic "brick" phone (n = 26) or control (n = 30); 13% attrition; at 21 days one or both detox groups showed **tentative** improvements in sleep duration and daytime sleepiness (plus mood, stress, working memory) that were **not maintained at 2 months**; many participants reported boredom, functional reliance on phones and FoMO; the authors recommend testing targeted approaches such as switching off internet at bedtime — [Sullivan et al. 2026, CAMH](https://doi.org/10.1111/camh.70118); [preprint 2025](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/ujv78_v1)
- Conference abstract (weak evidence): 220 youth (median age 17–19) with elevated anxiety/depression randomized to reduce smartphone social-media use to ~1 h/day (≈50% reduction, objectively tracked) for 3 weeks vs no restriction: greater increases in sleep duration and reductions in depression/anxiety/FoMO; increased sleep mediated the mood effect — [Davis et al. 2023, JAACAP abstract](https://www.jaacap.org/article/S0890-8567(23)01629-5/fulltext)
- ADULT/young-adult evidence — He et al. 2020 (PLOS One; RCT, n = 38 Chinese college students with poor sleep and > 30 min pre-bed phone use): no phone use in the 30 min before bedtime for 4 weeks (enforced with the phone's built-in "bedtime"/Screen Time lock where possible) reduced SOL by ~12 min, increased sleep duration by ~18 min, improved PSQI and reduced pre-sleep arousal vs control — [He et al. 2020, PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0228756)
- NSF consensus summary of the intervention literature: interventions reducing evening screen use among adolescents were associated with earlier sleep onset and longer sleep; overall evidence "mixed, with many studies that produced null results. However, strategies that targeted evening interactive screen use were generally successful" — [NSF consensus 2024](https://www.sleephealthjournal.org/article/S2352-7218(24)00090-1/fulltext)

### Inferences
- Realistic expected benefit for this teen from a firm evening screen curfew on school nights: **~15–25 min more sleep per night and an earlier sleep onset**, mostly via earlier lights-out, not via faster falling asleep; benefits scale with compliance.
- A **targeted** restriction (evening/bedtime, interactive content) is more promising and more acceptable than total abstinence, which teens find hard and whose effects faded after the detox ended.
- The null SCREENS sleep result likely reflects younger children (mean ~9 y) with less late-evening device use and a daytime-focused limit, so it does not strongly contradict the adolescent pre–post data — but it is a reminder that RCT confirmation is lacking.
- Evidence grade for "an evening screen curfew lengthens adolescent sleep": **Moderate** (consistent but non-randomized adolescent trials + one young-adult RCT + expert consensus); for "total digital detox": **Weak** (pilot RCT, non-durable).

### Gaps
- No fully powered adolescent RCT of a 1-h pre-bed curfew or phone-free bedroom with objective sleep measurement was found as of October 2026.
- No trial reported effects on night awakenings specifically.
- Long-term maintenance (> 2 weeks) of curfew effects in adolescents is unknown.

## 3. Practical screen rules: how long before bed to stop, phone outside the bedroom, Do Not Disturb/bedtime modes, app limits, what to replace scrolling with — evidence vs expert opinion

### Takeaway
The rules with the best support are "no screens once in bed", "phone not used and not reachable overnight (ideally outside the bedroom, notifications silenced)" and "stop interactive screen activities in the last hour before lights-out"; the specific "1 hour" figure, Do Not Disturb/Downtime settings, app limits and particular replacement activities are recommended by the AAP and supported by young-adult trials, but have not been tested as single components in adolescent RCTs.

### Cited Findings
- AAP policy (2016 media policy statement for school-aged children and adolescents, presented by Moreno): "Recommend that children not sleep with devices in their bedrooms, including TVs, computers, and smartphones. Avoid exposure to devices or screens for 1 hour before bedtime"; designate media-free locations (e.g., bedrooms) — [AAP policy statement slides (Moreno)](https://www.nationalacademies.org/cdn/materials/9fba0cb9-79b4-4ab0-bb07-b1b3ba56eb5e). The AAP Family Media Plan (updated 2024) operationalizes such rules (screen-free zones/times) as a family tool — [AAP Family Media Plan 2024, Pediatrics](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/154/6/e2024067417/199968/The-Family-Media-Plan)
- AAP Center of Excellence Q&A (2023): avoid screens 1 hour before bed; keep phones and screens out of the child's bedroom at night ("it can be hard to stop scrolling or watching short videos"); choose relaxing rather than action-packed content for evening viewing; teens 14–17 should aim for 8–10 h of sleep — [AAP Q&A: Screen time affecting sleep](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/screen-time-affecting-sleep/)
- AAP Q&A (2024) tips for teens: charge the phone somewhere it cannot be reached from bed; use phone settings to silence notifications at night; no social media in the hour before bedtime; replace with showering, choosing tomorrow's outfit, reading, journaling, listening to a podcast or art; schedule social-media time earlier in the evening — [AAP Q&A: Sleep quality and social media](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/sleep-quality-and-social-media/)
- Evidence on location: phone outside the bedroom was not associated with shorter sleep 1 year later, while ringer-on (OR 1.25 for disturbance) and even silent/vibrate (B = −0.15 h) were, relative to phone off — [Nagata et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2024.06.006); mere bedtime access to a device: OR 1.79 for inadequate sleep — [Carter et al. 2016](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2571467)
- Evidence on timing: objective data show **in-bed** screen use, not use in the 2 h before getting into bed, predicted less sleep; the authors call for replacing the blanket "no screens for an hour before bed" rule with limits on interactive in-bed use — [Brosnan et al. 2024](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2822859). In contrast, cross-sectional data link device use in the last hour before bed with SOL ≥ 60 min (phone OR 1.48) — [Hysing et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4316480/). Clock-time curfew (21:00) and 1-h-before-bed curfews both lengthened sleep in pre–post trials — [Perrault et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsz125); [Dibben et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10909457/)
- The NSF panel explicitly did not set a timing/duration threshold — [NSF consensus 2024](https://www.sleephealthjournal.org/article/S2352-7218(24)00090-1/fulltext)
- Built-in phone tools — ADULT/young-adult evidence: in the He et al. RCT, the phone's own "bedtime" lock (Screen Time on Apple/Huawei, password-protected; screen turns grey and apps cannot open) was used to enforce a 30-min pre-bed restriction that improved SOL (~−12 min) and duration (~+18 min) — [He et al. 2020](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0228756). A nudge-based RCT (Olson et al. 2022; Study 2, n = 70 adults) combining disabling non-essential notifications, keeping the phone silent (vibration off) and out of reach at bedtime, greyscale and hiding social apps reduced screen time by ~1 h/day and improved sleep quality (+1.23 vs −0.60 points on a 0–10 scale vs a screen-time-monitoring control) — [Olson et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11469-022-00826-w)
- Replacement activities: reading in bed was associated with longer sleep in ABCD (B = +0.07) — [Nagata et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jadohealth.2024.06.006); using technology as a sleep aid was associated with later bedtimes and shorter sleep **except books** — [Bauducco et al. 2024](https://researchnow-admin.flinders.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/116753136/Bauducco_Bidirectional_P2024.pdf); many teens use YouTube/music overnight as "white noise", with YouTube running the longest overnight — [Common Sense Media 2023](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/sites/default/files/research/report/2023-cs-smartphone-research-report_final-for-web.pdf)
- For teens who use devices to cope with negative thoughts at night, removing screens without an alternative "might ultimately have a negative impact on their sleep because of an increase in negative thoughts and no tools to handle them" (theoretical) — [Bauducco et al. 2024](https://researchnow-admin.flinders.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/116753136/Bauducco_Bidirectional_P2024.pdf)

### Inferences
Specific screen rules for the target teen (example uses lights-out 22:30; shift all times with the actual target bedtime):

| Rule | Specific setting | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Bed is screen-free: no phone/tablet/laptop/console use once in bed | From getting into bed to morning alarm | **Moderate** (objective repeated-measures + large observational + young-adult data) |
| Phone not reachable overnight | Charge outside the bedroom (or at minimum across the room); use a stand-alone alarm clock | **Weak–moderate** (observational: access OR 1.79; outside-room not linked to shorter sleep; no adolescent RCT) |
| Silence the phone overnight | Phone off, or Do Not Disturb/Sleep Focus with **no sound and no vibration**, allowing only a few emergency contacts | **Weak** (ABCD ringer-on ARR 1.23/OR 1.25; silent/vibrate still worse than off) |
| Evening device curfew for interactive content | Stop gaming, messaging, social feeds, short-video scrolling and multitasking ≥ 60 min before lights-out (21:30 for 22:30) | **Moderate** that an evening curfew helps (+17–21 min sleep in pre–post trials); the exact 60-min figure is **expert opinion** (AAP) |
| All-screen curfew | Optional but simplest: all screens off 30–60 min before lights-out; if any screen is used in this window, keep it passive (a calm show) and out of bed | **Expert opinion** for the threshold; Brosnan suggests passive pre-bed viewing outside bed is low-risk |
| Enforce with built-in tools rather than willpower | iOS Screen Time "Downtime"/Sleep Focus or Android Digital Wellbeing "Bedtime mode", scheduled e.g. 21:30–07:00, preferably with a parent-held passcode; app limits on the most "sticky" apps (short-video, games) in the evening | **Weak** (young-adult RCTs; no adolescent trial) |
| Don't check the phone during night awakenings | Phone out of reach makes this automatic; if awake, use a non-screen activity | **Weak** (cross-sectional ARR 1.34) |
| Replace scrolling with a fixed wind-down routine | Shower, prepare bag/clothes, paper book or e-ink reader, journaling/to-do list, calm audio on a timer from a non-interactive device | **Weak / expert opinion** (reading associated with longer sleep; AAP tips) |
| Move social time earlier | Schedule chatting/social media/gaming for after homework but before the curfew; tell friends you are offline after the curfew (addresses FoMO) | **Expert opinion** (AAP tip; FoMO mechanism from observational path analysis) |

- If the teen relies on the phone to calm worries at night, the replacement routine (and relaxation/CBT-I techniques covered by other researchers) should be introduced at the same time as the phone ban.

### Gaps
- No adolescent study tested Do Not Disturb, greyscale, app timers or "Downtime" individually with objective sleep outcomes.
- The optimal curfew length (30 vs 60 vs 90 min) has not been compared experimentally in adolescents.
- Whether audio (music/podcasts) at sleep onset helps or hurts adolescent sleep is unresolved (it is common; evidence is mixed/observational).

## 4. Gaming: effects of evening gaming (duration, arousal, competitive/violent content) on sleep latency and quality; bedtime procrastination ("revenge bedtime procrastination") in teens

### Takeaway
In lab studies of adolescent boys, a normal 50–60-min gaming session before bed lengthens sleep onset only slightly (minutes), but prolonged gaming (150 min) cut TST by ~27 min, lowered sleep efficiency below 85% and reduced REM sleep — and gaming is not self-limiting; in real life gaming in bed is among the most sleep-costly activities (≈ −17 min sleep per 10 min). Bedtime procrastination driven by problematic smartphone use mediates part of the phone–poor-sleep link in teens and predicts shorter sleep 6 months later.

### Cited Findings
- **King et al. 2013 (J Sleep Res; lab, within-subject, n = 17 boys aged 16 ± 1 without sleep problems):** fast-paced violent game for 150 vs 50 min ending just before usual bedtime → objective SE −7 ± 2% (falling below 85%), TST **−27 ± 12 min**, near-moderate REM reduction (d = 0.48), subjective SOL +17 ± 8 min and worse subjective sleep quality (d = 0.53); heart rate did not differ — [King et al. 2013, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23137332/). A systematic review notes objective SOL increased by only 3.5 min, gaming ended 10 min before usual bedtime, and after 50 min players wanted a further 37 min of play (vs 14 min after 150 min) — "unmonitored regular videogaming seems unlikely to be self-limiting" — [Peracchia & Curcio 2018 review, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6361300/)
- **Weaver et al. 2010 (J Clin Sleep Med; lab, n = 13 male "evening-type" adolescents, mean 16.6 y):** 50 min of pre-sleep gaming vs passive DVD → SOL median 7.5 vs 3 min (statistically significant but "only slightly"), reduced subjective sleepiness, no change in REM or SWS; associated with cognitive alertness (EEG alpha power), not heart rate; "the direct effect ... may be more modest than previously thought" — [Weaver et al. 2010, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2854707/)
- Earlier experiments summarized by Weaver et al.: 60 min of gaming at 18:00–19:00 in 11 young adolescents (mean 13.5) increased SOL by 21.7 min (Dworak et al.); ADULT EVIDENCE: 2 h 45 min of gaming in 7 young adults increased SOL by only 2.3 min (Higuchi et al.) — [Weaver et al. 2010, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2854707/)
- Real-world objective data: each 10 min of gaming in bed → −17 min TST (95% CI −28 to −7) — [Brosnan et al. 2024](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2822859); playing video games in bed before sleep → trouble falling/staying asleep ARR 1.22 (1.14–1.29) — [Nagata et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10823798/); console use in the last hour before bed → SOL ≥ 60 min OR 1.13, ≥ 2 h/day console gaming → sleep < 5 h OR 2.03 (16–19-year-olds) — [Hysing et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4316480/); video gaming related to delayed bedtimes in the adolescent meta-analysis — [Bartel et al. 2015](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/protective-and-risk-factors-for-adolescent-sleep-a-meta-analytic-/); 86% of gaming studies showed adverse sleep associations — [Hale & Guan 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4437561/)
- NSF consensus: experimental studies in which participants played video games increased SOL, shortened sleep and reduced deep sleep; reducing interactive content such as gaming encouraged earlier bedtimes and longer sleep — [NSF consensus 2024 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38806392/)
- **Bedtime procrastination:** Turkish adolescents (n = 245, 13–18): 90.6% used smartphones in bed, on average 4.9 days/week for 2.3 h/day; 64.9% did ≥ 3 activities in bed (social networks 76.7%, videos 68.2%, messaging 53.5%); bedtime procrastination partially mediated the link between problematic smartphone use and poor sleep quality (indirect β = −0.24) — [Bozkurt et al. 2024, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11059092/). Chinese high-school students (n = 318, mean 16.9; 6-month longitudinal): baseline problematic smartphone use predicted later bedtime procrastination (β = .11), and baseline bedtime procrastination predicted shorter sleep at follow-up (β = −.16); FoMO had no direct effect — [Zhang et al. 2021, Current Psychology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12144-021-01863-9)
- "Revenge"-type motive in teens' own words: "sometimes their days are so busy that they only get to relax with their phone at bedtime, and that pushes sleep later" — [Common Sense Media 2023](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/sites/default/files/research/report/2023-cs-smartphone-research-report_final-for-web.pdf); theoretical "time filler" mechanism linked to the adolescent circadian delay — [Bauducco et al. 2024](https://researchnow-admin.flinders.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/116753136/Bauducco_Bidirectional_P2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a 16-year-old male gamer, the biggest risk is **session length and the inability to stop** (not violence per se): limit evening gaming on school nights to ~≤ 60 min, end it ≥ 60 min before lights-out, never game in bed, and use an external hard stop (console/PC parental-control timer, scheduled "Downtime", or a rule of "no new match after 21:00") because games with matches/ranked play are designed to continue. Grade: **Weak–moderate** (small lab experiments in teenage boys + consistent observational data).
- Move gaming to the afternoon/early evening (after homework) rather than eliminating it; this also reduces "revenge" procrastination by giving leisure time earlier. Grade: **Expert opinion**.
- Problematic/compulsive use (loss of control, gaming despite consequences) is the subgroup with the strongest sleep associations; persistent loss of control is a point at which professional evaluation (e.g., for gaming disorder/problematic internet use, which the AAP asks clinicians to screen for) is reasonable — [AAP policy statement slides](https://www.nationalacademies.org/cdn/materials/9fba0cb9-79b4-4ab0-bb07-b1b3ba56eb5e).

### Gaps
- No adolescent experiment cleanly compares violent/competitive vs non-violent/cooperative content at equal duration; content effects remain unquantified.
- No adolescent studies on online multiplayer late-night gaming with peers (social obligation) and sleep with objective measures were found.
- "Revenge bedtime procrastination" is largely a popular-media concept; peer-reviewed adolescent evidence is limited to small cross-sectional studies (e.g., a small Indonesian student survey) and generic bedtime-procrastination scales; no intervention trials in teens were found.

## 5. Caffeine and energy drinks: half-life, timing (Drake 2013 and later), caffeine content of teen drinks, adolescent limits, teen sleep evidence, tolerance/withdrawal and tapering

### Takeaway
In adults, caffeine shortens sleep by ~45 min on average, and a large dose (400 mg) even 6 h before bed cut objective sleep by > 1 h; meta-regression suggests a coffee-sized dose (~107 mg) needs ≥ 8.8 h and a pre-workout (~218 mg) ≥ 13.2 h before bedtime. In adolescents, energy-drink use shows a dose-dependent association with less sleep (≈ −57 min and +25 min to fall asleep at ≥ 4 drinks/week). Limits: AAP — energy drinks should never be consumed by adolescents and caffeine is best avoided; Health Canada ≤ 2.5 mg/kg/day; EFSA ≤ 3 mg/kg/day; in Russia sale of energy drinks to minors has been illegal since 1 March 2025. Withdrawal headache (≈50%) starts 12–24 h after stopping and lasts 2–9 days, so reductions should be gradual (~25% steps per week in adult trials).

### Cited Findings

**Pharmacokinetics and timing**
- Typical caffeine half-life is 3–6 h and varies up to ~8 h across healthy adults (ADULT EVIDENCE) — [Gardiner et al. 2023, Sleep Med Rev](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764); EFSA: caffeine clearance in children and adolescents is "at least that of adults" — [EFSA caffeine topic page](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine)
- **Drake et al. 2013 (J Clin Sleep Med; ADULT EVIDENCE; randomized, placebo-controlled, n = 12 healthy normal sleepers aged 19–48, mean habitual intake 115 mg/day):** 400 mg caffeine at bedtime, 3 h or 6 h before bedtime vs placebo, measured at home with a portable monitor: objective TST was reduced by **1.1–1.2 h at every time point, including 6 h before bed**; the 6-h condition reduced self-reported TST by 41 min (p = 0.08), i.e., people underestimated the effect; latency to persistent sleep +17.2 min (3 h); wake during sleep +27.6 min (3 h) and +8 min (6 h). Authors: refrain from substantial caffeine ≥ 6 h before bedtime; afternoon caffeine "should at a minimum be restricted to before 17:00" — [Drake et al. 2013, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3805807/)
- **Gardiner et al. 2023 (Sleep Med Rev; meta-analysis of 24 studies, predominantly ADULT):** caffeine reduced TST by 45 min and SE by 7%, increased SOL by 9 min and WASO by 12 min; light sleep (N1) +6.1 min, deep sleep (N3/N4) −11.4 min. Meta-regression cut-offs to avoid TST loss: coffee (107 mg per 250 mL) ≥ **8.8 h** before bed; standard pre-workout serve (217.5 mg) ≥ **13.2 h**; no clear cut-off for one cup of black tea (47 mg per 250 mL). For a 22:00 bedtime: coffee before 13:12, pre-workout before 08:50; cut-offs are "a starting point" given individual half-life variation — [Gardiner et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764)
- Circadian effect (ADULT EVIDENCE; n = 5, ~49-day within-subject, double-blind): ~200 mg (2.9 mg/kg, a "double espresso") 3 h before habitual bedtime delayed the melatonin rhythm by ~40 min (d = 0.93), about half the delay produced by 3 h of bright evening light — [Burke et al. 2015, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4657156/)
- EFSA: single doses of 100 mg (~1.4 mg/kg) "may affect sleep duration and patterns in some adults, particularly when consumed close to bedtime" — [EFSA topic page](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine); EFSA's draft opinion states that, as in adults, doses of ~1.5 mg/kg "may increase sleep latency and reduce sleep duration in some children and adolescents, particularly when consumed close to bedtime" — [EFSA draft opinion (consultation PDF)](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/150115.pdf)

**Recommended limits for adolescents**
- EFSA 2015: 3 mg/kg bw/day proposed as a level of no concern for habitual caffeine intake by children and adolescents (derived from adult acute data; information insufficient for a dedicated adolescent limit); adults 400 mg/day, single doses 200 mg — [EFSA topic page](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine); [EFSA opinion 2015](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102). Typical adolescent intake in Europe: 0.4–1.4 mg/kg/day; ~8% of adolescents may consume > 200 mg from energy drinks in a single session around exercise — [EFSA topic page](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine); [EFSA draft](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/150115.pdf)
- Health Canada: adolescents 13+ no more than **2.5 mg/kg/day** (precautionary; Health Canada notes healthy older, heavier adolescents aged 14–18 "may be able to consume caffeine in a similar manner to healthy adults") — [Health Canada, Caffeine in foods](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/food-safety/food-additives/caffeine-foods.html); [Health Canada advisory](https://healthycanadians.gc.ca/recall-alert-rappel-avis/hc-sc/2017/63362a-eng.php/). Worked examples: 60 kg → 150 mg/day, 70 kg → 175 mg/day — [AboutKidsHealth (SickKids)](https://teens.aboutkidshealth.ca/caffeine-and-energy-drinks?hub=food&language=en)
- AAP: "caffeine and other stimulant substances contained in energy drinks have no place in the diet of children and adolescents"; energy drinks "are not appropriate for children and adolescents and should never be consumed" — [AAP clinical report 2011, Pediatrics](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/127/6/1182/30098/); AAP (2025): "avoiding caffeine is the best choice for all kids" — [HealthyChildren.org 2025](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/healthy-living/nutrition/Pages/the-effects-of-caffeine-on-kids-a-parents-guide.aspx); 30–50% of teens report consuming energy drinks — [AAP News 2023](https://publications.aap.org/aapnews/news/27276/Children-should-avoid-drinks-with-sugar-caffeine)
- A frequently quoted "≤ 100 mg/day for ages 12+" limit is attributed to the AAP by Harvard's Nutrition Source — [Harvard Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/caffeine/) — but I could not locate this figure in an AAP policy document; the AAP documents found discourage caffeine altogether (conflict/uncertain attribution).
- **Russia:** Federal Law No. 304-FZ of 8 August 2024 prohibits retail sale of non-alcoholic tonic (including energy) drinks to minors, in force from 1 March 2025 — [Kremlin legal portal](http://www.kremlin.ru/acts/bank/50978); the ban does not cover tea, coffee and drinks based on them — [ConsultantPlus](https://www.consultant.ru/document/cons_doc_LAW_491247/38e03565f72304422c502bdb3c3af28741b18d1c/); administrative fines up to ~500,000 rubles were proposed by the State Duma — [State Duma news, 1 Mar 2025](http://duma.gov.ru/news/60996/)

**Caffeine content of drinks popular with teens**
- FDA (per 12 fl oz / 355 mL): caffeinated soft drink 23–83 mg; green tea 37 mg; black tea 71 mg; regular brewed coffee 113–247 mg; energy drinks 41–246 mg (54–328 mg per 16 fl oz) — [FDA, "Spilling the Beans"](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much)
- Mayo Clinic (per serving): brewed coffee 8 oz (237 mL) 96 mg; espresso 1 oz (30 mL) 63 mg; instant coffee 8 oz 62 mg; cola 8 oz 33 mg; energy drink 8 oz 79 mg; energy shot 2 oz (60 mL) 200 mg — [Mayo Clinic caffeine chart](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/in-depth/caffeine/art-20049372)
- EU-standard energy drinks are ~32 mg/100 mL: Red Bull 250 mL 80 mg, 355 mL 114 mg, 473 mL 151 mg; Monster (original) 500 mL 160 mg; Rockstar 500 mL 155 mg — [Verbraucherzentrale Niedersachsen table](https://www.verbraucherzentrale-niedersachsen.de/sites/default/files/medien/141/dokumente/Energy-Drink-Tabelle.pdf); Austrian lab test of 13 brands including Burn, Red Bull, Monster: 249–307 mg/L — [Konsument.at test](https://archiv.vki.at/konsument/tabellen/318889263766_6.html)
- Russian market: Roskachestvo (Sept 2024) tested 20 brands (Red Bull, Adrenaline Rush, Flash Up, Gorilla, Tornado, Burn, Black Monster, Drive Me, Lit Energy and others): **114–170 mg caffeine per 0.5 L can**; Burn and Lit Energy exceeded Roskachestvo's sugar standard (14.67 and 13.97 g sugars/100 mL) — [RIA Novosti on Roskachestvo study](https://ria.ru/20240906/roskachestvo-1970955797.html)
- Brand-specific Russian values from lower-reliability secondary sources: Adrenaline Rush 0.449 L ≈ 138 mg (≈ 30.8 mg/100 mL, attributed to Roskachestvo) — [schems.org aggregator](https://schems.org/tables/tablitsa-kofeina-v-energetikah); Flash Up ≈ 109 mg and Burn ≈ 175 mg per 0.5 L — [cartica.ru blog, 2021](https://cartica.ru/brendy-i-sorta/skolko-kruzhek-kofe-v-energetike.html) (the Burn figure exceeds Roskachestvo's 2024 range — conflicting/unverified)
- Pre-workout supplements: "standard serve" ≈ 217.5 mg caffeine — [Gardiner et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764)

**Caffeine/energy drinks and adolescent sleep**
- Adolescent meta-analysis (41 studies, 85,561 adolescents): caffeine was associated with decreased TST; "cautious use of ... caffeine, tobacco and alcohol should be considered" — [Bartel et al. 2015](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/protective-and-risk-factors-for-adolescent-sleep-a-meta-analytic-/)
- Norwegian 15–16-year-olds (n = 1,353, cross-sectional): even energy drinks 1–3×/month → −0.48 h sleep vs never; ≥ 4×/week → **−0.95 h (57 min)** sleep and **+25.4 min** shut-eye latency (≈ 38 min in non-users vs > 60 min in high users); high consumers 31 percentage points less likely to get ≥ 8 h; dose–response; 52.5% of boys vs 25.2% of girls consumed energy drinks — [Kaldenbach et al. 2022, BMC Public Health](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-022-12972-w)
- Systematic review (57 studies, children/young people ≤ 21, 2016–2022): 13 studies examined sleep; short sleep duration and poor sleep quality were consistently associated with more frequent energy-drink consumption; boys drink more; 75% of studies cross-sectional — [Ajibo et al. 2024, Public Health (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38228408/); [full text](https://research.tees.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/76667682/Consumptionofenergydrinksbychildrenandyoungpeople.pdf)

**Tolerance, withdrawal and cutting down**
- Caffeine withdrawal (ADULT EVIDENCE, critical review of experimental studies): validated symptoms include headache, fatigue, decreased alertness, drowsiness, depressed mood, difficulty concentrating, irritability, "foggy" feeling; headache incidence 50%; clinically significant distress/impairment 13%; onset 12–24 h after abstinence, peak 20–51 h, duration 2–9 days; occurs after abstaining from doses as low as 100 mg/day; severity rises with dose — [Juliano & Griffiths 2004, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15448977/)
- Nordic expert group (cited by EFSA) for children/adolescents: tolerance with withdrawal symptoms LOAEL 1.0–1.3 mg/kg bw (NOEL 0.3 mg/kg) — [EFSA draft opinion](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/consultation/150115.pdf)
- Tapering (ADULT EVIDENCE): RCT of a brief manualized treatment (n = 67; mean baseline 666 mg/day) using 5 weeks of "caffeine fading" — no more than 75% of baseline in week 1, then 50%, and so on — produced significant reductions in self-reported and salivary caffeine sustained to 1 year; gradual fading is used specifically to reduce withdrawal symptoms — [Evatt et al. 2016, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4737992/); a manual-only 6-week gradual reduction programme was also effective vs delayed treatment — [Sweeney et al. 2019, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30580203/)

### Inferences
- **Daily ceiling for this teen:** ≤ 2.5 mg/kg/day (≈ 140–175 mg for 55–70 kg; Health Canada) as the upper bound, ≤ 3 mg/kg/day (EFSA) as an absolute maximum; the sleep-friendly target for a teen with sleep-onset problems is **0–100 mg/day, all before early afternoon**. Note these ceilings are based on anxiety/behaviour, not sleep, so staying under them does not guarantee no sleep effect. Grade: **Guideline/expert consensus**.
- **Timing rule (target lights-out 22:30):** any drink with ≥ ~80–100 mg (coffee, energy drink, strong tea/cold-brew, large cola) — last one by **~13:30** (≥ 9 h; Gardiner) and never later than **16:30** (6 h; Drake). Caffeinated pre-workouts: **avoid** (would need ≥ 13 h; a 200-mg dose 3 h before bed also delays the body clock ~40 min). Small doses (one cup of black/green tea ~30–50 mg, one 330-mL cola ≈ 45 mg) after ~16:00–17:00 should also be avoided by a teen who already has trouble falling asleep, because EFSA flags ~1.4–1.5 mg/kg (~90–100 mg for 65 kg) near bedtime as sleep-relevant and effects are additive. Grade: **Moderate (ADULT EVIDENCE: RCT + meta-regression)**, extrapolated to adolescents.
- **Energy drinks: none.** Each 250-mL can ≈ 80 mg; each 0.449–0.5 L can ≈ 115–170 mg — i.e., a single large can already reaches or exceeds the Health Canada daily ceiling for a 50–65 kg teen. Grade: **Moderate** (consistent dose–response observational data in teens + AAP guidance; also illegal to buy for a 16-year-old in Russia).
- **Hidden caffeine:** colas, iced teas, "energy" chewing gum, chocolate, pre-workouts and some sports products — read labels; energy drinks for gaming are a common teen pattern (inference from co-occurrence of gaming and energy drinks in boys; not directly sourced).
- **Cutting down without headaches:** reduce total daily caffeine by ~25% of the starting amount each week over 4–5 weeks (e.g., 2 large cans/day ≈ 300 mg → 225 → 150 → 75 → 0), moving remaining doses to the morning first; expect any withdrawal to peak 1–2 days after a reduction step and fade within about a week. Grade: **Moderate (ADULT EVIDENCE)**.
- **People underestimate caffeine's effect on their own sleep** (Drake: objective loss > 1 h, self-reported 41 min, n.s.), so "it doesn't affect me" is not a reliable guide; a 2-week caffeine-free trial is a reasonable self-experiment.

### Gaps
- No adolescent RCT of caffeine timing/dose on sleep was found; all timing cut-offs are extrapolated from adults.
- Tolerance to caffeine's sleep-disrupting effects in habitual (adolescent) users is not well quantified in the sources reviewed.
- Reliable laboratory-verified per-can caffeine values for specific Russian brands (Adrenaline Rush, Flash Up, Burn, Drive Me, Gorilla) were not available in primary sources; only Roskachestvo's range (114–170 mg/0.5 L) is solid.
- Whether energy-drink sugar contributes independently to sleep problems is unknown.

## 6. Exercise: effects of regular physical activity on adolescent sleep; effects of evening and vigorous late exercise; recommendations for teens who train in the evening

### Takeaway
Regular physical activity is associated with earlier bedtimes and better sleep in adolescents, and RCT meta-analyses in children/adolescents show improved sleep quality and efficiency (but not longer TST), albeit with high heterogeneity; evening exercise in general does not harm sleep (adult meta-analyses), but vigorous/high-strain exercise ending ≤ 1–2 h before bed can delay sleep onset — large adult wearable data show no effect when exercise ends ≥ 4 h before sleep and ~36 min later sleep onset when a maximal session ends 2 h before habitual sleep onset.

### Cited Findings

**Regular physical activity (adolescents)**
- Adolescent meta-analysis (41 studies, 85,561 adolescents 12–18): physical activity was associated with earlier bedtimes; parent-set bedtimes and physical activity "may be beneficial" — [Bartel et al. 2015](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/protective-and-risk-factors-for-adolescent-sleep-a-meta-analytic-/)
- Lang et al. 2016 (Sleep Med Rev): 21 studies (16,549 participants aged 14–24) reviewed, 12 meta-analysed (cross-sectional): adolescents with higher subjective and objective physical activity are more likely to experience good sleep, subjectively and objectively; most studies used subjective measures of unknown validity — [Lang et al. 2016, Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26447947)
- **Kalak et al. 2012 (J Adolesc Health; RCT, n = 51, mean age 18.3):** 30 min of moderate running every weekday morning for 3 weeks vs control → objectively increased SWS and decreased SOL (sleep EEG); improved subjective sleep quality, mood and concentration; less daytime sleepiness — [Kalak et al. 2012, Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23174473)
- Meta-analysis of 27 RCTs in children/adolescents aged 6–18 (searched to Feb 2025): exercise improved sleep quality (SMD −1.06; 95% CI −1.76 to −0.36), sleep efficiency (SMD −2.17, direction as reported), WASO (SMD −0.53) and sleep-onset delay (SMD −0.30), but **not TST** (SMD 0.02; −0.23 to 0.27); sleep latency effect non-significant (SMD −1.05; −3.29 to 1.19) — [Song et al. 2025, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41137907/)
- Meta-analysis of 14 RCTs of aerobic exercise in children/adolescents (searched to Aug 2025): improved sleep-related outcomes SMD 0.91 (0.40–1.43) but I² = 91.5%, prediction interval −0.98 to 2.80, GRADE certainty **very low** (includes clinical groups such as ADHD) — [Aerobic exercise meta-analysis, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13456893/)
- Norwegian 15–16-year-olds: daily physical activity → 13 percentage points higher likelihood of > 8 h sleep; physically active adolescents had shorter shut-eye latency — [Kaldenbach et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-022-12972-w)
- Guideline context: ≥ 60 min/day of MVPA is recommended for ages 5–17 — [Wang et al. 2024, Frontiers in Neurology (citing WHO guidance)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neurology/articles/10.3389/fneur.2024.1438786/full)

**Evening and vigorous late exercise (ADULT EVIDENCE)**
- **Stutz, Eiholzer & Spengler 2019 (Sports Med; 23 studies, healthy adults):** a single evening exercise session (ending on average 1.8 h, range 0.12–4 h before bed) vs no exercise → SWS +1.3 percentage points, REM latency +7.7 min, stage 1 −0.9 pp; no change in SOL (−0.06 min), SE or WASO; "do not support the hypothesis that evening exercise negatively affects sleep, in fact rather the opposite", but SOL, TST and SE "might be impaired after vigorous exercise ending ≤ 1 h before bedtime". Example: vigorous running (80% HR reserve) ending 1 h before bed → SOL +14.0 min, TST −14.6 min, SE −3.1 pp, heart rate +26 bpm at bedtime; moderate exercise ending 0.5–1 h before bed and vigorous exercise ending 2.5 h before bed had no effect; higher physical stress relative to habitual activity → SE −3.2 pp, WASO +21.9 min (moderator effects disappeared after removing one study) — [Stutz et al. 2019, Sports Med](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0); [full text (ETH)](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/e97ceae9-be5e-4ed8-ad86-dd282b3fa7ed/content)
- **Frimpong et al. 2021 (Sleep Med Rev; 15 acute high-intensity exercise studies, 194 adults aged 18–50):** high-intensity exercise ending 0.5–4 h before bedtime decreased REM (−2.34%) with no other significant sleep changes; regular evening high-intensity exercise did not disrupt sleep; "acute evening HIE performed 2–4 h before bedtime does not disrupt nighttime sleep" — [Frimpong et al. 2021, Sleep Med Rev](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1087079221001209). The authors' summary: when exercise ended 2 h before bedtime there were benefits (sleep onset, duration); when it ended < 2 h before bedtime it took longer to fall asleep and sleep was shorter — [Concordia University news, 2021](https://www.concordia.ca/news/stories/2021/09/28/intense-workouts-before-bedtime-wont-guarantee-a-good-nights-rest-new-research-shows.html)
- **Leota et al. 2025 (Nature Communications; 14,689 physically active adults, 4,084,354 person-nights, WHOOP wearable, observational):** later timing × higher strain was dose-dependently associated with later sleep onset, shorter and lower-quality sleep, higher nocturnal heart rate and lower HRV; exercise ending **≥ 4 h before sleep onset** was not associated with sleep changes regardless of strain; maximal vs light strain ending 2 h before habitual sleep onset → +36 min later sleep onset, −22.2 min (−5.4%) sleep, +3.9 bpm resting HR, −14.1% HRV; ending 2 h **after** habitual sleep onset → +80 min later onset, −42.6 min sleep; when exercise ended ≥ 6 h before habitual sleep onset, maximal exercise was associated with earlier sleep onset and longer sleep — [Leota et al. 2025, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12000559/)

### Inferences
- **Daily activity:** aim for ≥ 60 min/day MVPA, preferably with some of it in daylight in the morning/afternoon (e.g., walking/cycling to school, PE, after-school sport); expect better sleep quality/continuity rather than more sleep hours. Grade: **Moderate** (adolescent RCT meta-analyses with high heterogeneity + one adolescent RCT + consistent observational data).
- **Evening sessions:** moderate exercise can be done up to ~1 h before bed. **Vigorous/high-strain sessions (intervals, competitive matches, heavy lifting, long hard runs) should end ≥ 2 h before lights-out (by 20:30 for 22:30), and ideally ≥ 3–4 h (by 18:30–19:30) when the schedule allows.** Grade: **Weak–moderate (ADULT EVIDENCE)** — the ≤ 1–2-h harm is supported by small experiments; the 4-h figure comes from large observational wearable data.
- **For teens whose club training ends late (e.g., 20:00–21:00):** finish with a 10–15-min easy cool-down; warm shower; eat a recovery meal/snack soon after (see §7); no caffeinated pre-workout or energy drink before practice; keep post-training screen use passive and short; dim, calm wind-down; and accept that lights-out may need to be ~30 min later on those nights — if so, protect the fixed wake time and move the hardest sessions to weekends/earlier days where possible. Grade: **Expert opinion** (built from adult data above + caffeine evidence §5).
- A wearable's nocturnal resting HR/HRV after late hard sessions can show the teen the effect for himself (trackers are covered elsewhere).

### Gaps
- No adolescent-specific experiments on evening vigorous exercise timing and sleep were found; all timing thresholds are from adults.
- Lang et al. 2016 pooled effect sizes and the dose–response (minutes of activity per minute of sleep) in adolescents were not available in the abstracts retrieved.
- The 27-RCT youth meta-analysis abstract does not specify how many trials were in healthy adolescents vs clinical/child samples, or effect direction coding for some outcomes.

## 7. Food and drink: late heavy meals, high sugar intake, going to bed hungry, fluids before bed, and "sleep foods" (kiwi, tart cherry juice, milk, high-carbohydrate dinners)

### Takeaway
Evidence on meal timing and specific foods is sparse, small and mostly adult: a main meal ~3–4 h before bed is associated with faster sleep onset/fewer awakenings in adults, yet an adolescent-athlete crossover found a dinner 1.5 h before bed improved sleep vs 3.5 h, so going to bed hungry is not advisable; kiwi and tart cherry show small, low-quality adult benefits, and a 2023 systematic review found no clear evidence that any specific food or diet measurably changes sleep.

### Cited Findings
- **High-GI carbohydrate dinner (ADULT EVIDENCE; Afaghi et al. 2007, AJCN; n = 12 healthy men aged 18–35, crossover, PSG):** an isocaloric very-high-carbohydrate jasmine-rice meal (GI 109) eaten 4 h before bed → SOL 9.0 min vs 17.5 min after a low-GI meal (GI 50) at 4 h, and vs 14.6 min after the same high-GI meal eaten 1 h before bed; no other sleep variables changed; the test meal (90% carbohydrate, glycaemic load 175) is far from a normal meal — [Afaghi et al. 2007, AJCN](https://ajcn.nutrition.org/article/S0002-9165(23)27929-0/fulltext)
- **Meal timing (young adults, ADULT EVIDENCE; cross-sectional):** among 793 university students aged 18–29, eating within 3 h of bedtime was associated with nocturnal awakenings (OR 1.61; adjusted 1.43) but not with SOL > 30 min or short sleep; total beverages at bedtime were not associated with awakenings — [Chung et al. 2020, Nutrients (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7215804/). Among 264 young Japanese adults, a dinner-to-bed interval ≤ 3.79 h or 3.80–4.94 h was associated with longer sleep latency (26.4 and 30.8 min) than ≥ 4.95 h (19.6 min), although sleep efficiency tended to be better with shorter intervals — [Yasuda et al. 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2674-0311/2/2/11)
- **Adolescent athletes (conflicting; Soleymani Lehmann et al. 2022, IJSNEM):** 12 rugby players aged 15.8 ± 0.7, crossover, 5 days per condition, PSG: dinner 1.5 h before bed vs 3.5 h before bed → TST **+24 min**, SE +4.8%, WASO −25 min, fewer micro-arousals (−25%) and awakenings ≥ 90 s (−30%) — [Soleymani Lehmann et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2022-0014)
- NZ adolescents (n = 47, aged 13–17; wearable cameras + actigraphy; master's thesis, weak): no association between eating after 21:30 and sleep; usual evening intake of caffeinated drinks (by questionnaire) was associated with later sleep onset — [Phetcharoen 2019 (thesis)](http://hdl.handle.net/10523/9084)
- Diet quality (ADULT EVIDENCE, controlled feeding study of 26 normal-weight adults summarized in a scoping review): diets low in fibre and high in saturated fat were associated with less restorative sleep — [Alruwaili et al. 2023 scoping review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41606-023-00090-4)
- Systematic review of 2012–2022 literature (Netzer et al. 2023): "None of the investigations revealed clear evidence of an effect of a certain diet or food on sleep"; studies in youth link late gaming, ultra-processed foods and short sleep, but without showing that the food itself shortens sleep — [Netzer et al. 2023, Sleep Breath](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11325-023-02921-1)
- **Kiwifruit (ADULT EVIDENCE):** uncontrolled pre–post study, 24 adults aged 20–55 with sleep complaints (22 women), 2 kiwifruit 1 h before bed nightly for 4 weeks: SOL −35.4%, WASO −28.9%, PSQI −42.4%, TST +13.4%, SE +5.41% — [Lin et al. 2011, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21669584/). Randomized single-blind crossover (24 men, mean age 29), two kiwifruit (fresh or dried) with one evening meal vs water: improvements mainly in subjective morning alertness/ease of awakening (e.g., +24% ease of awakening in poor sleepers with dried kiwi) and trends for getting to sleep; poor sleepers tended to wake more often after dried kiwi — [Kanon et al. 2023, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10043399/)
- **Tart cherry (ADULT EVIDENCE):** systematic review/meta-analysis of 8 studies of low–moderate quality (many using concentrates/supplement forms): objective TST effect size 1.21 (I² = 81.5%) and objective SE 0.63, but no significant subjective benefit — [Stretton et al. 2023, Current Sleep Medicine Reports](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40675-023-00261-w)

### Inferences
- **Dinner timing:** for lights-out at 22:30, have the main meal ~18:30–20:00 (≈ 2.5–4 h before bed) on most days. Grade: **Weak** (adult small trials/cross-sectional; conflicting adolescent athlete trial).
- **Don't go to bed hungry, especially on training days:** a moderate, carbohydrate-containing evening meal or snack 1–2 h before bed (e.g., rice/pasta/bread/porridge with some protein, milk or yoghurt, fruit such as kiwi) is reasonable; avoid very large, fatty or spicy meals in the last 2 h if the teen notices reflux/discomfort or awakenings. Grade: **Weak / expert opinion**.
- **Fluids:** drink normally during the day; if he wakes to urinate, shift larger drinks earlier and keep the last ~60 min modest. Grade: **Expert opinion** (one young-adult cross-sectional study found no link between bedtime beverages and awakenings).
- **"Sleep foods":** 2 kiwifruit or a glass of tart cherry juice in the evening are harmless foods that can be tried, but expect at most small effects; they should not displace behavioural changes. Milk/warm milk: no evidence located. Grade: **Weak (ADULT EVIDENCE)**.
- **Sugary energy drinks and sodas** in the evening are a problem mainly via caffeine (§5).

### Gaps
- No adolescent RCTs on meal timing, high-sugar intake, bedtime hunger, or bedtime fluids and sleep were found.
- No evidence was located for milk/warm milk as a sleep aid in adolescents (searched as part of food reviews; nothing specific retrieved).
- Effects of high sugar intake per se on adolescent sleep (independent of caffeine) remain unquantified.

## 8. Nicotine/vaping and alcohol: effects on adolescent sleep

### Takeaway
Adolescent vapers report insufficient sleep more often (adjusted ORs ≈ 1.3–1.7; dual users worse) and higher nicotine exposure is linked to trouble sleeping (OR ≈ 1.8), consistent with adult evidence that nicotine lengthens sleep latency, fragments sleep, reduces deep sleep, and that overnight/early withdrawal increases awakenings; in 18–21-year-olds a high alcohol dose did not speed sleep onset but suppressed REM and fragmented the second half of the night.

### Cited Findings
- **Nicotine pharmacology (ADULT EVIDENCE; Jaehne et al. 2009, Sleep Med Rev, 52 studies):** during nicotine consumption — increased sleep latency, sleep fragmentation, decreased SWS, reduced SE, increased daytime sleepiness, and in most studies REM suppression; withdrawal effects often mask replacement effects — [Jaehne et al. 2009, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19345124/); insomnia complaints are reported in up to 39% of cases during nicotine withdrawal — [Jaehne et al. 2009, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1087079208001329). PSG in 33 smokers (median age 29): arousal index and wake time increased 24–36 h after stopping smoking — [Jaehne et al., PubMed 24797355](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24797355/)
- **US high-school students (YRBS 2017, n = 11,296):** exclusive e-cigarette users vs non-users: insufficient sleep < 8 h aOR 1.57 (1.01–2.43) and < 7 h aOR 1.61 (1.16–2.24); dual users < 7 h aOR 1.73 (1.14–2.62) — [Merianos et al. 2021, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33667467/)
- YRBS 2017 + 2019 pooled (n = 28,135 adolescents): current vaping aOR 1.33 and former vaping aOR 1.29 for insufficient sleep — [Baiden et al. 2022, Sleep Med](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2022.10.005), as summarized in [Birur et al. 2025, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11809507/)
- Narrative review of 9 studies (2019–2023; 106,628 adolescents aged 12–18): vapers and dual users report more insufficient school-night sleep; in males, dual use was associated with longer sleep latency (+6.27 min); two studies suggest insufficient sleep may lead to vaping initiation (bidirectional) — [Birur et al. 2025, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11809507/)
- NHANES 2005–2018, US adolescents aged 16–19 (n = 2,630): serum cotinine ≥ 3 ng/mL → trouble sleeping OR 1.82 (1.09–3.02) in the fully adjusted model — [Du et al. 2024, Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-72215-3)
- Adolescent meta-analysis: tobacco use associated with decreased TST — [Bartel et al. 2015](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/protective-and-risk-factors-for-adolescent-sleep-a-meta-analytic-/)
- Dependence markers in teen vapers: among US high-school students using only e-cigarettes, wanting to use within 30 min of waking rose to 19.2% by 2022 (13.9% in 2023) — [Jackson et al. 2025 (NYTS), PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12529234/)
- **Alcohol (late adolescence; Chan et al. 2013, ACER; placebo-controlled PSG, n = 24 aged 18–21, breath alcohol 0.084% at lights-out):** no change in TST or SOL; less REM overall and delayed REM onset; first half of the night: more SWS, less REM; **second half: more WASO, lower SE, less SWS**, no REM rebound. Authors note that in adults alcohol typically shortens SOL, but this was not seen in late adolescents — [Chan et al. 2013, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3987855/)

### Inferences
- **No nicotine in any form** (vapes, snus/nicotine pouches, cigarettes). For a teen with slow sleep onset and night awakenings, nicotine is a stimulant at bedtime and a withdrawal trigger overnight/early morning. If he already vapes, quitting may transiently worsen sleep for some days (adult withdrawal data), and nicotine dependence is a point where professional cessation support is warranted. Grade: **Moderate** (consistent adolescent observational associations + adult mechanistic/experimental data).
- **No alcohol:** even when it feels sedating, it fragments the second half of the night and suppresses REM — directly relevant to "waking up unrefreshed". Grade: **Moderate** (experimental data in 18–21-year-olds + adult literature).

### Gaps
- No adolescent experimental/PSG studies of vaping, nicotine pouches or nicotine withdrawal on sleep were found; all adolescent data are cross-sectional surveys with self-reported sleep.
- No data were found on low-dose (1–2 drinks) alcohol effects in 16-year-olds (ethically constrained).

## 9. Studying late: sacrificing sleep for study, and sleep's role in memory consolidation before exams

### Takeaway
Within the same student, nights when sleep was cut to study more than usual were followed by more trouble understanding lessons and doing worse on tests the next day; experimental "Need for Sleep" studies in 15–19-year-olds show that a week of 5-h nights degrades attention, working memory and mood (not fully recovered after 2 nights), impairs encoding of new facts (26–65% more forgetting) and particularly harms crammed (massed) learning, whereas spaced study largely protects recall.

### Cited Findings
- **Gillen-O'Neel, Huynh & Fuligni 2013 (Child Development; N = 535, 9th-grade mean age 14.88; 14-day diaries in 9th, 10th and 12th grade):** regardless of how much a student generally studied, sacrificing sleep to study more than usual was followed by more trouble understanding material taught in class and a higher likelihood of struggling on an assignment or test the next day; sleep-for-study trade-offs became more frequent in later grades — [Gillen-O'Neel et al. 2013, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22906052/)
- **Lo et al. 2016 (Sleep; Need for Sleep; n = 56 aged 15–19 from top high schools; 7 nights of 5 h vs 9 h time in bed, boarding school):** cumulative deterioration of sustained attention, working memory, executive function and processing speed, more subjective sleepiness and less positive mood; sleepiness and sustained attention had not returned to baseline after 2 recovery nights — [Lo et al. 2016, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4763363/)
- **Huang et al. 2016 (Sleep; n = 56 aged 15–19; 5 h vs 9 h for a week):** recall of vocabulary studied in single massed sessions ("cramming") was impaired more by sleep restriction at all retention intervals, whereas recall of items spaced over 4 days was similar between sleep groups; the spacing benefit was ~2-fold greater in the sleep-restricted group — [Huang et al. 2016, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989257/)
- **Cousins et al. 2019 (n = 29 sleep-restricted vs 30 controls aged 15–18; 5 nights of 5 h):** facts learned after 4 restricted nights were forgotten more: 26% more at 30 min (d = 0.66), 34% at 3 days (d = 0.69) and 65% at 42 days (d = 1.17) — [Cousins et al. 2019, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31303555/); picture-encoding memory was also worse after 5 nights of 5 h in 59 adolescents (p = 0.001) — [Cousins et al. 2017, J Sleep Res](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.12578)
- Studying with digital media after 21:00 was associated with worse sleep quality and shorter sleep, and after 23:00 with insufficient sleep, in Finnish 15–20-year-olds — [Kortesoja et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9842555/); using a laptop for studying in bed was associated with trouble falling/staying asleep (ARR 1.08) in early adolescents — [Nagata et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10823798/)
- (Naps belong to the schedule notes:) under restricted sleep, a mid-afternoon nap enhanced memory for facts about as much as spending that time cramming — [Cousins et al. 2019, npj Science of Learning](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-019-0047-z)

### Inferences
- **Do not trade sleep for study on school nights:** set a fixed "books closed" time ~60 min before lights-out (≈ 21:30 for 22:30) and keep it before tests — the last extra hour of study is likely to cost more in next-day comprehension than it gains. Grade: **Moderate** (within-person diary data + adolescent sleep-restriction RCTs); the exact 60-min buffer is **expert opinion**.
- **Plan for spacing:** start revision several days before an exam in short daily sessions instead of cramming the night before; spaced practice protects recall even when sleep is short. Grade: **Moderate–strong** (adolescent RCT + broad spacing-effect literature).
- **Sleep after learning** is part of studying: reviewing key material earlier in the evening and then sleeping a full night supports encoding and retention (Need for Sleep data); all-nighters are the worst option.
- Study on a desk, not in bed, and with non-essential notifications off (also limits drift into social apps). Grade: **Weak/expert opinion**.

### Gaps
- No adolescent RCT tested a homework "stop time" rule.
- Effects of a single late night (e.g., 1–2 h lost before one exam) vs chronic restriction on exam performance in 16-year-olds are not well quantified in the sources found.

## 10. Synthesis: specific rules (cut-off times, amounts, durations) with evidence grades for the target 16-year-old

### Takeaway
For a 16-year-old with a fixed ~06:30–07:30 wake-up, the highest-yield, best-supported changes are: no screens in bed and no phone access overnight, an evening curfew on interactive screen use (gaming/chat/short video) ≥ 60 min before lights-out, no energy drinks and no caffeine after early afternoon, no nicotine or alcohol, ≥ 60 min/day of activity with hard sessions ending ≥ 2 h (ideally ≥ 4 h) before bed, dinner ~2.5–4 h before bed without going to bed hungry, and a fixed homework stop time with spaced rather than crammed revision.

### Cited Findings
- AAP: teens 14–17 should aim for 8–10 h of sleep per night — [AAP Q&A 2023](https://www.aap.org/en/patient-care/media-and-children/center-of-excellence-on-social-media-and-youth-mental-health/qa-portal/qa-portal-library/qa-portal-library-questions/screen-time-affecting-sleep/)
- Key quantitative anchors (detailed with sources in §§1–9): in-bed interactive screen use −9 min sleep per 10 min and gaming −17 min per 10 min — [Brosnan et al. 2024](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2822859); evening screen curfew +17 to +21 min sleep — [Perrault et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsz125), [Dibben et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10909457/); ringer on vs off ARR 1.23 for trouble falling/staying asleep — [Nagata et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10823798/); caffeine −45 min TST on average, coffee ≥ 8.8 h and pre-workout ≥ 13.2 h before bed — [Gardiner et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764); 400 mg even 6 h before bed −1.1 to −1.2 h objective sleep — [Drake et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3805807/); energy drinks ≥ 4×/week −57 min sleep and +25 min to fall asleep in 15–16-year-olds — [Kaldenbach et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-022-12972-w); caffeine limits 2.5 mg/kg/day (Health Canada) / 3 mg/kg/day (EFSA) — [Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/food-safety/food-additives/caffeine-foods.html), [EFSA](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine); vigorous exercise ending ≤ 1 h before bed may impair sleep onset — [Stutz et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0); no association when exercise ends ≥ 4 h before sleep onset — [Leota et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12000559/); withdrawal onset 12–24 h, peak 20–51 h, duration 2–9 days — [Juliano & Griffiths 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15448977/); tapering ≤ 75% → 50% of baseline over successive weeks — [Evatt et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4737992/); sleep-for-study trade-offs worsen next-day learning — [Gillen-O'Neel et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22906052/)

### Inferences
Rule set expressed relative to target lights-out time **B** (example column: B = 22:30; with a 06:45 wake-up this gives ~8 h in bed — schedule targets themselves are covered by the schedule notes):

| Domain | Rule | Example (B = 22:30) | Evidence grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Screens | No screens of any kind once in bed | Bed from 22:15–22:30 is screen-free | Moderate |
| Screens | Phone charged outside the bedroom (or out of reach), stand-alone alarm clock; phone off or DND/Sleep Focus with no sound **and no vibration** | Phone "parked" by 22:00 | Weak–moderate (location/access); Weak (silencing) |
| Screens | Interactive screen curfew (games, messaging, social feeds, short videos, multitasking) | Stop by 21:30 (B − 60 min) | Moderate that a curfew helps; 60-min value = Expert opinion |

| Screens | Night awakenings: do not check the phone | — | Weak |
| Gaming | ≤ ~60 min of gaming on school nights; no new match after the curfew; external timer; never in bed | Gaming ends ≤ 21:30, preferably earlier (after homework) | Weak–moderate |
| Caffeine | Daily total ≤ 2.5 mg/kg (≈ 150–175 mg at 60–70 kg); sleep-friendly target 0–100 mg | — | Guideline (expert consensus) |
| Caffeine | Last coffee/strong tea/cola-sized caffeinated drink ≥ 9 h before B; never within 6 h | Before ~13:30; absolute latest 16:30 | Moderate (ADULT EVIDENCE) |
| Caffeine | No energy drinks (any brand; 0.45–0.5 L ≈ 115–170 mg) and no caffeinated pre-workouts | — (sale to minors banned in Russia since 01.03.2025) | Moderate (teen observational dose–response + AAP) |

| Exercise | ≥ 60 min/day MVPA, some in daylight | Any time before evening | Moderate |

- **Expected benefit if adopted together:** individual levers are modest (≈ 15–25 min more sleep from a screen curfew; avoidance of a potential ~45-min caffeine-related loss; fewer night-time phone awakenings), but they are largely additive for a teen whose problem is late sleep onset against a fixed wake time.
- **Where professional evaluation is warranted (pointers only):** persistent loss of control over gaming/phone use, nicotine dependence, or sleep problems persisting despite consistent application of these habits for several weeks — the clinical thresholds and red flags are covered by the medical/insomnia notes.

### Gaps
- No adolescent RCT has tested a combined "lifestyle bundle" (screens + caffeine + exercise timing) on objective sleep.
- Exact individual thresholds (curfew length, caffeine cut-off for a given teen's metabolism, training-end time) cannot be derived from population data; a 2-week self-experiment with a sleep diary or tracker is the practical way to individualize (trackers covered elsewhere).
