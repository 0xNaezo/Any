# Self-applicable behavioral and cognitive techniques for adolescent insomnia (CBT-I components) and digital CBT-I programs — evidence as of October 2026

*Scope: trouble falling asleep, night awakenings, unrefreshing sleep in a 16-year-old with a fixed weekday wake time (~06:30–07:30). Supplements, medication and clinician-delivered therapy are out of scope (mentioned only where the evidence says professional evaluation is warranted). Tags: **[TEEN]** = adolescent evidence (≈12–19 y); **[ADULT]** = adult evidence only; **[CHILD]** = <13 y. Evidence grades used in the Inferences sections are the researcher's judgement: **Strong** = consistent RCT/meta-analytic evidence in adolescents with adequate certainty; **Moderate** = consistent but low-certainty adolescent evidence, or strong adult evidence with partial adolescent support; **Weak** = small/uncontrolled/single studies, or adult-only small trials; **Expert opinion** = no direct trial evidence.*

## 1. How effective is CBT-I in adolescents? (meta-analyses, effect sizes, daytime functioning, anxiety/depression, guideline status)

### Takeaway
Every adolescent meta-analysis from 2017 to 2026 finds that multicomponent CBT-I improves insomnia severity and sleep-onset latency (SOL). In the RCT-only pooled analysis the effect sizes are moderate to large (SMD ≈ −1.0 for insomnia severity and SOL), and SOL typically shortens by about 10–23 minutes. Effects on night waking (WASO), total sleep time (TST), objective (actigraphy) outcomes, daytime sleepiness and long-term follow-up are smaller and inconsistent. Mental-health benefits are small: depressive symptoms SMD ≈ 0.24, plus some anxiety benefit. GRADE certainty is low to very low, because trials are small, often waitlist-controlled and at high risk of bias. Neither major insomnia guideline (AASM 2021, ESRS 2023) covers adolescents; both recommend CBT-I first-line for **adults**, and the European guideline accepts digital delivery.

### Cited Findings

#### Meta-analyses and reviews (chronological)
- **Blake et al. 2017** (Clin Child Fam Psychol Rev) **[TEEN]**: 9 trials (n = 357, mean age 14.97 y, 62% female). There were only 4 RCTs, all with high risk of bias, so the authors pooled within-group (pre–post) data (n = 221). Post-intervention changes were: subjective TST **+29.5 min** (95% CI 17.2–41.8), SOL **−21.4 min** (−30.8 to −12.1), sleep efficiency (SE) **+5.3%**, WASO d = 0.59; objective SOL **−16.2 min**, objective SE **+2.8%**. Global sleep quality, daytime sleepiness, depression and anxiety also improved, and gains were "generally maintained" — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28331991/)
- **Åslund et al. 2018** (J Clin Sleep Med) **[TEEN/CHILD; mean age 14.6]**: 6 RCTs, n = 528, interventions with ≥4 sessions. SOL fell vs control by **−9.3 min** (diary) and **−19.5 min** (actigraphy). Actigraphic TST rose **+11.5 min** (P = .05); self-reported TST did not change. At post-treatment there was **no effect on WASO or daytime sleepiness**. At 4–8-week follow-up SOL remained shorter (−15.9 min diary; −23.7 min actigraphy), WASO became significant (−14.4 min ratings; −7.5 min actigraphy), and TST still did not change. The authors caution: "treatment protocols were heterogeneous and risk of bias high" — [JCSM](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.7498)
- **Ma et al. 2018** (Braz J Med Biol Res) **[ages 5–19]**: 6 RCTs + 4 open-label trials, n = 464. SOL fell (actigraphy MD −14.8 min; sleep log −12.3 min) and SE rose (+4.3% actigraphy; +5.5% log). There was **no significant effect on WASO or TST** — [PMC6002144](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6002144/)
- **Mei et al. 2024** (Front Public Health) **[TEEN]**: 8 RCTs, n = 599. Post-treatment effects were insomnia severity **SMD −1.06** (95% CI −1.65 to −0.47), SOL **−0.99** (−1.65 to −0.32), TST **+0.50** (0.10–0.90) and SE **+0.57** (0.26–0.87). At follow-up only insomnia remained significant (SMD −0.79); SOL, TST and SE were no longer significant, with fewer trials contributing. In the follow-up forest plot, de Bruin 2018 SOL was −0.06 (−0.30 to 0.18) — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2024.1413694/full)
- **Hornsey et al. 2024** (JAACAP umbrella review, 93 systematic reviews/meta-analyses covering 393 RCTs) **[CHILD/TEEN]**: for youth with a formal sleep-disorder diagnosis (mainly insomnia), behavioral interventions improved night waking (SMD −0.97, **very low** certainty), SE (−0.49, very low) and SOL (−0.57, very low). The reviewers "could not find any result characterized by a high or moderate level of evidence" (reasons: lack of blinding, imprecision, publication bias) — [JAACAP](https://www.jaacap.org/article/S0890-8567(24)02021-5/fulltext)
- **Cleary et al. 2025** (J Sleep Res; digital CBT-I) **[TEEN, 12–19 y]**: 9 studies, n = 486; only 2 were RCTs. Within-subject effects were insomnia symptoms Hedges' **g = 1.40**, subjective SOL g = 0.72, WASO g = 0.47 and TST g ≈ 0.29 (subjective) / 0.23 (objective). "Other objective measures of sleep did not improve." 7 of 9 programs included therapist support; the only unguided program was Sleep Ninja. The reduction in insomnia severity matched adult digital CBT-I (SMD 1.34 vs 1.36) — [J Sleep Res](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14466)
- **Ewart et al. 2025** (Behav Sleep Med; school-based CBT-I) **[TEEN, mean 15.3 y]**: 8 studies, n = 323, pooled within-group. Subjective TST **+23.9 min** and SOL **−8.3 min**; sleep quality g = 0.38. Objective changes were **not significant** (TST +20.9 min, P = .10; SOL −1.35 min; SE +0.5%). Anxiety improved (g = 0.37); depression did not (P = .196) — [T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15402002.2025.2529856)
- **Rohmah et al. 2026** (Worldviews Evid Based Nurs; network meta-analysis of **22 RCTs**, 7 delivery formats; search to Sept 2025) **[TEEN]**: web-based CBT-I vs usual care gave TST **+32.8 min**, SOL **−23.1 min**, SE **+6.9%** and insomnia severity **−5.05 points**. Insomnia severity reductions vs usual care for other formats: app-based CBT-I −2.16, face-to-face −1.62, group −1.52 points. No format differed on WASO. Heterogeneity was very high (I² ≈ 87–97%) — [PMC13360328](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13360328/)
- **Galling et al. 2026** (Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry) **[CHILD/TEEN, mean age 13.5]**: 8 RCTs, n = 904. Insomnia-focused interventions reduced depressive symptoms vs control, **SMD 0.24** (95% CI 0.09–0.39). Delivery format, including digital, did not moderate the effect; completion was 87% — [SpringerMedicine abstract](https://www.springermedicine.com/insomnia-disorders/insomnia-disorders/effects-of-insomnia-focused-psychological-and-behavioral-interve/53075946)
- **Reddy et al. 2023** (J Nerv Ment Dis; 7 RCTs to June 2020): "most studies … showed a strong effect of CBT-I on symptoms of depression"; anxiety evidence is limited. The authors call the findings preliminary — [doi](https://doi.org/10.1097/nmd.0000000000001613)
- A 2024 BMJ Paediatrics Open review says adolescent sleep treatments are "largely adapted from adult approaches" and that clinicians "must extrapolate from adult literature" — [BMJ Paeds Open](https://bmjpaedsopen.bmj.com/content/8/1/e001229)

#### Key adolescent RCTs
- **de Bruin et al. 2015** (SLEEP): n = 116, ages 12–19 (mean 15.6, 25% male), DSM-IV insomnia. Six weekly sessions (psychoeducation, sleep hygiene, restriction of time in bed, stimulus control, cognitive therapy, relaxation) were delivered either as guided internet therapy or as group therapy, vs waitlist. Versus waitlist, actigraphic SE improved β = 1.09 (internet) and 0.91 (group), and actigraphic SOL β = −0.87 and −0.99. Gains were maintained at 2 months. High end-state functioning was reached by 63–91% of treated participants vs 31–40% on the waitlist; clinically significant change by 24–57% vs 0–20% — [PMC4667374](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4667374/)
- **SENSE trial** (Blake et al. 2016, J Consult Clin Psychol): n = 123 at-risk adolescents (12–17 y, high anxiety plus sleep problems). Seven 90-min group sessions of CBT plus mindfulness were compared with an *active* "study skills" control. The sleep group improved more in global sleep quality (medium effect), SOL and daytime sleepiness (small effects), objective SOL (medium; about 11 min) and anxiety (small). There was **no effect on depression** (CES-D), and no effect on TST or SE — [doi](https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000142); [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/64x2x6bk)
- **Sleep Ninja RCT** (Werner-Seidler et al. 2023, J Child Psychol Psychiatry): n = 264, 12–16 y. A fully automated app was compared with weekly SMS sleep tips (active control). Insomnia severity improved **d = 0.41** at 6 weeks and **d = 0.39** at 14 weeks. Depression improved d = 0.28 and anxiety d = 0.27 at 6 weeks only. Sleep quality improved d = 0.39–0.44. There were **no effects on fatigue, daytime sleepiness, wellbeing or sleep-related behaviours**, and no adverse events — [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.13795); [full text PDF](https://researchnow-admin.flinders.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/132459860/Werner_Seidler_Effects_P2023.pdf)

#### Guidelines (all adult-scoped)
- **AASM 2021** (Edinger et al.): STRONG recommendation for multicomponent CBT-I in **adults**. CONDITIONAL recommendations for brief behavioral therapy, stimulus control, sleep restriction and relaxation. CONDITIONAL recommendation **against** sleep hygiene as a single therapy. There was "insufficient evidence" to recommend paradoxical intention, intensive sleep retraining, biofeedback, cognitive therapy or mindfulness as single components — [PMC7853203](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)
- **European Insomnia Guideline 2023** (Riemann et al., ESRS): CBT-I is first-line "in adults of any age" (grade A), may be delivered in person or digitally (A), and "sleep restriction and stimulus control are the most active ingredients of CBT-I" (B). The guideline "addresses adult patients over the age of 18 years. The literature on insomnia in children and adolescents was not reviewed" — [J Sleep Res](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jsr.14035); [summary](https://www.mdpi.com/2514-183X/8/1/10)
- **European expert guidance for typically developing children** (Bruni et al. 2024): a stepwise approach, starting with sleep hygiene and bedtime routines, then behavioral therapies. It states that CBT-I evidence in children/adolescents is "promising," "at least in the short term" — [PMC11192690](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11192690/)

### Inferences
- **Evidence grade.** Multicomponent CBT-I for adolescent insomnia: **Moderate** for short-term reduction of insomnia severity and sleep-onset latency. **Weak** for WASO, objective TST, daytime sleepiness and durability beyond about 2–6 months. **Weak-to-moderate** for small mental-health co-benefits (depression SMD ≈ 0.24, conflicting in individual trials).
- **Conflicts to report.** Daytime sleepiness improved in Blake 2017 (within-group) and SENSE (small effect), but not in Åslund 2018 or the Sleep Ninja RCT. Depression improved in Galling 2026 and in Sleep Ninja at 6 weeks, but not in SENSE or the school-based meta-analysis.
- **Target teen.** He complains of waking unrefreshed and daytime sleepiness. CBT-I reliably helps sleep onset and insomnia distress, but has the weakest evidence for daytime sleepiness. If sleepiness persists after insomnia symptoms improve, insufficient sleep opportunity (fixed early wake time) or circadian delay (Q10) is the more likely driver.
- **Guided vs self-guided.** Most effective adolescent programs were guided by a therapist; the one large unguided app RCT produced effects about half as large (d ≈ 0.4) as therapist-guided trials, though against an active control. A self-directed teen should therefore expect modest gains and benefit from parental/structured support.

### Gaps
- No adolescent-specific clinical guideline on CBT-I was found. ESRS 2023 explicitly did not review adolescent literature; AASM 2021 is adult-only.
- Long-term (≥12-month) controlled adolescent data are scarce. The de Bruin 1-year follow-up was seen only through Mei 2024's forest plot (attenuated SOL/SE effects), not read in full.
- No adolescent component-dismantling trial exists, so which CBT-I component works for teens is unknown.

## 2. Stimulus control: rules, evidence, and adaptation for teens who use the bed for homework, phone and gaming

### Takeaway
Stimulus control (стимул-контроль) aims to re-link bed with sleep and to fix the wake time. Its rules are: go to bed only when sleepy; get up when unable to sleep; use the bed only for sleep; wake at the same time every day; avoid daytime napping. It has a conditional AASM recommendation (low-quality evidence, adults) and is called one of the "most active ingredients" of CBT-I by ESRS (adults). In adolescents it has been tested only inside multicomponent packages (de Bruin; Sleep Ninja). The main adolescent RCT did **not** routinely apply the "get up after 20 minutes" rule, because teens had little time awake in bed. Teen-specific adaptations (desk vs bed, phone out of bed) are expert opinion.

### Cited Findings
- AASM definition of stimulus control: "(a) go to bed only when sleepy; (b) get out of bed when unable to sleep; (c) use the bed/bedroom for sleep and sex only (no reading, watching TV, etc. in bed); (d) wake up the same time every morning; (e) refrain from daytime napping." **[ADULT]** — [AASM 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)
- AASM evidence for stimulus control: "a small body of low quality evidence from 8 studies showing clinically meaningful improvements in one critical outcome" (remission rate). It "may need to be adapted for safety in some patient populations, such as those at high risk for falls." **[ADULT]** — [AASM 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)
- ESRS 2023: "Sleep restriction and stimulus control are the most active ingredients of CBT-I (B)" **[ADULT]** — [summary of ESRS 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2514-183X/8/1/10)
- de Bruin 2015 adolescent RCT: "we did not apply the technique in which a patient is advised to get up if awake in bed for longer than 20 minutes" (kept as a last resort, and no participant met criteria). The reason given: "The adolescents in our study did not experience much (self-reported) WASO." **[TEEN]** — [PMC4667374](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4667374/)
- Sleep Ninja dedicates training session 2 to stimulus control. The app is designed "to be used during the day and not at bedtime" to avoid evening screen exposure **[TEEN/CHILD]** — [ANZCTR ACTRN12623000587606](https://www.anzctr.org.au/Trial/Registration/TrialReview.aspx?id=385818); [pilot](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-026502)
- In pediatric "faded bedtime with response cost," a child unable to fall asleep within **20 minutes** is removed from bed, does a quiet, unrewarding activity for 20 minutes, then returns to bed **[CHILD]** — [review, PMC7687719](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7687719/)
- **FERRET** adolescent sleep-hygiene program (n = 22, mean age 13) included the rule "no other activities in bed except for sleep." It improved adolescent sleep-hygiene, PSQI and SDSC scores, but objective sleep duration did not change significantly **[TEEN, weak]** — [Hale et al. 2018 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5839336/)
- Teen sleep guidance: "Do not watch TV, study, exercise, or get involved in 'energizing' activities in the 30 minutes before bedtime" **[expert]** — [Nationwide Children's](https://www.nationwidechildrens.org/specialties/sleep-disorder-center/sleep-tips-for-adolescents)
- The SENSE adolescent workbook asks teens to set "a set time for homework and activities to be finished and all media to be switched off," plus set lights-out and wake times **[TEEN program material]** — [SENSE workbook](https://psychologicalsciences.unimelb.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/2780585/Workbook-for-publication-2018-for-upload.pdf)
- Monitoring the clock while trying to sleep increases pre-sleep worry and lengthens perceived SOL (see Q4/Q6). This supports the usual "no clock-watching" add-on to stimulus control **[ADULT]** — [Tang et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbtep.2005.07.004)

### Inferences
- **Evidence grade.** **Moderate** in adults as a single component (conditional/low-quality, but a core ingredient per ESRS). **Expert opinion / component-level** in adolescents, with no teen trial isolating it.
- **Practical self-application for a 16-year-old** (derived from the AASM rules, de Bruin's teen adaptation and the SENSE/Sleep Ninja materials):
  1. Fix the wake-up time first (school days; weekends kept close — schedule details belong to the schedule section).
  2. Go to bed when sleepy (heavy eyes, nodding), not merely "because it's time"; but not later than the planned latest bedtime.
  3. Make the bed "sleep-only" after a set evening time. Homework, gaming and scrolling move to a desk, chair or floor cushion. If the room is small, use a visible "day mode" for the bed (sit on top of the cover, lights on) vs "night mode" (under the covers, lights off).
  4. Phone charges outside the bed (ideally outside the room); use an ordinary alarm clock turned away from view.
  5. If lying awake and getting frustrated or alert, get up — judged by feel, about 15–20 minutes, without checking a clock. Go to another spot with dim light and do something quiet and boring (paper book, drawing, folding laundry). Return when sleepy; repeat if needed.
  6. Because de Bruin's adolescents rarely needed the get-up rule, a teen whose main problem is lying awake for long periods at sleep onset or after awakenings should use it, while one who falls asleep within ~20–30 minutes can skip it.
- **Caution.** Getting up at night should be safe (dim light, no tripping hazards). Getting up to use a screen defeats the purpose.

### Gaps
- No adolescent trial tested stimulus control alone, or the "bed only for sleep" rule specifically against multi-use bedrooms (homework/gaming in bed).
- No evidence identifies the optimal get-up threshold (15 vs 20 minutes) for teens.

## 3. Sleep restriction / sleep compression: evidence, safety in already sleep-deprived teens, self-administration, milder alternatives

### Takeaway
Sleep restriction therapy (SRT, ограничение времени в постели) limits time in bed to the person's actual average sleep time, then extends it as sleep efficiency rises. It is effective in adults, but acutely it **reduces objective sleep by about 1–1.5 h/night, increases sleepiness and impairs vigilance for several weeks**. AASM flags it as possibly contraindicated for drivers/high-risk occupations, mania-prone people and poorly controlled seizures. In adolescents, SRT has been used only inside therapist-guided protocols (where it "mostly resulted in later bedtimes"), and in one child dismantling RCT delivered by psychologists. The only unguided teen app (Sleep Ninja) omits it. Strict self-administered SRT is therefore **not supported** for a sleep-deprived 16-year-old. Milder options exist (sleep compression, a floor on time in bed, not lying in bed awake, an age-based bedtime from a fixed wake time).

### Cited Findings
- **Definition.** "Limiting time in bed equal to the patient's sleep duration … Time in bed is initially limited to the average sleep duration, and subsequently increased or decreased based on sleep efficiency thresholds" **[ADULT]** — [AASM 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)
- **AASM evidence and harms** **[ADULT]**:
  - Evidence is "a small body of low quality evidence from 6 studies" showing improved responder/remission rates.
  - Early harms include "increased daytime sleepiness."
  - SRT "may be contraindicated" for "those working in high risk occupations" ("heavy machinery operators or drivers"), and requires caution in "those predisposed to mania/hypomania or with poorly controlled seizure disorders."
  - Source: [AASM 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)
- **Kyle et al. 2014** (SLEEP; n = 16 adults with insomnia, single-component SRT) **[ADULT]**:
  - PSG total sleep time fell **91 min** (night 1), **78 min** (night 8) and **69 min** (night 22) vs baseline.
  - Psychomotor-vigilance lapses increased (ES 0.69–0.78) and reaction times slowed.
  - Epworth sleepiness rose in weeks 1–3 (4.95 → 8.69 in week 1).
  - All returned to baseline by 3 months.
  - Source: [doi](https://doi.org/10.5665/sleep.3386)
- **2020 RCT** (n = 150 post-menopausal women; SRT vs CBT-I vs sleep education; minimum time in bed **5 h** "for safety as is standard") **[ADULT]**:
  - Titration rules: +30 min if SE ≥90%; +15 min if 85–89%; no change if 80–84%; −15 min if <80%.
  - Post-treatment MSLT showed no excess odds of excessive sleepiness for SRT (OR 0.94) or CBT-I (OR 0.62) vs control.
  - The authors note excessive sleepiness "is a common concern … especially after treatment when patients are no longer monitored."
  - Source: [PMC7053018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7053018/)
- **Spielman SRT chapter** (Univ. Pennsylvania CBT-I resources) **[ADULT, expert]**:
  - Indicated when sleep efficiency is <85%. Lower limit of time in bed is 5 h, with a 30-min increase allowed "to quickly forestall severe sleepiness."
  - Not for people "who need to maintain optimal vigilance" (e.g., drivers), or those with epilepsy, parasomnias or sleep-disordered breathing.
  - Gentler variants: initial time in bed = average sleep + 30 min; or **sleep compression** — "reduce TIB by half the difference between baseline TIB and baseline TST," then by a quarter of the difference at later sessions.
  - Source: [PDF](https://www.med.upenn.edu/cbti/assets/user-content/documents/BTSD%20Spielman%20Chapter%20on%20SRT.pdf)
- **de Bruin 2015** (adolescents, therapist-guided) **[TEEN]**: "the exercise of restriction of time in bed mostly resulted in later bedtimes and stable get up time." Parents were told their support "could be helpful (e.g., restriction of time in bed)." Group-therapy participants shortened time in bed during treatment but lengthened it again after — [PMC4667374](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4667374/)
- **Cain et al. 2022** (J Sleep Res; dismantling RCT; n = 61, ages 6–14, mean 9.1) **[CHILD/young TEEN]**:
  - Two 60-min sessions with a psychologist; sleep restriction or bedtime restriction vs time-in-bed regularization.
  - During the 2-week treatment, TST fell (d = 1.38–2.27) and evening sleepiness rose (d = 1.01–1.47). SOL improved vs control (d = 1.10–1.21).
  - There were no adverse effects on cognition or parasomnias.
  - Time in bed increased at 1 month and benefits held.
  - Source: [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13658)
- **Sleep Ninja** (unguided teen app) lists its core strategies as "psychoeducation, stimulus control, sleep hygiene and sleep-related cognitive therapy." It gives "recommended bedtimes based on sleep guidelines"; the child version gives "sleep scheduling based on required wake time." Sleep restriction is not listed — [pilot abstract](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-026502); [ANZCTR](https://www.anzctr.org.au/Trial/Registration/TrialReview.aspx?id=385818)
- A 2025 adolescent digital-intervention review (preprint, not peer-reviewed) lists "optional sleep restriction technique" among the active components of effective adolescent digital CBT-I — [Research Square preprint](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-6609677/v1)
- **Adolescent sleep need and experimental restriction** **[TEEN]**:
  - Adolescents "benefit from at least eight to ten hours of sleep each night" — [BMJ Paeds Open 2024](https://bmjpaedsopen.bmj.com/content/8/1/e001229)
  - In an experimental protocol, 6.5 h in bed (≈6.1–6.3 h sleep) for 5 nights induced daytime sleepiness, inattention and oppositionality in adolescents with ADHD (n = 48 analysed), and had done so in typically developing adolescents in prior studies — [Becker et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6441371/)
- **Clinical adolescent example** (BMJ Paeds Open 2024): a teen with sleep efficiency of 78% restricted time in bed. Once SE was above 85% for a week she added 15 min (earlier bedtime) until SE was 85–90% "and she did not feel fatigued in the day." She "found it hard to implement sleep restriction because she was anxious about being sleep deprived at school"; motivational interviewing was used — [BMJ Paeds Open](https://bmjpaedsopen.bmj.com/content/8/1/e001229)
- **Typical untreated teen pattern** (Sleep Ninja pilot baseline, 12–16 y): 9 h 39 min in bed, 7 h 40 min asleep, SE ≈ 80%, about 29 min awake in bed after the final waking. After the app, time in bed after waking fell significantly — [BMJ Open pilot](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/9/5/e026502)

### Inferences
- **Evidence grade.** SRT efficacy: **Moderate–strong in adults**, **Weak in adolescents** (component of guided packages only). Safety of self-administered SRT in teens: **no evidence**, with documented acute sleepiness/vigilance costs in adults. **Recommendation: do not self-administer strict SRT** (5–6 h windows) at 16, especially if the teen drives, rides a bike or scooter in traffic, has seizures, mood swings suggestive of hypomania, or snoring/apnea signs.
- **Milder self-applicable alternatives** (inference; none validated in teens):
  1. **Stop "excess" time in bed.** Don't go to bed hours before sleepiness to "catch up," and get up soon after the alarm rather than lying in bed. Sleep Ninja's pilot showed reductions in morning time in bed are achievable.
  2. **Age-based sleep window from a fixed wake time.** E.g., wake 06:45 and aim for a sleep opportunity of about 8.5–9 h, so lights-out no earlier than ~21:45–22:15 and only when sleepy (the Sleep Ninja approach).
  3. **Gentle sleep compression with a floor.** If a 2-week diary shows SE <85% with ≥9 h in bed, cut time in bed by about half the gap between time in bed and sleep time (Spielman). **Never go below about 8 h in bed** — a researcher-proposed floor based on the 8–10 h need and on teen restriction studies showing impairment at 6.5 h; it has not been validated. Add 15 min when SE >85–90% for a week.
  4. Involve a parent, as de Bruin suggested.
- **Red line.** If the teen drives, any schedule change that raises sleepiness means no driving while drowsy (AASM caution). In many countries a 16-year-old is not yet driving; the country is unknown.

### Gaps
- No study defines a safe minimum time in bed for adolescents undergoing sleep restriction or compression.
- No trial of self-administered SRT or sleep compression in adolescents was found.
- No data on drowsy-driving incidents during adolescent CBT-I.

## 4. Cognitive techniques: sleep worry and unhelpful beliefs, scheduled/constructive worry, to-do list, cognitive shuffle, paradoxical intention, cognitive restructuring, clock-watching

### Takeaway
Cognitive therapy for insomnia works in adults, though more slowly than behavioral therapy, and its gains are durable. In teens it is part of most CBT-I packages, and Sleep Ninja measurably reduced unhelpful sleep beliefs, but it did not reduce rumination, which mediates the insomnia–depression link. Evidence by technique:
- **Paradoxical intention:** moderate evidence (adult; old trials). Its effect on SOL is ~0 when compared with active treatments.
- **Constructive worry / scheduled worry time:** small adult/college RCTs showing reduced pre-sleep cognitive arousal.
- **Bedtime to-do list:** one PSG lab RCT in healthy young adults (n = 57, d = 0.63).
- **Cognitive shuffle (serial diverse imagining):** only conference abstracts with developer involvement.
- **Avoiding clock-watching:** experimental support.
None of these single techniques has been tested in adolescents with insomnia.

### Cited Findings
- **Cognitive therapy vs behavioral therapy vs full CBT-I** (Harvey et al. 2014; n = 188 adults, 8 sessions) **[ADULT]**: responders at post-treatment were CBT 67.3%, behavioral therapy 67.4%, cognitive therapy 42.4%. At 6 months: CBT 67.6%, cognitive therapy **62.3%**, behavioral therapy **44.4%**. "Both BT and CT are effective, with a more rapid effect for BT and a delayed action for CT" — [PMC4185428](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4185428/)
- **AASM 2021** found "no studies meeting our inclusion criteria" for cognitive therapy as a single component, and insufficient evidence for paradoxical intention or mindfulness as single components **[ADULT]** — [AASM review](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.8988); [guideline](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)
- **Unhelpful beliefs in teens.** Sleep Ninja reduced dysfunctional beliefs about sleep (DBAS) vs control: d = 0.13 post, **d = 0.40** at 14 weeks. Pre-sleep arousal fell (d = 0.10, post only) **[TEEN]** — [Werner-Seidler 2023](https://researchnow-admin.flinders.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/132459860/Werner_Seidler_Effects_P2023.pdf)
- **Rumination.** In the same trial (n = 264), rumination (but not unhelpful beliefs) mediated the insomnia–depression link. "CBT-I led to reductions in unhelpful beliefs about sleep, but not in rumination." Authors: "Targeting rumination may improve current therapeutic approaches" **[TEEN]** — [Li et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13932)
- **Catastrophic beliefs** — e.g., "if I don't sleep tonight, I am going to have a rubbish week" — and family scripts ("we are all poor sleepers") "can contribute to sleep difficulties" **[TEEN clinical review]** — [BMJ Paeds Open 2024](https://bmjpaedsopen.bmj.com/content/8/1/e001229)
- Cognitive restructuring featured in 7 of 9 adolescent digital trials, and constructive worry in 6 of 9 **[TEEN; preprint]** — [Salamanca-Sanabria et al. 2025 preprint](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-6609677/v1)
- **Constructive worry** (Carney & Waters 2006; N = 33 undergraduates with insomnia; 5 nights) **[young ADULT]**:
  - The constructive-worry group had lower pre-sleep cognitive arousal than a worry-monitoring control — [doi](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15402010bsm0401_2)
  - The full dissertation reports **no significant effects** on somatic arousal, anxiety, sleep diary or actigraphy.
  - Procedure: 15 min between 6 and 8 pm. List at least 3 problems likely to keep you awake on one half of a page and "the next step that could contribute to the resolution" on the other half. Fold the sheet and put it on the nightstand. If worry arises at bedtime, remind yourself "they had dealt with their problems when they were at their problem-solving best."
  - Source: [LSU dissertation](https://repository.lsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1563&context=gradschool_dissertations)
- **Constructive worry added to behavior therapy** (Jansson-Fröjmark et al.; n = 22; 4 weeks) **[ADULT]**: adding it to sleep restriction plus stimulus control gave larger reductions in insomnia severity (controlled d = 1.10–1.68) and worry (d = 0.76–1.64). Responders were 80–100% vs 18–27%, but "none of the participants remitted"; this was a small feasibility RCT — [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8260.2011.02018.x)
- **Scheduled worry period** ("stimulus control for worry"; McGowan & Behar 2012; n = 53 high-trait worriers): a daily 30-min time- and place-restricted worry period for 2 weeks beat "focused worry" on worry, anxiety, negative affect **and insomnia** **[ADULT]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22977265/)
- **Bedtime to-do list** (Scullin et al. 2018, J Exp Psychol Gen) **[young ADULT, healthy]**:
  - Design: 57 healthy 18–30-year-olds wrote for 5 min before a single PSG night.
  - Instruction: "spend the next five minutes writing down everything you have to remember to do tomorrow and over the next few days."
  - Result: the to-do-list group fell asleep faster than the "completed activities" journaling group (d = 0.63). More specific lists were associated with faster sleep onset (r = −0.39).
  - The authors call for an RCT in people with insomnia.
  - Source: [PMC5758411](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5758411/)
- **Cognitive shuffle / serial diverse imagining (Beaudoin)** **[young ADULT, very preliminary]**:
  - Conference abstract: 154 university students (137 female) with excess cognitive pre-sleep arousal were randomized to the imagining app (a concrete word every 8 s, to be imagined), structured problem-solving, or both. All groups improved (partial η² .43–.71). No untreated control; developer-affiliated (CogSci Apps / mySleepButton) — [SFU Summit](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/16196)
  - Developer blog: preliminary, developer-funded results vs a backward-counting app control claim a greater drop in insomnia severity and lower perceived SOL; the developer labels these "merely preliminary" — [mySleepButton blog](https://mysleepbutton.com/en/blog/preliminary-results-of-a-study-comparing-the-cognitive-shuffle-with-backward-counting-like-counting-sheep/)
- **Paradoxical intention** **[ADULT]**:
  - AASM description: "Patients are instructed to remain awake as long as possible after getting into bed" — [AASM 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)
  - Meta-analysis (Jansson-Fröjmark et al. 2021; 10 trials, 8 from North America, several decades old): vs passive controls, large improvements (difficulty falling asleep g = 1.71). Vs active controls, moderate improvements in difficulty falling asleep, awakenings and restedness, but SOL **g = 0.00** and negligible TST. Sleep-performance anxiety fell (g = 1.04; 3 studies). Fail-safe N was small; "methodologically stronger studies are needed" — [J Sleep Res](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13464)
- **Clock monitoring** (Tang, Schmidt & Harvey 2007) **[ADULT]**:
  - Experiment 1 (30 good + 30 poor sleepers): clock monitors reported more pre-sleep worry and longer SOL.
  - Experiment 2 (38 adults with primary insomnia): clock monitoring vs a neutral digit display raised worry and subjective SOL (36.6 vs 27.4 min at baseline) and increased overestimation of SOL (24.3 vs 10.8 min).
  - Effects on objective sleep were mixed.
  - Source: [paper PDF](https://www.med.upenn.edu/cbti/assets/user-content/documents/Tang2007_SleepingwiththeEnemy-ClockMonitoringintheMaintenanceofInsomnia.pdf)
- The SENSE adolescent workbook teaches that night-time is not a good time for dealing with worries, and uses a "worry box" and a set worry time **[TEEN program material]** — [SENSE workbook](https://psychologicalsciences.unimelb.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/2780585/Workbook-for-publication-2018-for-upload.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Evidence grades.**

  | Technique | Grade | Basis |
  |---|---|---|
  | Cognitive restructuring within CBT-I | Moderate | Adult RCT; teen belief change in Sleep Ninja |
  | Paradoxical intention | Moderate (adult) / no teen data | Old adult trials |
  | Constructive worry / scheduled worry | Weak | Small college/adult RCTs; effect on arousal more than on sleep |
  | To-do list | Weak | Single-night lab study, healthy adults |
  | Cognitive shuffle | Weak / preliminary | Abstracts, conflicts of interest |
  | Hiding the clock | Weak–moderate | Experimental, adult |
- **Practical self-application.**
  1. **Early-evening constructive worry**, 10–15 min after homework (Carney & Waters protocol), on paper.
  2. **5-min specific to-do list** at the start of the wind-down (Scullin wording).
  3. **Re-examine "8 hours or tomorrow is ruined."** Use a diary to compare the predicted with the actual next-day performance after short nights. Replace the belief with, e.g., "A short night is unpleasant but I can still function; one night doesn't decide the week." (This is standard CBT-I cognitive work; teen data show DBAS can shift.)
  4. **In bed, if the mind races**, use a neutral imagery or distraction task (shuffle-style: picture unrelated objects every few seconds). Harmless, though weakly evidenced.
  5. **Turn clocks away and keep the phone out of reach.**
  6. **Paradoxical intention** (lie in the dark with eyes open, passively trying to stay awake rather than trying to sleep) may suit a teen who "tries too hard" to sleep. Stop it if it raises anxiety (researcher's caution; no teen data).
- **Rumination.** Given the Sleep Ninja mediation findings, a teen with persistent rumination or low mood may need more than sleep techniques — a reason for professional evaluation if mood symptoms are present.

### Gaps
- No adolescent studies of constructive worry, to-do lists, cognitive shuffle, or paradoxical intention as single techniques.
- The cognitive shuffle has no peer-reviewed full-length RCT; only abstracts and developer posts were found.
- The minutes-faster figure for the to-do list was not extracted (only d = 0.63 was verified).

## 5. Relaxation and arousal reduction: PMR, slow breathing (6/min, 4-7-8), body scan, mindfulness, yoga nidra/NSDR, guided imagery; evidence for Calm, Headspace, Insight Timer, Balance

### Takeaway
- **Relaxation therapy** (PMR, abdominal breathing, autogenic training, imagery, meditation) has a **conditional AASM recommendation in adults**. In teens it appears only within multicomponent programs (de Bruin; SENSE with mindfulness; Sleep Ninja relaxation audio).
- **Mindfulness** improves subjective sleep vs non-specific controls (adults ES ≈ 0.33) but is **not better than active sleep treatments**, with very few adolescent studies.
- **Slow breathing** has small, mixed studies. **4-7-8** has no robust sleep-outcome evidence. **Yoga nidra** has a handful of small adult RCTs at moderate–high risk of bias. **"NSDR"** has no dedicated trials found.
- **Commercial apps:** Calm and Headspace have industry-linked adult RCTs showing small-to-moderate effects on pre-sleep arousal, sleepiness and insomnia severity. No adolescent sleep RCTs were found for any of these apps, and no sleep RCTs for Insight Timer or Balance. Only Insight Timer offers Russian-language content.

### Cited Findings
- **AASM relaxation therapy:** "Structured exercises designed to reduce somatic tension (eg, abdominal breathing, progressive muscle relaxation; autogenic training) and cognitive arousal (eg, guided imagery training; meditation)." CONDITIONAL recommendation **[ADULT]** — [AASM 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)
- **Teen programs:** de Bruin's adolescent protocol included relaxation techniques — [PMC4667374](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4667374/). Sleep Ninja includes "clinician-guided relaxation audio" and sleep meditation — [Black Dog fact sheet](https://www.blackdoginstitute.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/BDI_Sleep-Ninja_Fact-sheet-v2.pdf). The SENSE CBT+mindfulness program improved SOL, sleep quality and anxiety vs an active control in 12–17-year-olds — [SENSE](https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000142)
- A preprint review reports that adding body-scan meditation to adolescent CBT-I "may have an additional positive effect on sleep" (citing a de Bruin study) **[TEEN; preprint, not peer-reviewed]** — [Salamanca-Sanabria 2025](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-6609677/v1)
- **Mindfulness meta-analyses:**
  - Kanen et al. 2015: 16 studies, n = 575, ages 8–87. Sleep-log effects: SE ES 0.88, TST 0.47, WASO −0.84, SOL −0.55. PSG/actigraphy changes not significant. "Interpretation is limited … especially in adolescent populations" (2 adolescent studies) **[mixed]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/276102944_The_Effects_of_Mindfulness-Based_Interventions_on_Sleep_Disturbance_A_Meta-Analysis)
  - Rusch et al. 2019: 18 RCTs, n = 1,654. Vs specific active controls (e.g., evidence-based sleep treatments) ES 0.03 — no difference (low strength of evidence). Vs non-specific active controls ES 0.33 post and 0.54 at follow-up (moderate strength) **[ADULT]** — [PMC6557693](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6557693/)
- **Slow-paced breathing:**
  - Tsai et al. 2015: 14 self-reported insomniacs + 14 good sleepers. 20 min of 0.1 Hz (6/min) breathing before bed reduced SOL, awakenings and wake time and raised SE on PSG in the insomnia group (within-subject comparison) **[ADULT]** — [doi](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.12333)
  - Laborde et al. 2019: n = 64 healthy young adults (mean 22 y). 15 min/evening for 30 days via app (4.5 s in / 5.5 s out) vs 15 min of social media improved PSQI and overnight vagal activity **[young ADULT]** — [PMC6406675](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6406675/)
  - Kuula et al. 2020: n = 20 young adults. A single 30-min session at 5 breaths/min had "no robust effects" on PSG sleep **[young ADULT]** — [Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-64218-7)
- **4-7-8 breathing:** a physiology study in 43 healthy 19–25-year-olds showed acute HRV and blood-pressure changes but measured no sleep outcomes — [PMC9277512](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9277512/). A 2026 RCT in nursing students (registered n = 82; PSQI after 4 weeks) is published, but its results were not retrieved — [Eur J Integr Med 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eujim.2026.102620); [NCT06103344](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06103344)
- **Yoga nidra:**
  - Datta et al. 2021: RCT, n = 41 adults with chronic insomnia, yoga nidra vs CBT-I. Both improved subjective and objective TST and wake time; yoga nidra increased N3 — [doi](https://doi.org/10.25259/nmji_63_19)
  - Systematic review 2025: 6 RCTs, n = 244. "Most studies had moderate to high RoB" and adverse events were poorly reported — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41144325/)
  - Online yoga nidra RCT (n = 362): small effects on stress and mood (d = 0.08–0.16). The sleep-quality effect vs waitlist was not significant (CI −0.18 to 0.02) — [PMC12080877](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12080877/)
  - All of the above are **[ADULT]**.
- **Calm app** (Huberty et al. 2021, PLOS One; n = 263 adults with ISI >10; ≥10 min/day for 8 weeks vs waitlist) **[ADULT]**:
  - Calm reduced fatigue, daytime sleepiness (d = 0.30), and cognitive (d = 0.33) and somatic (d = 0.46) pre-sleep arousal.
  - Sleep quality was assessed within the intervention group only.
  - The lead author was Calm's Director of Science (declared).
  - Sources: [PLOS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0244717); [COI statement](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33411779/)
- **Headspace** **[ADULT]**:
  - 8-week RCT in 135 university staff: better sleep quality at weeks 2, 5 and 8. Fitbit-measured sleep duration was +24 min at week 5 only; no SE effect — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41637757/)
  - "Headspace Sleep Program" RCT (n = 132 adults with clinical insomnia; CBT-I techniques plus mindfulness vs waitlist): insomnia severity 14.8 → 11.95 (post) → 10.70 (3-week follow-up). Remission 12–14% vs 0–3%; "effect sizes were small" — [PMC12059489](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12059489/)
- **Smartphone sleep apps generally:** a 2024 meta-analysis of 19 RCTs found insomnia g = 0.60 and sleep disturbance g = 0.70, with no effect on daytime sleepiness or WASO; dropout 13.1% **[mostly ADULT]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39213858/). The ESRS 2023 guideline cites a network meta-analysis in which "smart phone applied CBT-I did not attain significant effects compared with control" **[ADULT]** — [ESRS 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14035)
- **Language and cost of the meditation apps:**

- No randomized sleep trials were found for Insight Timer or Balance (searched; none surfaced).

### Inferences
- **Evidence grades.**

  | Technique | Grade |
  |---|---|
  | Relaxation within CBT-I | Moderate (adult) / component-level (teen) |
  | Mindfulness | Weak–moderate (adult); weak (teen, inside SENSE) |
  | Slow breathing ~6/min | Weak |
  | 4-7-8 | Expert opinion / very weak |
  | Yoga nidra | Weak |
  | NSDR | No direct evidence |
  | Calm / Headspace | Weak (adult, industry-linked) |
  | Insight Timer / Balance | No sleep-trial evidence |
- **Practical self-application.**
  1. Learn one technique in the **daytime** for 1–2 weeks before relying on it at night. Options: PMR (tense and release muscle groups), slow breathing at about 6/min (inhale ~4–5 s, exhale ~5–6 s, as in Laborde's app protocol), or a body scan.
  2. Then use it in the wind-down and when awake at night.
  3. Treat it as "letting the body settle," not as a tool to force sleep, to avoid sleep effort / performance anxiety (link to paradoxical intention).
  4. For Russian-language guided audio, Insight Timer's free library is the practical option, though quality varies by teacher. Calm, Headspace and Balance have no Russian.
  5. If audio is used at night, start it before lights-out or keep the screen dark (screen/light topics belong to other sections).
- **Caution.** For a small minority, relaxation or meditation can increase awareness of anxiety; stop if distress rises (general caution; no teen sleep data found).

### Gaps
- No adolescent RCTs of PMR, breathing, yoga nidra/NSDR, or of Calm/Headspace/Insight Timer/Balance for sleep were found.
- The results of the 2026 4-7-8 RCT were not retrieved.
- No guided-imagery-specific trial was retrieved (AASM groups it within relaxation).

## 6. Night awakenings: what to do, and normalizing brief awakenings

### Takeaway
Brief awakenings are a normal part of sleep architecture at all ages, and the awakening index rises with age through adolescence. The insomnia problem is prolonged, distressing wakefulness, often fuelled by checking the time and worrying. Standard CBT-I guidance applies (no clock or phone checking; if awake and frustrated, get up to a dim, boring activity and return when sleepy), but the evidence base is adult and component-level. Adolescent CBT-I effects on WASO are inconsistent, and teens in de Bruin's trial had little WASO. In the Sleep Ninja pilot, night awakenings fell from 1.47 to 0.87 per night.

### Cited Findings
- **Brief awakenings in healthy sleepers** **[ADULT, incl. late teens]**: in 55 community controls (late teens to early 70s), one-night PSG showed a median of ~4 classic (Rechtschaffen & Kales) awakenings per hour and ~21 brief EEG arousals per hour by ASDA criteria. "High arousal frequencies occur in the normal population" — [Mathur & Douglas 1995](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/18.5.330)
- **Pediatric normative PSG** **[1–18 y]**: "awakening index" increases with age while WASO decreases with age — [Scholle et al. 2011](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1389945711001122)
- **2026 meta-analysis** (66 studies, n = 2,947 healthy children): the number of awakenings increases with age — [Van Eyck et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1183/16000617.0224-2025)
- **Sleep Ninja pilot** (12–16 y with sleep difficulties): night awakenings fell from 1.47 to 0.87 per night; WASO did not change significantly — [BMJ Open](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/9/5/e026502)
- **Adolescent WASO outcomes are mixed** **[TEEN]**:
  - No WASO effect: Ma 2018 ([PMC6002144](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6002144/)); Åslund 2018 at post-treatment ([JCSM](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.7498)); Rohmah 2026 NMA ([PMC13360328](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13360328/))
  - WASO improved: Åslund at follow-up; Cleary 2025 within-group g = 0.47 ([JSR](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14466)); Hornsey 2024 night waking SMD −0.97, very low certainty ([JAACAP](https://www.jaacap.org/article/S0890-8567(24)02021-5/fulltext))
- **Get out of bed when unable to sleep** is an AASM stimulus-control instruction **[ADULT]** — [AASM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/). The pediatric version uses 20 minutes, followed by a quiet, unrewarding activity — [PMC7687719](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7687719/)
- **Clock monitoring** increases worry and overestimation of time awake **[ADULT]** — [Tang et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbtep.2005.07.004)
- **de Bruin adolescents** rarely met criteria for the 20-minute get-up rule (little WASO) **[TEEN]** — [PMC4667374](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4667374/)

### Inferences
- **Evidence grade.** Normalizing awakenings: **Moderate** (normative PSG data). Get-up rule for awakenings: **Moderate (adult) / expert opinion (teen)**. Not checking clock/phone: **Weak–moderate** (adult experiments).
- **Practical script for a night awakening** (derived from stimulus control and clock-monitoring evidence):
  1. Tell yourself: "Waking between sleep cycles is normal; I don't need to know the time."
  2. Don't look at the phone or clock.
  3. Stay in bed relaxed (breathing or body scan) as long as you feel calm and drowsy.
  4. If you become alert or frustrated (feels like ~15–20 min), get up to a dim spot and do something boring (paper book, puzzle). Return when eyelids are heavy.
  5. Keep the same wake time next morning regardless of the night.
- **When to escalate.** If awakenings come with loud snoring, gasping or pauses in breathing, leg kicking or urges to move, nightmares, pain, or frequent urination, or if unrefreshing sleep and sleepiness persist despite adequate time in bed, professional evaluation is warranted. Medical red flags are covered by another section.

### Gaps
- No adolescent study tested the get-up rule or the no-clock instruction specifically for middle-of-night awakenings.
- No normative adolescent data were extracted on how many awakenings are typically *remembered*.

## 7. Wind-down routine: recommended duration and components; evidence

### Takeaway
A 30–60 minute wind-down is universally recommended for adolescents and is built into evidence-based teen programs (SENSE: 30 minutes; Sleep Ninja: nightly wind-down reminders and a personalized routine). However, no trial has isolated its effect, so the evidence is component-level or expert opinion. One small study found restricting phones in the hour before bed advanced lights-out by about 17 minutes and added about 19 minutes of sleep.

### Cited Findings
- "Make the 30 to 60 minutes before bedtime a quiet or wind-down time … reading a book or listening to soothing music … Do not watch TV, study, exercise, or get involved in 'energizing' activities in the 30 minutes before bedtime" **[expert, teen-directed]** — [Nationwide Children's](https://www.nationwidechildrens.org/specialties/sleep-disorder-center/sleep-tips-for-adolescents)
- The SENSE adolescent workbook prescribes "a wind-down routine that you do half an hour before bed." Suggested contents: dim lights; mindfulness of breath or another relaxation exercise; peaceful music; quiet reading ("nothing too exciting"); a bath; a caffeine-free warm drink. It also recommends a personal plan with "a set time for homework and activities to be finished and all media to be switched off" **[TEEN program material]** — [SENSE workbook](https://psychologicalsciences.unimelb.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/2780585/Workbook-for-publication-2018-for-upload.pdf)
- Sleep Ninja provides "reminders to start a wind-down routine each night" (pilot), and a "personalised wind-down routine and reminders" (child version) **[TEEN]** — [pilot](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2018-026502); [ANZCTR](https://www.anzctr.org.au/Trial/Registration/TrialReview.aspx?id=385818)
- Expert recommendations: "regular bedtime routines in the hour before bed, consisting of calming activities and avoidance of screen media." AASM guidance endorsed by the AAP: screens off 30 minutes before bedtime. A phone restriction in the hour before bed among 63 adolescents (mean 16 y) led to lights-out 17 min earlier and TST +19 min per night **[TEEN, small study]** — [Hale et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5839336/)
- Behavioral review table for youth: "Set/maintain regular bedtime routine; Avoid stimulating play, electronics 1 hour before bed" — [PMC7687719](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7687719/)

### Inferences
- **Evidence grade.** **Expert opinion / component-level (Weak).**
- **Practical 45-minute template for the target teen** (assembled from the cited materials; screen and light details belong to other sections):

  | Time before lights-out | Step |
  |---|---|
  | T − 60 | Homework and gaming end; phone set to charge outside the bed |
  | T − 45 | 5-min to-do list; lights dimmed |
  | T − 40 | Shower; prepare for the morning |
  | T − 25 | Calm, non-screen activity (paper reading, music) |
  | T − 10 | Relaxation or breathing |
  | T | Lights out only when sleepy |

- Constructive worry belongs earlier in the evening, not in the wind-down. The routine should be the same every night so it becomes a cue.

### Gaps
- No RCT isolating the wind-down routine or its optimal duration in adolescents was found.

## 8. Sleep hygiene education alone: insufficient as a stand-alone treatment

### Takeaway
Sleep hygiene education (гигиена сна) alone is not an adequate treatment for insomnia:
- **Adults:** AASM 2021 recommends against it as a single therapy (conditional). Meta-analyses show it is inferior to CBT-I by about 3.8 insomnia-severity points (42 RCTs, 2025).
- **Adolescents:** school-based sleep education raises knowledge but yields small, short-lived or absent changes in sleep behavior.
It remains a useful foundation within CBT-I.

### Cited Findings
- **AASM 2021**, Recommendation 6: "We suggest that clinicians not use sleep hygiene as a single-component therapy for the treatment of chronic insomnia disorder in adults (CONDITIONAL)", "due to the lack of evidence for its efficacy" — although "certain common-sense principles … may nevertheless be helpful in a comprehensive treatment approach" **[ADULT]** — [AASM 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853203/)
- **Chung et al. 2018** (Fam Pract; 13–15 RCTs, mostly adults, mean age ~52):
  - Sleep hygiene education produced small-to-medium pre–post improvements but "was significantly less efficacious than CBT-I."
  - Differences: about 8% in diary sleep efficiency and 2 PSQI points. Only subjective measures differed.
  - "None of the studies included only young adults or adolescents."
  - Source: [doi](https://doi.org/10.1093/fampra/cmx122); [pre-print with tables](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/103689/2/Yeung_Sleep_Hygiene_Education.pdf)
- **Ruan et al. 2025** (42 RCTs, n = 4,245 adults): sleep hygiene education was inferior to CBT-I (insomnia severity MD 3.8 points), to partial CBT-I (4.5), to exercise (2.9) and to acupressure (1.9). 86% of trials were at high risk of bias **[ADULT]** — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40449065/)
- **School-based sleep education** **[TEEN]**:
  - Chung et al. 2017: 7 RCTs; 1,876 educated students vs 2,483 controls. Weekday TST SMD 0.23 and better mood immediately after, but "the improvements were not maintained at follow-up"; no SOL effect — [J Sch Health](https://doi.org/10.1111/josh.12509)
  - Hong Kong cluster RCT (n = 3,713): sleep knowledge improved (d = 0.51), but there was "no significant impact on sleep duration or pattern" — [Pediatrics 2015](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/135/3/e635/75501/A-School-Based-Sleep-Education-Program-for)
  - Gaskin et al. 2024: 21 studies, 10,867 youths. "Sleep education interventions were typically ineffective"; effects were "at best, transient" — [Sleep Advances](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpae019)
  - Blunden et al. 2012 review: knowledge increased but "did not necessarily equate to sleep behaviour change" — [Sleep Med Rev](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1087079211000918)
  - 2026 meta-analysis of cluster RCTs: overall effectiveness "not supported." Programs incorporating behavior-change techniques were associated with better TST (g = 1.06) and weekday bedtime (g = −0.56), with high heterogeneity — [J-STAGE 2026](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jsleepenvi/20/1/20_13/_article/-char/en)
- "Sleep hygiene alone cannot address insomnia … but it provides a solid foundation for the more active components of the intervention, such as sleep restriction and stimulus control" **[TEEN clinical review]** — [BMJ Paeds Open 2024](https://bmjpaedsopen.bmj.com/content/8/1/e001229)
- The Sleep Ninja RCT used weekly sleep-hygiene tips by SMS as the **active control**; the full CBT-I app outperformed it on insomnia severity (d ≈ 0.4) **[TEEN]** — [Werner-Seidler 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.13795)

### Inferences
- **Evidence grade** that sleep hygiene alone is insufficient: **Moderate** (adult meta-analyses and guideline; consistent adolescent school data; the Sleep Ninja RCT effectively tested CBT-I vs hygiene tips in teens).
- **Practical implication.** A 16-year-old who "already knows the rules" but still can't sleep needs active components: stimulus control, schedule regularity, cognitive techniques, a sleep diary and behavior-change support (goal setting, self-monitoring, parental support) — not more tips.

### Gaps
- Irish et al. 2015 (Sleep Med Rev) was not retrieved directly; conclusions rest on AASM 2021 and the Chung/Ruan meta-analyses instead.

## 9. Digital CBT-I programs and apps for teens (2026): evidence, age limits, cost, language — including Russian-language options

### Takeaway
- **Only one fully automated CBT-I app has been designed for and RCT-tested in adolescents: Sleep Ninja** (Black Dog Institute, Australia). It is free, English-only, intended for ages 12–16, piloted in 16–18-year-olds in New Zealand, and its effect is modest (d ≈ 0.4). It **omits sleep restriction**. Its App Store listing appears to be Australia-region only; US, UK and RU store pages returned "not found."
- The best-evidenced adult programs are unavailable to a 16-year-old: **Sleepio/SleepioRx** (18+, English, US prescription or NHS); **Somryst** (22+, US prescription only); **SHUTi** (now offered mainly for research).
- Free US government apps **Insomnia Coach** and **CBT-i Coach** are English-only and tested only in adult veterans.
- **No Russian-language CBT-I program with published trial evidence was found.** Russian-capable apps (Zomni, No Insomnia Lab, OpenSleep "Я Сплю") and paid Russian courses (sleepclass.ru) are "evidence-informed" at best.
- An adolescent network meta-analysis favors **web-based (largely guided)** CBT-I; ESRS prefers guided digital CBT-I.

## 10. Insomnia vs delayed body clock: how to tell, and how the approach differs

### Takeaway
- **Delayed sleep–wake phase (DSWPD, задержка фазы сна):** when free to sleep on their own schedule (weekends, holidays), the teen falls asleep quickly and sleeps long and well, just late. The school-day problem is "can't fall asleep early plus can't wake up," with morning sleepiness.
- **Insomnia:** difficulty sleeping persists even when timing is free, and is driven by arousal, worry and conditioned wakefulness.
- **Both together:** over half of adolescents meeting DSWPD criteria also meet insomnia criteria.
- **Approach:** a sleep diary of at least 7–14 days that includes free days is the key tool. For delay, shift timing (morning light, consistent wake time — covered in other sections). For insomnia, use CBT-I components. CBT-I can shorten sleep latency but is unlikely to advance clock timing, so suspected delay should be addressed first or in combination.

### Cited Findings
- **DSWPD** "is often confused with sleep initiation insomnia." "When released from such schedule constraints, sleep duration and quality is normal; it is just delayed." Assessment should look for "otherwise normal sleep when left to sleep ad libitum," using diaries and actigraphy on free days, often across three weekends — [Nesbitt 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803043/)
- **Prevalence** in a Norwegian teen study was 3.3%, and "over half of the respondents meeting the diagnostic criteria for DSWPD also met those … for insomnia." In an Australian clinic sample only 57% had a true melatonin-confirmed phase delay; 43% were "non-circadian" — [Nesbitt 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5803043/)
- **ICSD-3-TR DSWPD criteria:**
  - A. Delayed major sleep episode, with inability to fall asleep and difficulty waking at the required time.
  - B. Duration ≥3 months.
  - C. On an ad libitum schedule, improved sleep quality and duration but a still-delayed phase.
  - D. Sleep log (plus actigraphy if possible) for ≥7 days, preferably 14, including school/work and free days.
  - E. Not better explained by another disorder.
  - Source: [PMC9993405](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9993405/)
- **Response to ad libitum scheduling** "distinguishes disorders of phase from other differential diagnoses, such as chronic insomnia … which do not see improvements in sleep quality or wakefulness when allowed to sleep as much or as often as desired." Logs typically show a delay "usually greater than 2 hours." "Over half of adolescents with DSWPD also meet criteria for insomnia" — [Semin Pediatr Neurol 2023 review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071909123000608)
- **Gradisar's interview screen for teens:** difficulty falling asleep on school nights; difficulty getting out of bed; first feeling alert only by mid-morning; feeling alert after dinner; waking spontaneously and feeling "heaps better" on weekends; late, long, restful sleep on free days. "Once asleep, these adolescents stay asleep" — [APS InPsych 2014](https://psychology.org.au/inpsych/2014/april/gradisar)
- **DSPD in teens:** sleep onset is delayed 3–6 h vs conventional 10–11 pm; unrestricted sleep is normal in length (often ≥9 h). Forced early waking "may mask the true nature of the problem, resulting in a diagnosis of psychophysiological insomnia" — [Bartlett et al., MJA 2013](https://www.mja.com.au/journal/2013/199/8/circadian-rhythm-disorders-among-adolescents-assessment-and-treatment-options)
- **Clinical vignette** of a 16-year-old misdiagnosed with insomnia who actually had DSPD — [Baroni, JAACAP 2019](https://www.jaacap.org/article/S0890-8567(18)32061-6/abstract)
- **Treatment interaction:** cognitive and behavioral techniques "are likely to reduce the sleep latency of adolescents with a delayed circadian rhythm … [but] are unlikely to advance the timing of sleep onset." "It is recommended to treat suspected circadian rhythm delay before undertaking CBT-I," and bright light therapy can be combined with CBT-I — [Ewart et al. 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15402002.2025.2529856)
- **CBT-I plus bright light** in adolescents with delayed phase produced earlier bed and wake times, reduced latency and awakenings, longer sleep, and reduced sleepiness and fatigue, with gains maintained at 6 months (Gradisar 2011 RCT, as summarized) — [PMC7687719](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7687719/)
- **Sleep Ninja pilot:** wake time did not shift (school constraint); the authors conclude "a focus on bed times rather than rise times is likely to be most useful" for this age group — [BMJ Open](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/9/5/e026502)

### Inferences
- **Evidence grade.** The differentiation criteria are **Strong / consensus** (ICSD-3-TR). Combined treatment is supported by one adolescent RCT (**Moderate**).
- **Self-check for the target teen** (two weeks of a paper diary including a weekend or holiday):

  | Pattern in the diary | Suggests | Focus |
  |---|---|---|
  | Sleep onset on free days is late (e.g., 01:00–02:00) but quick, with long, refreshing sleep | Circadian delay (± insufficient sleep) | Timing/light measures (other sections) before or alongside CBT-I |
  | Long time to fall asleep or long awakenings even on free days, with worry or racing mind in bed | Insomnia | CBT-I components (stimulus control, cognitive techniques, relaxation; gentle compression only, per Q3) |
  | Both patterns present (common) | Mixed | Combine both approaches |

- **Unrefreshing sleep and daytime sleepiness** in a teen with a fixed 06:30–07:30 wake time strongly suggests insufficient sleep and/or delay. Persistent sleepiness despite ≥8.5–9 h of sleep opportunity warrants medical evaluation (red flags are covered by another section).

### Gaps
- No validated self-administered questionnaire for distinguishing insomnia from DSWPD in teens was retrieved (diagnosis rests on clinician interview plus diary/actigraphy).
- Melatonin-based phase assessment (DLMO) is clinical/research-only and outside self-help scope.
