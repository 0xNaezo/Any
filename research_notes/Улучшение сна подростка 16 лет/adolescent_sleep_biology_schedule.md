# Adolescent sleep biology and evidence-based sleep-schedule strategy for a 16-year-old with a fixed early school wake time

_Evidence-grade key used in these notes: **Strong** = guideline/consensus backed by meta-analyses or several RCTs/large prospective cohorts with consistent results; **Moderate** = at least one adolescent RCT or controlled lab experiment, or consistent meta-analyses of observational data; **Weak** = small, single, cross-sectional, abstract-only, or adult-only extrapolation; **Expert** = consensus or narrative opinion. Tags: **ADOL** = adolescent data (about 13–19 y); **ADULT** = adult-only evidence; **YA** = young adults. "Secondary" = figure reported by a citing paper; the primary paper was not opened in this session. Research done in October 2026. Out of scope here and covered by other researchers: light devices and light dosing, screens/caffeine/exercise/food, CBT-I and relaxation, bedroom environment, trackers/smart alarms/snooze, medical red flags._

## 1. Why teen sleep shifts later: pubertal DLMO delay, slower build-up of sleep pressure, and the collision with early school start

### Takeaway
Puberty delays the circadian clock: DLMO (dim-light melatonin onset) moves later, with about 1 h shifts in the steepest two-year windows and up to about 2 h overall versus middle childhood. Homeostatic sleep pressure also builds more slowly while awake, though it does not dissipate faster during sleep. Older teens therefore stay alert about 1 h longer after DLMO, but they need no less sleep. Early school start times fix the wake time while sleep onset stays late. Sleep gets cut short, and the teen is woken during his biological night. This shows up as morning sleep latencies of about 5 min and REM-onset morning naps.

### Cited Findings
**Overall model**
- **[Expert/narrative review; ADOL]** Carskadon's "perfect storm" model has three parts. First, maturation of the circadian timing system produces a phase delay. Second, homeostatic sleep pressure rises more slowly. Third, psychosocial factors push sleep later: self-selected bedtimes, academic pressure, and evening technology and social networking. Early school start is the main force limiting sleep time, "so adolescents sleep too little and are asked to be awake at an inappropriate circadian phase." The model also states that these processes do not reduce the length of sleep needed. — [Carskadon 2011, Sleep in Adolescents: The Perfect Storm](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3130594/)
- **[Expert/review; ADOL]** A 2018 update of the model kept the same structure and cited newer work, including "Increased Sensitivity of the Circadian System to Light in Early/Mid-Puberty" (J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2015). — [Crowley, Wolfson, Tarokh, Carskadon 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6054480/)

**Circadian (Process C) delay: evidence and size**
- **[Moderate; ADOL; cross-sectional + longitudinal]** Two 1993 studies first linked the delay to puberty. Andrade et al. (longitudinal) found later sleep at more advanced Tanner stage. Carskadon et al. found that 6th-grade girls who rated themselves more mature had more evening-type preference. A later lab study held sleep schedules fixed for two weeks and found DLMO positively correlated with Tanner stage. — [Carskadon 2011](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3130594/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; longitudinal cohort, n=94]** Crowley et al. 2014 (PLoS One) followed a 9–10 y cohort (n=38) and a 15–16 y cohort (n=56). Each was measured about every 6 months for 2.5 years with home actigraphy and lab salivary DLMO. DLMO and self-selected sleep onset both delayed with age. "The largest (~1 h) shift" in DLMO occurred between ages 11–13 in the younger cohort and between 17–19 in the older cohort. Weekday wake times moved earlier with age through high school. — [Crowley et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0112199)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; same study]** The phase angle from DLMO to sleep onset was about 1 h in younger adolescents and about 2 h in the older cohort, similar to adults on self-selected schedules. The waking day averaged 15 h 20 min in the younger cohort and 16 h 22 min in the older cohort. The authors attribute this to slower accumulation of sleep pressure, plus more bedtime autonomy. — [Crowley et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0112199)
- **[Expert; ADOL]** "Beginning at the onset of puberty, adolescents develop as much as a 2-hour sleep-wake phase delay (later sleep onset and wake times) relative to sleep-wake cycles in middle childhood." In practice, "the average adolescent has difficulty falling asleep before 11 PM, and the ideal wake time is around 8 AM"; "optimal sleep amounts remain at about 9 to 9¼ hours." — [Owens, Belon, Moss 2010, JAMA Pediatrics (introduction)](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/383436)
- **[Expert; NSF]** The later shift in circadian rhythms persists "until a teen reaches their early 20s." — [National Sleep Foundation, What to know about teens and sleep (2024)](https://www.thensf.org/what-to-know-about-teens-and-sleep/)
- **[Weak; ADOL; preliminary, n=27]** Intrinsic circadian period measured 24.27 h in 27 adolescents aged 9–15. This was significantly longer than adult averages reported by others. A longer "internal day" favors later timing unless morning light resets it. — [Crowley, Acebo, Carskadon 2007, Sleep Med (PDF)](http://www.sleepforscience.org/stuff/contentmgr/files/6033aedfc83ddcdaeb659ca3a6b64849/pdf/crowley_et_al_sleep_med_2007.pdf); restated in [Carskadon & Tarokh 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658519/)
- **[Weak; ADOL + animal]** Possible light mechanisms. Juvenile mice show an exaggerated delay response to evening light. Teens "stay up later" and so get phase-delaying evening light, while they "shield themselves from morning, phase-advancing light." Night-time melatonin levels decrease across puberty, and lower amplitude may fail to dampen evening arousal. — [Carskadon & Tarokh 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658519/)
- **[Weak; conflicting]** A preliminary study found no difference in melatonin suppression by 150 and 500 lux light between Tanner 1/2 and Tanner 3/4/5 adolescents. — [Crowley et al. 2007](http://www.sleepforscience.org/stuff/contentmgr/files/6033aedfc83ddcdaeb659ca3a6b64849/pdf/crowley_et_al_sleep_med_2007.pdf). A later 2015 study is titled "Increased sensitivity…to light in early/mid-puberty"; its details were not retrieved. — [Crowley et al. 2018 reference list](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6054480/)

**Homeostatic (Process S) changes**
- **[Moderate; ADOL; EEG modeling and MSLT]** Jenni et al. modeled slow-wave activity around 36 h of sleep deprivation. Process S built up more slowly during wake in mature adolescents (Tanner 5) than in pre/early-pubertal ones (Tanner 1–2), and had a higher upper asymptote. Dissipation of Process S during sleep did not differ. Tanner 1 participants fell asleep faster at the 22:30 and 00:30 evening tests. — [Crowley et al. 2007](http://www.sleepforscience.org/stuff/contentmgr/files/6033aedfc83ddcdaeb659ca3a6b64849/pdf/crowley_et_al_sleep_med_2007.pdf); [Carskadon 2011](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3130594/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL]** On a multiple sleep latency test across 36 h of extended wake, less mature adolescents fell asleep faster than more mature ones at 14.5 h and 16.5 h after waking. Summary: "the accumulation of sleep homeostatic pressure slows during puberty," dissipation "does not change until late adolescence," and "the need for sleep is stable (or increases)." — [Carskadon 2011](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3130594/)
- **[Weak; modeling, cross-species]** Adolescence brings a decline of more than 60% in slow-wave EEG activity. Two-process simulations show that slowing the homeostatic time constants "reliably produced a delay in the timing of sleep" without requiring a large decrease in sleep need. — [Hagenauer & Lee 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23998671/). The sleep EEG loses 40–50% of its amplitude across the second decade, thought to reflect synaptic pruning. — [Carskadon & Tarokh 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4658519/)

**Collision with early school start times**
- **[Moderate; ADOL; natural experiment]** Carskadon et al. 1998 followed students whose school start moved from 08:25 in 9th grade to 07:20 in 10th grade. In 10th grade they woke earlier but did not fall asleep earlier, and slept less. DLMO was later (mean 21:02 vs 20:24). Mean MSLT latency fell from 11.4 to 8.5 min. At the 08:30 test it fell from 10.9 to 5.1 min. In 10th grade, 48% had one REM-onset episode and 16% had two. Those with morning REM had later DLMO. — [Carskadon et al. 1998, SLEEP](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/21/8/871/2726004). The review interprets this as clocks "phase delayed relative to the early start time," favoring sleep during school hours. — [Carskadon 2011](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3130594/)
- **[Strong (policy) / Moderate (evidence); ADOL]** On 25 Aug 2014 the AAP recommended that middle and high schools start at 08:30 or later. — [JCSM 2014 editorial (written by the authors of the Kentucky crash study) summarizing AAP 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4224717/); [AAP 2014 policy cited in Bin-Hasan et al. 2020](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7075093/)
- **[Moderate; meta-analysis, 28 studies, n=1,774,509]** Later school start times were associated with longer sleep (small effect, ES=0.109, 95% CI 0.019–0.199) and less negative mood (ES=0.060). New start times of 08:30–08:59 gave better outcomes than 08:00–08:29. Benefits were concentrated in youth whose initial start was 07:30–07:59. There was no significant association with academic outcomes. — [Yip et al. 2022, Pediatrics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9665092/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; pre/post actigraphy, n=92 vs 88 sophomores]** Seattle moved start from 07:50 to 08:45. Median wake time moved 44 min later, sleep onset was not significantly later, and median school-day sleep rose 34 min (6 h 50 min to 7 h 24 min). Median grades were 4.5% higher, and tardies and absences improved in the lower-income school. — [Dunster et al. 2018, Science Advances](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6291308/); [UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2018/12/12/high-school-start-times-study/)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADOL; survey pre/post]** A 30-min delay in start time was associated with school-night sleep +45 min. Students sleeping 8 h or more rose from 16.4% to 54.7%. Sleepiness, fatigue and depressed mood all fell. — [Owens et al. 2010](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/383436)
- **[Weak; ADOL; actigraphy, n=41, 12–14 y]** Compared with afternoon-shift students (12:30 start), morning-shift students (07:00 start) had similar sleep onset but slept 1 h 45 min less on school days. Only the morning shift showed social jetlag (1.03 h vs −0.02 h). — [J Biol Rhythms 2020, afternoon school start study](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0748730420927603)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; random assignment to school shift, n=753]** In morning school (07:45), early chronotypes outperformed late chronotypes in all subjects, most in maths. The effect vanished in afternoon school, and late chronotypes benefited from evening classes. — [Goldin et al. 2020, Nature Human Behaviour](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-020-0820-2)

### Inferences
- A 16-year-old boy is most likely in late puberty. On a school schedule his DLMO is plausibly around 20:30–21:30 (the 10th-grade mean in Carskadon 1998 was 21:02). His spontaneous sleep onset is then roughly 2 h later, around 22:30–23:30, unless his clock is actively advanced. A 06:30–07:30 wake time is close to the end of his biological night, which explains waking unrefreshed and morning sleepiness even with "OK" hours.
- The biological tendency is real, but its size is amplified by light timing (late evening light, little morning light) and by weekend lie-ins (see Q3–Q4). These are the levers a teen can control without a school-policy change.
- Boys tend to mature later than girls, so the delay may still be progressing at 16 to 17. Crowley 2014 saw a steep DLMO delay at 17–19. Expect the schedule to need active defence for several years.
- Lying in bed long before the clock-driven sleep window opens is unlikely to produce sleep. The plan should shift the clock and the bedtime together (Q4), not just set an early lights-out.

### Gaps
- No retrieved source gave DLMO clock times by Tanner stage, or the cumulative childhood-to-age-20 DLMO delay in hours. "Up to about 2 h" rests on an expert statement (Owens 2010) plus the longitudinal ~1 h windows (Crowley 2014). Roenneberg 2004 ("end of adolescence" chronotype peak at about age 20) was not retrieved.
- Crowley & Eastman 2018 ("Free-running circadian period in adolescents and adults," J Sleep Res) was not retrieved. The claim that adolescents have a longer tau rests on preliminary n=27 data.
- The details and effect size of the 2015 "increased light sensitivity in early/mid-puberty" study were not retrieved, so the light-sensitivity mechanism remains partly unresolved.

## 2. How much sleep a 16-year-old needs, how many teens get it, and the measured costs of chronic short sleep

### Takeaway
Guidelines agree on 8–10 h per 24 h for 13–18-year-olds (AASM 2016; NSF 14–17 y). Only about 23% of US high-school students get 8 h or more on school nights (YRBS 2023), and this share is declining. Chronic short sleep in teens is linked to mood deficits (pooled OR 1.55), anxiety and depression risk, impaired attention, working memory and executive function (shown experimentally), higher obesity incidence (RR 1.30), more sports injuries (OR 1.58), worse glucose handling, and drowsy driving and crashes.

### Cited Findings
**Recommendations**
- **[Strong; guideline; ADOL]** AASM consensus (modified RAND method; 864 articles reviewed): "Teenagers 13 to 18 years of age should sleep 8 to 10 hours per 24 hours on a regular basis." The panel agreed that fewer than 8 h or more than 10 h regularly was inappropriate. Too little sleep is linked to attention, behavior and learning problems, and to higher risk of accidents, injuries, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, depression, self-harm, suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts. — [Paruthi et al. 2016, JCSM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5078711/); [AASM summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4877308/); [AASM teen health advisory 2019](https://aasm.org/advocacy/position-statements/teen-sleep-duration-health-advisory/)
- **[Strong; guideline]** NSF recommends 8–10 h for teens aged 14–17. — [Miller et al. 2018 citing NSF](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/41/4/zsy018/4833233); for adolescence "7–11 hours may be appropriate" (secondary, Hirshkowitz 2015) — [Yip et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9665092/). NSF's public page says teens "typically need between 8-10 hours, sometimes up to 11." — [NSF 2024](https://www.thensf.org/what-to-know-about-teens-and-sleep/)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADOL; daily diary, secondary]** About 9 h of sleep was needed for the lowest next-day depressed mood and anxiety (Fuligni et al.). — [Short et al. 2020 meta-analysis discussion](https://pure-oai.bham.ac.uk/ws/files/96044841/1_s2.0_S108707922030054X_main.pdf)

**Who meets it**
- **[Strong; national surveillance; ADOL]** In 2023, 23% of US high-school students got 8 h or more on an average school night. Males were more likely than females to do so, and the share fell from 2013 to 2023. — [CDC YRBS Data Summary & Trends 2013–2023](https://www.cdc.gov/yrbs/dstr/dietary-physical-sleep-behaviors.html); [CDC PDF](https://www.cdc.gov/yrbs/dstr/pdf/DSTR-Dietary-PhysicalActivity-SleepBehavior2013-2023-508.pdf). Healthy People 2030 lists 23.2% (2023) against a baseline of 25.4% (2017) and a target of 27.4%, status "getting worse." — [HP2030 SH-04](https://odphp.health.gov/healthypeople/objectives-and-data/browse-objectives/sleep/increase-proportion-high-school-students-who-get-enough-sleep-sh-04)
- **[Weak; survey]** "Less than 2 out of 10 teens report getting the NSF's recommended 8-10 hours on both school days and weekends." In the 2024 Sleep in America Poll, teens were nearly half as likely to get 8–10 h on school nights as on weekends. — [NSF 2024](https://www.thensf.org/what-to-know-about-teens-and-sleep/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; actigraphy]** Median school-day sleep was 6 h 50 min with a 07:50 start in Seattle. — [Dunster et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6291308/). Japanese high-school students (n=1,314) averaged 390 ± 56 min of weekday sleep. — [Napping/club study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33089175/)

**Mood, anxiety, depression, risk behaviour**
- **[Strong; meta-analysis, 74 studies, n=361,505 adolescents]** Shorter sleep was associated with 55% higher odds of mood deficits (OR 1.55; I²=97.9%). By mood state: reduced positive affect at roughly double the odds, anger +83%, depressed mood +62%, negative affect +60%, anxiety +41%. The effect held across regions and regardless of covariate adjustment. — [Short et al. 2020, Sleep Med Rev](https://pure-oai.bham.ac.uk/ws/files/96044841/1_s2.0_S108707922030054X_main.pdf)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; prospective, secondary]** In about 3,000 US youths aged 11–17 (as reported by the review), sleeping less than 6 h per school night predicted more anxiety and depression symptoms one year later (Roberts & Duong). In YRBS data (n≈67,000; Weaver et al.), 7 h, 6 h and under 6 h showed stepwise higher odds of risky driving, risky sex, aggression and substance use. — [Uccella et al. 2023 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10136689/)

**Cognition (experimental, adolescent)**
- **[Moderate; ADOL; randomized parallel-group lab study, n=56, 15–19 y]** In the "Need for Sleep" study, 7 nights of 5 h in bed (versus 9 h) caused cumulative deterioration in sustained attention, working memory, executive function and speed of processing, more sleepiness and less positive mood. Effect sizes: processing speed (MAT) f²=1.14; sleepiness f²=0.50; positive affect f²=0.17; negative affect f²=0.07. Sustained attention and sleepiness had not recovered after 2 nights of 9 h recovery sleep. Controls given 9 h in bed slept about 8.0–8.2 h by polysomnography and kept their performance. — [Lo et al. 2016, SLEEP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4763363/)

**Metabolic**
- **[Moderate; meta-analysis of prospective studies]** Short sleep in adolescence predicted incident overweight or obesity (3 cohorts, n=26,652; RR 1.30, 95% CI 1.11–1.53; I²=0%). Each extra hour of sleep was associated with BMI −0.03 kg/m². — [Miller et al. 2018, SLEEP](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/41/4/zsy018/4833233)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; lab, n=58]** Teens on a split schedule (5 h night plus a 1.5-h nap) had a 44–49% larger glucose excursion on oral glucose tolerance tests than teens on 6.5 h of continuous night sleep. — [Lo et al. 2019, SLEEP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6519912/)

**Sports injuries**
- **[Moderate; systematic review/meta-analysis, 7 studies, adolescents under 19]** Chronically poor sleepers were more likely to be injured (OR 1.58, 95% CI 1.05–2.37). Milewski 2014: under 8 h per night, adjusted RR 1.7 (1.0–3.0). Von Rosen 2017 (elite adolescent athletes): under 8 h on weekdays, OR 2.4 (1.0–5.9). Most studies used self-reported sleep. — [Gao et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6446394/)

**Drowsy driving and crashes**
- **[Weak–Moderate; ecological pre/post]** In Fayette County, Kentucky, high-school start moved from 07:30 to 08:30. Crash rates for 17–18-year-old drivers fell 16.5% while the rest of the state rose 7.8%. — [Danner & Phillips 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2603528/). **Conflict:** an NHTSA re-analysis (16–17-year-olds, school days only) found no overall shift in Kentucky. A 75-min delay in Forsyth County, North Carolina showed a 14% drop in the all-day crash rate. — [NHTSA Traffic Tech 812227](https://www.nhtsa.gov/sites/nhtsa.gov/files/documents/812227-schoolstarttimesandteendrivercrashestt.pdf)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ecological]** In Fairfax County, start moved from 07:20 to 08:10 and teen crash rates fell from 31.63 to 29.59 per 1,000 licensed drivers (OR 1.07, 95% CI 1.0–1.14), with no change elsewhere in Virginia. Evening chronotypes had 15.2% higher adjusted prevalence of drowsy driving. — [Bin-Hasan et al. 2020, JCSM](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7075093/). After a 70-min delay (07:10 to 08:20), frequent drowsy driving fell from 32.6% to 21.9%, and weekday teen crash rates fell versus neighbouring counties. — [Meltzer et al. 2022, J Adolescence](https://doi.org/10.1002/jad.12053)
- **[Weak; context]** More than half of fall-asleep crashes involve drivers aged 25 or younger. Per mile, drivers aged 16–19 are three times as likely as drivers 20 and older to be in a fatal crash. — [JCSM editorial 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4224717/). AAA estimates that 16.5% of fatal crashes involve a drowsy driver. — [NHTSA](https://www.nhtsa.gov/sites/nhtsa.gov/files/documents/812227-schoolstarttimesandteendrivercrashestt.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Numeric target for this teen:** at least 8 h of actual sleep on every school night (the AASM floor), with about 9 h as the optimum (Fuligni's mood optimum; Lo's 9-h-in-bed controls). Because sleep efficiency is below 100%, about 8 h of real sleep needs about 8.5–9 h in bed (see Q7).
- He is in the roughly 77% majority if he sleeps under 8 h. Daytime sleepiness, low mood or irritability, and attention problems are the expected dose-dependent costs, not unusual weakness. The experimental data (Lo 2016) show these effects are causal.
- If he drives, chronic short sleep plus an evening chronotype is a safety issue. Adolescent evidence links early start and short sleep to drowsy driving. A practical rule (inference): no driving in the first 30 min after waking, or after a night under about 6 h.

### Gaps
- The Dewald 2010 meta-analysis of sleep and school performance is cited by retrieved papers, but its effect sizes were not retrieved. No adolescent RCT of home sleep extension with grades as the outcome was found.
- YRBS prevalence by single year of age (16 y) was not retrieved.
- Crash evidence is ecological and partly contradictory (NHTSA re-analysis). No individual-level data linked a teen's sleep hours to his own crash risk.

## 3. Sleep regularity versus duration; social jetlag and weekend catch-up sleep: harm, help, and how big a lie-in is reasonable

### Takeaway
Regularity is an independent pillar of sleep health. In adolescents, irregular timing (especially variable wake times) predicts depressive symptoms, substance use, worse executive function and cardiometabolic risk even after controlling for duration. The NSF 2023 consensus rated regularity "important for health" at the top of its scale. Weekend catch-up sleep is double-edged:
- In sleep-deprived teens, up to about 1–2 h of extra weekend sleep is associated with better mood than none.
- More than 2 h of social jetlag or catch-up, or weekend sleep 1.5× weekday sleep or more, is associated with more depression and lower well-being.
- A typical teen weekend (bed +1.5 h, wake +3 h) delays the adolescent clock by about 45 min in two nights, making Sunday-night sleep onset and Monday mornings harder.

### Cited Findings
**Regularity: consensus and adult anchors**
- **[Strong/Expert; NSF consensus; mostly adult evidence]** A panel reviewed 63 publications using a modified Delphi RAND/UCLA method. Consensus: daily regularity in sleep timing is important for health (median rating 9, the highest) and for performance. When weekday sleep is insufficient, weekend catch-up sleep is important for health (rating 8). "Irregular schedules were not associated with improved outcomes in any study." The panel concluded that individuals should optimize regularity, "however, when sleep duration is inadequate during work days, 1-2 hours of catch-up sleep on non-work days may be beneficial." No single variability threshold could be set, but a 1-h standard deviation in sleep onset was associated with 23% higher metabolic-syndrome risk and 18% higher cardiovascular risk. Some data suggest irregular timing may hurt performance more than insufficient sleep. — [Sletten et al. 2023, Sleep Health](https://www.sleephealthjournal.org/article/S2352-7218(23)00166-3/fulltext); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37684151/); [NSF press release](https://www.thensf.org/sleep-schedules-sleep-timing-guideline/)
- **[Weak; secondary]** "Some of the strongest papers showed GPA was worse among college students when their sleep was more irregular." — [Weaver Q&A, Harvard Gazette/MedicalXpress 2023](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2023-09-qa-importance-regularity.html)
- **[Moderate; ADULT; prospective, n=79,666 UK Biobank, accelerometry]** Regular sleepers (SRI 71 or higher) versus irregular (SRI 51 or lower) had lower risk of depression (HR 0.62) and anxiety (HR 0.67). Irregular sleepers who still met duration guidelines had elevated risk (depression HR 1.48; anxiety HR 1.35). Each 1-SD increase in SRI gave 14% lower depression risk, and there was no interaction with duration. — [UK Biobank SRI cohort (PMC12404321)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12404321/)

**Regularity: adolescent studies**
- **[Moderate; ADOL; review of 26 studies (25 cross-sectional, 1 longitudinal)]** Greater night-to-night variability and irregularity were associated with obesity (especially central adiposity), type 2 diabetes, cardiac autonomic dysregulation and higher blood pressure. "One hour of irregularity in the timing of the sleep midpoint is considered abnormal for a typical adolescent," and irregularity peaks in adolescence. — [Morales-Ghinaglia & Fernandez-Mendoza 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9975080/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; prospective, n=295, mean 15.4 y, 1-week actigraphy, 6-month follow-up]** A lower Sleep Regularity Index was associated with substance-use history, first-onset substance use and a prospective rise in depressive symptoms, "even after accounting for sleep duration and subjective sleep disturbances." Irregular teens also had shorter sleep, longer sleep latency, more social jetlag and later midpoints. — [Walsh et al. 2025, J Sleep Res](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.14468)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADOL; n=46, mean 12.8 y, about 133 nights of actigraphy]** Sleep was most regular on school days. Higher school-day SRI went with longer sleep, shorter latency, higher efficiency, and fewer depressive symptoms at 6 months; effects were small to medium. — [Castiglione-Fontanellaz et al. 2023, J Sleep Res](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.13865)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADOL; high-school actigraphy]** Night-to-night variability in sleep duration (B=0.72) and in **wake time** (B=0.63) was associated with depressive symptoms. Variability in sleep onset was not, after adjustment including social jetlag. — [Kwon, Seo, Hasler 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11693472/)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADOL; n=350, mean 14.3 y, 14 days actigraphy + diaries]** Higher SRI buffered the next-day link between poor nights (long latency, disturbance) and anxious mood or somatic symptoms. — [Yan et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12353120/)
- **[Weak; ADOL; conference abstract, n=84, 14–18 y]** Variability of sleep midpoint predicted worse executive function (BRIEF; R²=0.063). — [Jex et al. 2025, SLEEP abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsaf090.0227)

**Social jetlag (SJL = |mid-sleep on free days − mid-sleep on school days|)**
- **[Moderate; meta-analysis, 8 studies, n=148,410, ages 10–24]** SJL of 2 h or more was associated with depressive symptoms (OR 1.44, 95% CI 1.18–1.77). SJL of 1–2 h was not significant (OR 1.05, 1.00–1.09). Overall r=0.16. — [Sun et al. 2025, BMC Psychiatry](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12888-025-07066-x)
- **[Moderate (GRADE very low); meta-analysis, 14 studies, n=164,529, adolescents/young people]** SJL correlated with depression (Fisher's z=0.27) and anxiety (z=0.21). SJL of 1–2 h gave OR 1.12 (1.05–1.20) and over 2 h gave OR 1.87 (1.73–2.02). — [Lu & Tsai 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12811408/)
- **[Weak; ADOL; n=146, about 16 y, Brazil, diaries]** Average SJL was 1 h 58 min (girls) and 1 h 49 min (boys) in morning-class students. — [Diogo et al. 2024, Sleep Science](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0043-1777831). SJL rises across adolescence and peaks at about 16–17 y (secondary). — [Uccella et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10136689/)
- **[Weak; ADOL; n=542, mean 15.6 y, actigraphy]** Short sleep, later bedtimes and more SJL were associated with lower school performance. — [Bacaro et al. 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3425/13/2/178)

**Weekend catch-up sleep (WCS): mixed and dose-dependent**
- **[Moderate (cross-sectional only); meta-analysis, 10 studies, n=326,871, US and Korea, adults and adolescents]** WCS was associated with lower depression odds (OR 0.80, 0.68–0.90). Moderate WCS (0–2 h) appeared protective, while more than 2 h had limited protective effect. The benefit was concentrated in people sleep-deprived on weekdays. — [Zhou et al. 2025, J Affect Disord](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40021063/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; n=270,619, Korea YRBS 2015–2019]** No catch-up (ratio of weekend to weekday sleep under 1.0) combined with under 5 h on weekdays had the strongest association with depressive symptoms and suicidal ideation. A ratio of 1.5 or more was associated with more depressive symptoms even with good sleep quality. The paper also reports that 35–40% of US secondary students extend weekend sleep by more than 2 h (secondary). — [Lee et al. 2022, Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-14352-1)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADOL; n=504, mean 16.2 y, 7-day actigraphy]** Among teens sleeping under 7 h, WCS over 120 min was associated with lower subjective well-being than WCS of 0–59 min. — [Tonetti et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9819690/). Secondary, from US National Comorbidity Survey-Adolescent data (Zhang et al.): WCS over 2 h went with more mood and behaviour disorders and suicidality than WCS of 1 min–2 h, while 1 min–2 h went with less anxiety than none. — [Tonetti et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9819690/). **Conflict:** a Korean high-school study (Koo et al.) found WCS over 2 h associated with lower depression risk (secondary). — [Lee et al. 2022](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-022-14352-1)

**Experimental circadian cost of lie-ins**
- **[Moderate; ADOL; two lab-assessed experiments, ages 15–17, n=12 and n=33]** On a "typical" weekend (bed 1.5 h later, wake 3 h later, 1.5 h more sleep per night), DLMO delayed 45 ± 31 min (Exp 1) and 46 ± 34 min (Exp 2) between Friday and Sunday; effect size about 1.35. Two modified weekends did not prevent the delay. A NAP weekend (wake only 1 h later plus a 2-h afternoon nap, bedtime still later) delayed DLMO 41 ± 34 min. A LIGHT weekend (1 h of blue light at the late wake time) delayed it 38 ± 28 min. — [Crowley & Carskadon 2010, Chronobiol Int](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3415304/)
- **[Moderate; YA (n=16; young adults per citing literature)]** Delaying only weekend wake time by about 3 h on two mornings delayed DLMO by 42 min. Sunday-night sleep latency rose by 8 min, and fatigue and sleepiness were higher on Monday and Tuesday ("Monday morning blues"). — [Taylor, Wright, Lack 2008, Sleep Biol Rhythms](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1111/j.1479-8425.2008.00356.x); age of sample per [citing paper summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227780817_Sleeping-in_on_the_weekend_delays_circadian_phase_and_increases_sleepiness_the_following_week)
- **[Weak; ADOL; conference abstract/press]** A simulated weekend sleep pattern reset the clock later and was associated with poorer Monday functioning: "teenagers may be giving themselves jetlag over the weekend." — [AASM press release on Crowley SLEEP 2007 abstract](https://aasm.org/late-weekend-sleep-among-teens-may-lead-to-poor-academic-performance/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL]** Two recovery nights of 9 h did not restore sustained attention or sleepiness after a week of 5-h nights. — [Lo et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4763363/). Sleep architecture was also not restored after 2 recovery nights. — [Ong et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28329386/)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADOL; small controlled maintenance study, n=14]** A maintenance protocol kept DLMO stable at home for 3 weeks (−4.9 ± 22.9 min) with stable weekday sleep. Its rules: the same wake time on all 7 days, weekend bedtimes no more than 1 h later, afternoon naps on weekends for catch-up, and one weekend morning of light-box exposure. — [Crowley et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11417015/)
- **[Weak; ADOL; RCT follow-up]** Of 15 treated adolescents with delayed sleep phase, 2 relapsed by 6 months because of late weekend and holiday sleep onset and rise times. — [Gradisar et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3208844/)

### Inferences
- **Regularity versus duration:** for this teen they are complementary, not competing. Regular timing, above all a fixed wake time, appears to carry independent mental-health benefit. In adolescents, wake-time variability, not bedtime variability, was the one linked to depressive symptoms. Regularity is also the mechanism that lets the clock advance and stay advanced, which makes more sleep possible.
- **Weekend numeric rules (synthesis):**
  - Keep weekend wake time within **≤1 h** of the school-day wake time; the ideal is the same time, and **2 h is a hard ceiling**.
  - Keep weekend bedtime within **≤1 h** of the school-night bedtime.
  - Target **social jetlag under 1 h**, avoid 2 h or more, and keep weekend total sleep under about 1.5× weekday sleep.
  - If he still needs catch-up, take it as an early-afternoon nap (Q5) rather than a long morning lie-in.
- **Worked example:** school nights 22:45–06:30 put mid-sleep at about 02:37. Weekend nights 00:30–10:30 put mid-sleep at 05:30, giving SJL of about 2.9 h, in the high-risk band. Weekend nights 23:30–07:30 put mid-sleep at 03:30, giving SJL of about 0.9 h.
- The Sunday lie-in is the most costly: it hits just before the Monday 06:30–07:30 alarm. The adolescent clock delays about 40–45 min over one late weekend, while advances come slowly (Q4), so one late weekend can undo most of a week's progress.
- Crowley & Carskadon 2010 found that waking earlier plus a nap still delayed the clock when bedtimes stayed 1.5 h later. Crowley 2024 found a stable clock with a fixed wake and bedtimes no more than 1 h later. Taken together, this suggests late weekend nights (evening light, late sleep onset) are as important to control as late mornings.
- Catch-up should not become the strategy. The best evidence (Lo 2016) shows two weekend recovery nights do not fully repay a week of short sleep. Weekday sleep has to rise.

### Gaps
- No adolescent RCT randomizes teens to different lie-in lengths and measures mood or school outcomes. The 1–2 h guidance rests on mostly adult consensus plus cross-sectional adolescent data, some of it conflicting (Koo vs Lee/Tonetti).
- No validated adolescent cut-off for SRI exists. The "1 h midpoint irregularity is abnormal" statement comes from a narrative review.
- Windred et al. (SRI as a stronger mortality predictor than duration; adult) was cited by retrieved papers but not opened.

## 4. Behavioral protocols to shift a teen's sleep earlier, and how fast the clock can realistically move

### Takeaway
The best adolescent evidence (Crowley/Eastman lab RCTs, 2018–2024) shows that sleep can be moved meaningfully earlier with four elements:
- (a) a fixed wake time;
- (b) a gradual bedtime advance (1 h per week, or 15–30 min steps);
- (c) evening time management so the earlier bedtime actually happens;
- (d) morning bright light (dosing is covered by another researcher).

The results were DLMO about 0.6 h earlier, sleep onset about 1.5 h earlier and about +1.1–1.2 h of school-night sleep within 2 weeks, held for at least 3 more weeks with a "same-wake-time" maintenance plan. Under ideal lab conditions the teen clock can advance about 1 h over a weekend or about 2 h over 3 days. In daily life it delays faster (about 45 min per late weekend) than it advances. Late evenings with room light can block or reverse morning-light advances.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate; ADOL; randomized controlled lab/home trial, n=46, 14.5–17.9 y, school-night sleep ≤7 h, bedtimes ≥23:00]**
  - **Intervention:** school-night bedtime 1 h earlier than baseline in week 1 and 2 h earlier in week 2. An individualized evening time-management plan ("Sleep RouTeen") supported it. On the middle weekend, about 6,000 lux light for 2.5 h was given on both mornings, timed after the sleep midpoint.
  - **Results versus control:** DLMO advanced 0.6 ± 0.8 h vs −0.1 ± 0.8 h. By week 2, sleep onset was 1.5 ± 0.7 h earlier and school-night sleep was 1.2 ± 0.7 h longer. Teens reported more alertness on waking, less daytime sleepiness, better concentration, and less irritability and worry.
  - **Authors' view:** teens with early circadian phases "may only need a time management plan," while later phases probably need both time management and morning light.
  - — [Crowley et al., SLEEP 2023 (online 2022)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9832518/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsac202)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADOL; controlled, n=14, 15.3–17.9 y]** After the same 2-week intervention, DLMO advanced 37.0 ± 40.0 min (control −14.7 ± 16.6). Weekday sleep rose 69.7 ± 27.8 min and sleep onset came 103.7 ± 14.2 min earlier. In three home maintenance weeks the rules were: the same school wake time every day, early weekday bedtimes kept, weekend bedtimes up to 1 h later, weekend afternoon naps, and 2.5 h of light-box exposure on one weekend morning. The gains held (DLMO −4.9 ± 22.9 min). — [Crowley et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11417015/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; randomized lab study, n=37, 14.7–18.0 y]** Over one weekend, teens were woken 1 h after their usual weekend mid-sleep on Saturday and 1 h earlier on Sunday, with an early Saturday bedtime. DLMO advanced 0.6 ± 0.4 h in dim room light, 0.6 ± 0.5 h with 1.5 h of bright light, and 1.0 ± 0.4 h with 2.5 h of bright light (d=1.16 vs room light). The earlier wake and bed schedule alone produced a phase advance of more than 30 min. — [Misiunaite, Eastman, Crowley 2020, Front Neurosci](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2020.00099/full)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADOL; small lab experiment, n=6]** Sleep-satiated teens (10-h sleep opportunities) on a gradually earlier schedule with 90 min of about 6,000 lux morning light for 3 days advanced about 2 h. Teens put to bed 4.5 h later for 2 nights (evening room light plus sleep restriction) did not shift, or delayed 2–4 h, despite the same morning light. Title finding: "late bedtimes prevent circadian phase advances to morning bright light in adolescents." — [Crowley, Fournier, Eastman 2018, Chronobiol Int](https://doi.org/10.1080/07420528.2018.1504784)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; RCT of a clinician-delivered program, n=49, mean 14.6 y, diagnosed delayed sleep phase disorder]** This is cited for its behavioral scheduling elements only.
  - **Protocol:** morning light (sunlight, or a lamp of about 1,000 lux) started at the natural wake time, for at least 30 min and at most 2 h. Light time and rise time then moved **30 min earlier each day** to a target of about 06:00. After that, teens kept a regular rise time of 06:30–07:30, went to bed when sleepy, and avoided naps.
  - **Results versus waitlist:** improvements were moderate to large (d=0.65–1.24) in sleep latency, sleep onset, rise time, school-night total sleep, wake after sleep onset, sleepiness and fatigue. Per Table 2 (column order inferred from layout), school-night sleep onset went from about 00:06 to 23:28 and total sleep from 7.1 to 8.1 h. 13% vs 82% still met diagnostic criteria afterwards.
  - **Mechanism signal:** improvement in sleepiness correlated with the advance in school-night sleep onset (rs=0.60), not with the increase in total sleep (rs=−0.17).
  - — [Gradisar et al. 2011, SLEEP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3208844/); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22131604/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL]** In the adolescent phase-response curve to bright light, light beginning shortly after the midpoint of sleep produces the largest advances. — [Misiunaite et al. 2020, describing Crowley & Eastman 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2020.00099/full)
- **[Moderate; ADOL]** In the other direction, one typical late weekend delays DLMO by about 38–46 min. — [Crowley & Carskadon 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3415304/)
- **[Weak; ADOL]** Most adolescents with delayed sleep phase (85%) reported avoiding morning bright light, and their weekend rise times were much later. — [Gradisar et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3208844/)

### Inferences
- **How fast (realistic, at home):**
  - About 0.5–1 h of true clock (DLMO) advance over 1–2 weeks.
  - About 1–1.5 h earlier sleep onset over 2 weeks when bedtime discipline and morning light are combined.
  - Sleep onset moved further than DLMO in Crowley 2023 (1.5 h vs 0.6 h). Part of the gain came from simply getting into bed earlier with an evening plan, which narrowed the 2-h DLMO-to-sleep gap.
  - The lab best case is about 1 h per weekend or about 2 h per 3 days, under ideal sleep-satiated conditions with timed bright light.
  - Delays are easier: about 45 min from one late weekend.
- **Protocol synthesized for this teen (behavior-only):**
  1. **Baseline:** keep a sleep diary (paper or app) for 1–2 weeks. Record lights-out, estimated sleep onset, night awakenings, wake time and weekend times.
  2. **Fix the wake time** at his school time (e.g., 06:45) on **all 7 days**, at most 30–60 min later on weekends.
  3. **Advance lights-out by 15 min every 2–3 nights, or about 30–60 min per week.** Do not advance further while sleep latency is regularly over 20–30 min. Stop at the target from Q7.
  4. **Evening time management:** finish homework and activities and start wind-down so lights-out actually happens. Dim evening light and move screens earlier (details from other researchers).
  5. **Morning light** right after the fixed wake time (details from the light researcher). On weekends, waking at the usual time is the main tool; time-shifted light at a late wake time did not prevent drift in teens.
  6. **Maintenance:** follow the Crowley 2024 rules (same wake time, weekend bedtime no more than 1 h later, early-afternoon nap if needed, morning light at least one weekend morning).
- Do not go to bed hours earlier at once. Gradual steps matched to his clock and actual sleepiness avoid lying awake in bed, which drives conditioned insomnia (CBT-I aspects are covered elsewhere).
- **When evidence suggests professional evaluation:** chronically unable to fall asleep before about 00:00–01:00, and unable to wake for school, despite 2–4 weeks of consistent fixed-wake and gradual-advance behavior. That pattern matches delayed sleep-wake phase disorder, the most common sleep disorder in Gradisar's adolescent clinic. The RCT-supported program there was clinician-delivered (out of scope for recommendations here).

### Gaps
- No adolescent study isolates "fixed wake time 7 days/week" alone. It always comes bundled with bedtime and light changes.
- Adult numbers for daily advance rates with gradually advancing schedules (Burgess/Eastman) were not retrieved. Only adolescent figures are given above.
- The optimal step size (15 vs 30 vs 60 min) has not been compared head-to-head in teens.

## 5. Naps in adolescents: effects of length and timing on night sleep and on alertness and learning; evidence-based nap rules

### Takeaway
In sleep-restricted teens, an afternoon nap (60–90 min around 14:00–15:30) clearly improves afternoon and evening alertness, memory and mood (NUS split-sleep trials, Lo et al. 2016–2020). If total sleep in 24 h is 8 h or more, split and continuous schedules perform alike. Naps carry costs, though:
- Each extra hour of napping cuts about 20–30 min from that night's sleep.
- Late or long naps delay bedtime.
- Napping reduces homeostatic pressure needed for falling asleep at night.
- Replacing night sleep with nap sleep worsened glucose tolerance.

For a teen whose main complaint is difficulty falling asleep, short or no naps is the default. Adult data favor about 10–20 min for alertness without grogginess.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate; ADOL; randomized parallel-group, n=58, 15–19 y]** Split sleep (5 h at night 01:00–06:00 plus a 1.5-h nap 14:00–15:30) versus 6.5 h continuous (00:15–06:45). The split group had fewer vigilance lapses and better working memory, executive function, processing speed, alertness and mood, despite less total polysomnographic sleep. It had a 44–49% larger glucose excursion. Both groups were worse than teens given 9 h in bed. The authors advise "the recommended amount of nocturnal sleep." — [Lo et al. 2019, SLEEP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6519912/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; n=24 split + 29 continuous at 8 h, compared with 58 at 6.5 h]** With 8 h total opportunity per 24 h, split (night plus mid-afternoon nap) and continuous schedules "performed comparably" on vigilance, working memory, executive function, speed, sleepiness and mood. — [Lo et al. 2020, SLEEP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8061132/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; n=57, polysomnography]** A 1-h nap at 14:00 after 5-h nights reduced that night's homeostatic sleep pressure: longer N2 latency and lower total sleep time, sleep efficiency and slow-wave energy. Sleep debt still accumulated, and "there is no substitute for adequate nocturnal sleep." — [Ong et al. 2017, SLEEP](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28329386/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; naturalistic, n=153, mean 16.6 y, actigraphy + diary]**
  - Teens who napped did so about 2.3 times per week. Naps averaged 82 ± 53 min and started around 14:58; school-night sleep averaged 5.7 h.
  - On school days, napping 1 h longer shortened that night's sleep by 22 min (between-person) to 29 min (within-person).
  - On weekends, waking from a nap later than usual delayed bedtime.
  - Early school wake times increased nap likelihood.
  - The authors' conclusion: "naps may be detrimental to the same night's sleep only if they are long and occur late."
  - Secondary in the same paper: Japanese teens napping after 17:00 had bedtimes 30 min later than those napping 13:00–15:00. In an earlier NUS trial cited there, 90-min mid-afternoon naps in teens sleeping under 6.5 h changed night sleep latency little (only one of five nights was about 10 min longer).
  - — [Leong et al. 2024, SLEEP](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsae147)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; n=236 high-school students, one school week]** Shorter night sleep predicted longer napping the next day. Napping predicted shorter night sleep and worse sleep efficiency that night by actigraphy, and poorer self-rated quality. — [Jakubowski et al. 2017, Behav Sleep Med](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6499385/)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADOL; n=99 evening-type poor sleepers, mean 15.9 y, 15 days]** 76 of 99 napped at least once and 17 napped habitually. A nap day meant about 27 min less night sleep and later sleep onset. A later nap end time meant later sleep onset. Longer naps meant later onset and lower efficiency. — [Rea 2024 dissertation (Loyola)](https://ecommons.luc.edu/luc_diss/4131); [Rea, Bohnert, Crowley 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40307148/)
- **[Weak; ADOL; n=1,314, Japan, 15–17 y]** More nap days per week went with shorter night sleep. Napping 3 or more days per week was associated with more excessive sleepiness in class. — [Napping & club activities study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33089175/)
- **[Expert/review; ADOL]** Short afternoon naps are recommended. Prolonged or mistimed naps are linked to sleep inertia, metabolic changes and later bedtimes. — [Napping behavior in adolescents review 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41782-021-00155-3). NSF notes that late-day or evening naps make it harder to fall asleep. — [NSF 2024](https://www.thensf.org/what-to-know-about-teens-and-sleep/)
- **[Moderate; ADULT]** After restricted night sleep, a 10-min afternoon nap gave immediate performance gains. A 30-min nap gave none until 35–95 min after waking because of sleep inertia (Brooks & Lack 2006). Guidance: "if waking from a nap, limit sleep duration to less than 30 mins." — [Hilditch & McHill 2019, Nat Sci Sleep](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710480/)
- **[Weak; YA; n=384 college students]** Longer naps were associated with longer sleep latency and more wake after sleep onset that night. — [Mead et al. 2022, Ann Behav Med](https://academic.oup.com/abm/article/56/11/1101/6535107)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADOL]** Delayed-phase teens in the Gradisar RCT were told to avoid napping. — [Gradisar et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3208844/). The Crowley maintenance protocol, by contrast, used weekend afternoon naps instead of lie-ins and kept DLMO stable. — [Crowley et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11417015/)

### Inferences
- **Nap rules synthesized for this teen** (grade: moderate where adolescent, weak where adult extrapolation):
  1. **Default: no routine weekday naps** while the main problem is falling asleep at night and the bedtime is being advanced.
  2. **If he is dangerously sleepy** (driving, sports, exams), a **10–20 min** nap is the alertness tool with the least grogginess (adult evidence). Best timing is about 13:00–15:00, ending **by about 15:30–16:00** (inference from the adolescent timing data), and **never after about 17:00**.
  3. **Deliberate catch-up** (weekend, or after an unavoidably short night): one **60–90 min** nap starting about 13:30–14:30, instead of sleeping in. Expect about 15–30 min of post-nap grogginess and about 20–30 min less sleep that night per extra nap hour. The net gain is still positive (about +30–40 min per nap hour; inference from Leong 2024).
  4. **Do not use naps to replace night sleep.** Moving sleep from night to nap preserved cognition but worsened glucose tolerance.
- Naps count toward the AASM "per 24 hours" total. Night sleep should still carry the bulk (8 h or more), consistent with Lo 2019 and 2020.

### Gaps
- No adolescent RCT has tested a nap cut-off time (e.g., 15:00 vs 17:00) for its effect on night sleep onset. The cut-off above is inferred from observational teen data and expert guidance.
- Short naps (10–20 min) have not been studied in adolescents. Nap-length rules rely on adult data (Brooks & Lack).
- Nap effects on learning in adolescents beyond the NUS trials (e.g., Leong et al. 2023 nap-duration and memory work) were not retrieved.

## 6. Sleep inertia and morning grogginess in teens: causes, typical duration, and behavioral countermeasures

### Takeaway
Normal sleep inertia after an unrestricted morning awakening lasts about 10–30 min. It becomes longer and stronger (up to more than 70 min) after sleep loss, when waking during the biological night, and in later chronotypes. All three apply to a short-sleeping, phase-delayed 16-year-old on a 06:30–07:30 alarm: his teen peers on early schedules show morning sleep latencies of about 5 min and REM-onset naps. The best-supported fix is causal: more sleep and an earlier clock, as in Crowley 2023 where alertness on waking improved. Reactive tricks (light, cold water, sound, exercise) have only weak, mostly adult and mostly subjective evidence. Carbohydrate-rich rather than sugary breakfasts and prior-day physical activity predict better morning alertness in adults.

### Cited Findings
- **[Moderate; systematic review, 70 studies]** After a main sleep episode, 10 of 12 studies with objective measures found inertia lasting from under 7 to 32 min. With habitual morning awakening and no sleep loss (3 studies), durations were consistently 10–30 min. Three studies with prior sleep loss found under 20 to more than 70 min ("prior sleep loss seemed to exacerbate and/or prolong inertia"). Subjective sleepiness time constants were 20–40 min. The most promising countermeasures were short naps, light and caffeine; exercise, noise, face-washing and self-awakening were also studied. — [EASA research report D-4, Duration of sleep inertia](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/downloads/139387/en)
- **[Moderate; review; mostly ADULT]** Sleep inertia is "exaggerated by prior sleep loss and the time of day in which a person awakens." Proactive steps: limit prior wake time, minimize acute and chronic sleep loss, avoid waking in the biological night, keep naps under 30 min. Reactive countermeasures (light, sound, temperature) have been "either unsuccessful, or uninterpretable." One study found subjective but not objective benefit from light. Caffeine before sleep is the best-supported proactive measure (out of scope here). — [Hilditch & McHill 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710480/)
- **[Weak; structured review, 17 studies, ADULT]** "Current literature does not provide a convincing evidence-base for a reactive countermeasure." Face-washing after a 20-min nap gave an immediate but short-lived drop in subjective sleepiness and no performance benefit (Hayashi 2003). One minute of 2,000 lux light after a nap did not reduce inertia in the first 15 min, but modestly improved subjective ratings over the next 45 min. Temperature manipulation (cooling hands and feet) is promising only for subjective alertness. — [Hilditch, Dorrian, Banks 2016, Ind Health](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5136610/)
- **[Weak–Moderate; ADULT, n=18, about 22 y]** After waking from 8 h at habitual wake time, performance took about 10–30 min to recover. Later chronotypes (by DLMO or questionnaire mid-sleep) needed about 30 min or more; earlier chronotypes recovered within about 10–20 min. — [Ritchie et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5591034/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL]** On an early (07:20) school schedule, MSLT latency at 08:30 was 5.1 min (vs 10.9 min with an 08:25 start). REM sleep appeared on morning naps in about half of 10th-graders, and they had later DLMO. They were being woken near their circadian night. — [Carskadon et al. 1998](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/21/8/871/2726004); [Carskadon 2011](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3130594/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; RCT]** After the 2-week phase-advance and sleep-extension program, adolescents reported more alertness on waking and less daytime sleepiness. — [Crowley et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9832518/). In the delayed-phase RCT, daytime sleepiness improvements tracked earlier sleep timing (rs=0.60) rather than longer sleep. — [Gradisar et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3208844/)
- **[Moderate; ADULT; prospective, n=833]** Next-morning alertness improved independently with:
  - sleeping longer and/or later than one's usual;
  - more physical activity the previous day, and less movement at night;
  - a high-carbohydrate breakfast (versus a reference meal);
  - a lower blood-glucose response after breakfast.

  A high-protein breakfast predicted lower alertness, and a pure glucose drink the largest drop. Genetics played only a modest role. — [Vallat et al. 2022, Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-022-34503-2)
- **[Weak; ADOL; actigraphy, n=443 analysed]** Morning-type and evening-type adolescents differed in motor activity before waking but not after waking. The authors conclude that "morning affect" (the subjective ease of getting up) and motor sleep inertia may be different phenomena. — [Natale et al. 2024, Sensors](https://doi.org/10.3390/s24237668)

### Inferences
- **Causes for this teen, ranked:**
  1. Chronic sleep debt (likely under 8 h).
  2. Circadian misalignment: the alarm falls in his biological night, especially Mondays after weekend drift.
  3. Possibly waking from deep sleep after short nights, when deep sleep rebounds. This step is physiological inference, not sourced here.
- **Behavioral tactics with grades:**
  - **Increase sleep and advance the clock** (Q4, Q7): *moderate, adolescent RCT*. This is the main lever.
  - **Same wake time 7 days a week**, avoiding the Sunday lie-in: *moderate-indirect* (adolescent regularity and lie-in data).
  - **Plan for 15–30 min of grogginess:** wake 30–45 min before he must perform; avoid driving or high-stakes tasks in the first about 30 min. *Moderate* (systematic review duration data).
  - **Bright light and getting up out of bed right away:** *weak* for inertia itself, *moderate* for shifting the clock earlier (light details from another researcher).
  - **Breakfast within about 1 h**, carbohydrate-based and not a sugary drink: *weak–moderate, adult*.
  - **Physical activity during the previous day:** *weak–moderate, adult*. Brief movement or exercise right after waking has only *weak, adult* evidence.
  - **Cold water on the face:** *weak, adult*. Expect only a short subjective lift.
  - **Keep naps under about 20–30 min** when afternoon alertness is needed soon after: *moderate, adult*.

### Gaps
- No adolescent-specific experimental study of reactive sleep-inertia countermeasures (cold water, exercise, sound, light) was found.
- Hsiao et al. 2025 (Sci Rep, "linkage between chronotype, social jetlag, and sleep inertia") and Kovac et al. (brief exercise burst and sleep inertia, adults) were identified but not retrieved.
- Typical morning inertia duration specifically in 16-year-olds on school schedules has not been measured. Durations above are adult or mixed.

## 7. Practical arithmetic: lights-out times for 8, 9 and 10 h at wake times of 06:30, 07:00 and 07:30, and what is realistic for a teen

### Takeaway
Wake time minus target sleep minus about 15–20 min to fall asleep gives the latest lights-out. An extra 10–20 min should be allowed for normal night awakenings, and about 45–60 min if he is already well rested. Lab data on well-rested teens: 9 h in bed gave about 8.0–8.2 h of actual sleep. A typical 16-year-old falls asleep around 22:30–23:30 unless his clock is shifted. In practice:
- **8 h of sleep** is realistic with wake times of 07:00–07:30 (lights-out about 22:15–22:45), and with 06:30 after a modest 30–60 min advance (lights-out about 21:45–22:15).
- **9 h** is realistic only with a 07:30 wake (lights-out about 21:45–22:15) after an advance.
- **10 h** on school nights is not realistic for most 16-year-olds.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong/Expert; NSF consensus]** Good sleep quality across ages: sleep latency of 15 min or less, with 16–30 min also judged good. A latency of 46–60 min indicates poor quality, as does over 60 min. Awakenings longer than 5 min: 0–1 per night is good; for teens, more than 3 per night is not good. Wake after sleep onset of 20 min or less, and sleep efficiency of 85% or more, are good. — [Ohayon et al. 2017, Sleep Health](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2016.11.006); [full text](https://escholarship.org/content/qt9xc5x5h2/qt9xc5x5h2.pdf); [NSF checklist ("fall asleep in 30 min or less… awake 20 min or less… mostly asleep while in bed, i.e., 7 out of 8 hours")](https://www.thensf.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/NSF-2024-SleepQuality.pdf)
- **[Strong; guideline]** The target is 8–10 h per 24 h for ages 13–18. — [AASM 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5078711/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL; polysomnography]** Well-rested teens given 9 h in bed (23:00–08:00) slept about 7.99–8.18 h by polysomnography. Sleep-restricted teens given 5 h slept about 4.6–4.8 h, so efficiency is higher when sleep-deprived. Wrist actigraphy underestimated sleep by about 1 h versus polysomnography. — [Lo et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4763363/)
- **[Moderate; ADOL]** DLMO averaged about 21:02 in 10th-graders with a 07:20 school start. — [Carskadon et al. 1998](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/21/8/871/2726004). Older adolescents fall asleep about 2 h after DLMO. — [Crowley et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0112199). The "average adolescent has difficulty falling asleep before 11 PM" (expert). — [Owens et al. 2010](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/383436)
- **[Moderate; ADOL]** Late-sleeping teens (bedtimes 23:00 or later, about 6 h of weekday sleep) moved sleep onset 1.5 h earlier and gained 1.2 h of sleep within 2 weeks of structured behavioral and light intervention. — [Crowley et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9832518/). Adolescents with delayed sleep phase reached school-night onset of about 23:28 and about 8.1 h of sleep with a 06:30–07:30 rise-time rule. — [Gradisar et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3208844/)

### Inferences
**Arithmetic table.** "Asleep by" = wake time − target sleep. "Lights-out" = asleep-by − 15–20 min latency. In practice subtract another 10–20 min for normal night awakenings; for an already well-rested teen, about 45–60 min in total (Lo 2016 anchor).

| Wake time | 8 h sleep: asleep by | 8 h: lights-out | 9 h: asleep by | 9 h: lights-out | 10 h: asleep by | 10 h: lights-out |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 06:30 | 22:30 | 22:10–22:15 (with awakenings allowance about 21:50–22:05) | 21:30 | 21:10–21:15 (about 20:50–21:05) | 20:30 | 20:10–20:15 (about 19:50–20:05) |
| 07:00 | 23:00 | 22:40–22:45 (about 22:20–22:35) | 22:00 | 21:40–21:45 (about 21:20–21:35) | 21:00 | 20:40–20:45 (about 20:20–20:35) |
| 07:30 | 23:30 | 23:10–23:15 (about 22:50–23:05) | 22:30 | 22:10–22:15 (about 21:50–22:05) | 21:30 | 21:10–21:15 (about 20:50–21:05) |

**Realism check against teen biology.** Assume DLMO about 20:30–21:30 and spontaneous sleep onset about 22:30–23:30 before any advance.
- **Wake 07:30:** 8 h (lights-out about 23:00) is achievable now for most teens. 9 h (lights-out about 22:00) is achievable after about 30–60 min of advance.
- **Wake 07:00:** 8 h (lights-out about 22:30) is achievable now or after a small advance. 9 h (lights-out about 21:30) needs about 1–1.5 h of advance: borderline, possible over 2–4 weeks with the Q4 protocol.
- **Wake 06:30:** 8 h (lights-out about 22:00–22:15) needs about 30–60 min of advance, realistic within 1–3 weeks. 9 h (lights-out about 21:00–21:15) would need DLMO around 19:00–19:30, unrealistic for most 16-year-olds during the school year.
- **10 h on school nights:** unrealistic at any of these wake times, and above the typical need. Treat 10 h as an occasional recovery night ceiling, not a target.

**Recommended concrete targets for this teen** (synthesis; grade moderate for duration and regularity, weak to moderate for exact clock times):
1. Choose the latest school-day wake time that logistics allow (each 15 min later in the morning is sleep he will almost certainly get). Keep it 7 days a week, at most 30–60 min later on weekends.
2. Set an initial lights-out about 8.5 h before wake, e.g., 22:15 for a 06:45 wake. Move it earlier in 15-min steps every 2–3 nights toward 9 h in bed, but only while sleep latency stays at 20–30 min or less.
3. If he lies awake more than about 30 min on most nights at a given lights-out, the bedtime is ahead of his clock. Hold or step back 15 min and strengthen the clock-advancing behaviors (Q4) rather than forcing an earlier bedtime.
4. Success criteria (from the NSF quality indicators):
   - sleep latency 30 min or less;
   - wake after sleep onset 20 min or less, with at most 1 awakening longer than 5 min;
   - sleep efficiency 85% or more;
   - total sleep 8 h or more on school nights;
   - social jetlag under 1 h;
   - weekend wake time no more than 1 h later.
5. When reading wearable-derived sleep durations, note that wrist actigraphy underestimated adolescent sleep by about 1 h versus polysomnography in Lo 2016 (device accuracy is covered elsewhere).

### Gaps
- His individual DLMO is unknown, and no validated home method was retrieved. Bedtime targets must be titrated empirically with the sleep diary.
- No adolescent study tested whether teens can fall asleep reliably at a given clock time (e.g., 21:30) after a specific advance protocol. The feasibility judgements above are inferences from the DLMO-to-sleep phase angle (about 2 h) and Crowley's intervention results.
- Normative adolescent sleep efficiency at home with 8.5–9 h in bed was not retrieved separately from the lab anchor (Lo 2016).
