# Non-restorative sleep and excessive daytime sleepiness in adolescents (15–17 y): likely causes, self-checks, red flags, and when to see a doctor together with parents

> **Notes for the report writer.** Target person: 16-year-old male high-school student; fixed school-day wake time ~06:30–07:30; difficulty falling asleep, night awakenings, wakes unrefreshed, daytime sleepiness.
> **Scope guard:** several sources below also discuss treatments (melatonin, iron, nasal sprays, stimulants, surgery, CPAP). Those treatment details are deliberately **not** carried into these notes and must not become advice in the report. Use only symptoms, self-checks, and when and where to seek evaluation.
> **Evidence grades used:** STRONG = guideline action graded A/B, or consistent large adolescent population data or meta-analysis. MODERATE = a single large population study, or a meta-analysis with high heterogeneity. WEAK = small, clinic-based, cross-sectional, or case-control (spectrum-biased) studies. EXPERT = consensus definitions, good-practice statements, or narrative reviews. **[ADULT EVIDENCE]** = data not from adolescents. All sources were accessed in October 2026.

## 1. Most common causes of waking unrefreshed and daytime sleepiness in 15–17-year-olds (ranked, with prevalence)

### Takeaway
The most common cause by far is chronic insufficient sleep. About 3 in 4 high-school students sleep less than 8 h on school nights, usually because the normal adolescent shift to later sleep timing collides with early school wake-ups. Insomnia symptoms come next: about 1 in 5 to 1 in 3 adolescents depending on definition, and fewer boys than girls. A large share of what surveys call teenage "insomnia" carries signs of circadian delay. Mood/anxiety problems and substance use are the next most common contributors. Sleep-disordered breathing (~1–6%), restless legs (~2%), post-infectious fatigue, narcolepsy (~0.02–0.06%) and Kleine–Levin syndrome (~1–5 per million) are progressively rarer.

### Cited Findings
**A. Insufficient sleep (most common)**
- In the 2023 YRBS (national US school survey), only **23% of high-school students got ≥8 h on an average school night**, so roughly 77% did not. Males were more likely than females to get ≥8 h. The ≥8 h share declined over 2013–2023. [STRONG; self-report] — [CDC YRBS Data Summary & Trends 2013–2023](https://www.cdc.gov/yrbs/dstr/pdf/dstr-dietary-physicalactivity-sleepbehavior2013-2023-508.pdf)
- In 2021 the share of students not getting enough sleep was highest among females (80%), 12th-graders (84%) and Black students (84%). State figures ranged from 71% to 84%, and the share rose from 2009 to 2021. — [CDC FastStats: Sleep in High School Students](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/data-research/facts-stats/high-school-students-sleep-facts-and-stats.html)
- AASM consensus (2016; expert panel using a modified RAND method; 864 articles reviewed) says teenagers aged 13–18 should sleep **8–10 h per 24 h**. Regularly sleeping less is associated with attention, behaviour and learning problems, and with higher risk of accidents, injuries, hypertension, obesity, diabetes and depression. [EXPERT, evidence-based consensus] — [Paruthi et al., J Clin Sleep Med 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4877308/)
- Norway, youth@hordaland 2012 (n=10,220; ages 16–18): mean weekday sleep was **6 h 25 min**, a deficit of about 2 h. **65% reported taking >30 min to fall asleep.** Boys had later bedtimes and bigger weekday–weekend differences. [MODERATE–STRONG] — [Hysing et al., J Sleep Res 2013](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jsr.12055)
- A Western Australian children's-hospital pre-referral guideline states: "The most common cause of daytime sleepiness is insufficient sleep which should be excluded first". It also distinguishes sleepiness from fatigue (a subjective lack of physical and mental energy). [EXPERT] — [Perth Children's Hospital, Tiredness pre-referral guideline](https://www.pch.health.wa.gov.au/For-health-professionals/Referrals-to-PCH/Prereferral-guidelines/Tiredness)
- Excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) may affect **up to 42% of adolescents**, versus 19–27% in the general population. The review lists the main adolescent causes in this order: sleep deprivation, inadequate sleep hygiene, insomnia, circadian rhythm disorders, chronic somatic disease, psychiatric disorders, sleep-related movement and breathing disorders, parasomnias, hypersomnias, and drugs/medications. [EXPERT review] — [Narrative review of EDS in adolescents (PMC7384526)](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7384526/)
- Kentucky county-wide school district survey (1998–1999; ~10,000 students in grades 6–12 surveyed each year; analyses focused on high-school students): **43.3% scored ≥10 on the Epworth Sleepiness Scale** before a 1-hour later school start. After the change, 37% did. [MODERATE, ecological] — [Danner & Phillips, J Clin Sleep Med 2008](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/full/10.5664/jcsm.27345)

**B. Circadian delay / delayed sleep–wake phase (see Q2)**
- Clinical DSWPD affected **3.3% of 16–18-year-olds (boys 2.7%)** in Norway. — [Sivertsen et al., BMC Public Health 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-13-1163). Published estimates range from 1% to 16% depending on criteria. — [Narala et al., Curr Opin Pediatr 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/mop.0000000000001322)
- Norway, 3,867 high-school students: 34.5% met Bergen Insomnia Scale criteria (girls 41.0%, boys 26.8%). However, **88.4% of these reported ≥1 circadian-delay symptom**: trouble waking on school days, sleeping long into the day, waking ≥10:00 on free days, or oversleeping for school. Only 4.0% of the whole sample had insomnia without such symptoms. [MODERATE; cross-sectional] — [Arnesen et al., Chronobiol Int 2024/25](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07420528.2024.2444677)

**C. Insomnia (see Q2, Q6 and Q9)**
- In Norwegian 16–18-year-olds, insomnia prevalence was 23.8% by DSM-IV, **18.5% by DSM-5** (girls 23.6%, **boys 12.5%**) and 13.6% by quantitative criteria. — [Hysing 2013](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jsr.12055); sex split reported in [de Zambotti et al. review 2018](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5931364/)
- US 13–16-year-olds (Johnson 2006): DSM-IV insomnia lifetime prevalence 10.7%, current 9.4%. Across studies, rates range from 4% to 39% depending on criteria. — [de Zambotti et al. 2018](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5931364/)
- Australia (n=384; ages 13–18; questionnaires plus a 7-day diary): DSM-IV primary insomnia 7.8%, ICSD-II insomnia 10.9%, and frequent daytime consequences 34.6%. The DSM-IV figure rises to 20.8% if adolescents excluded for a possible mental disorder are included. [MODERATE] — [Dohnt et al., J Clin Sleep Med 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3365088/)
- Germany (ages 10–17; ISI; collected during COVID-19): about half reported mild-to-moderate insomnia symptoms in the past month, about one-fifth met moderate criteria, and 1.6% severe. — [Deutsches Ärzteblatt Int 2025](https://di.aerzteblatt.de/int/archive/article/245810)

**D. Substances (screens and caffeine are covered by other researchers)**
- Meta-analysis (18 adolescent studies; search to Oct 2021): any substance use was associated with sleep disturbance, OR 1.70. By substance: alcohol 1.77, coffee 1.58, smoking 1.66, and marijuana 1.29 (not significant). [MODERATE; high heterogeneity] — [Systematic review & meta-analysis (PMC12438825)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12438825/)

**E. Rarer causes (details in Q3–Q7)**
- OSA prevalence is 1.2–5.7% in children/adolescents. — [AAP guideline 2012](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-pdf/130/3/576/1088485/peds_2012-1671.pdf)
- Definite RLS affects 2.0% of 12–17-year-olds. — [Picchietti et al., Pediatrics 2007](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2006-2767)
- Narcolepsy affects about 25–60 per 100,000. — [Nexus Narcolepsy Registry, Sleep Med 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1389945721003439)
- Kleine–Levin syndrome affects 1–5 per million. — [Qasrawi & BaHammam 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9793345/)
- After infectious mononucleosis, 13% of adolescents met chronic fatigue syndrome criteria at 6 months, against 0.2% CFS in the general adolescent population. — [Katz et al., Pediatrics 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2756827/)

### Inferences
- **Approximate ranking for a 15–17-year-old boy (most to least common):**
  1. Insufficient sleep: ~3 in 4.
  2. Circadian delay: a near-universal adolescent shift; clinically significant DSWPD in ~3% of boys.
  3. Insomnia: symptoms in ~1 in 4 to 1 in 3; DSM-5 disorder in ~1 in 8 boys.
  4. Mood/anxiety problems and substance use: common, and linked to sleep in both directions.
  5. OSA: a few percent; more likely with obesity, loud snoring or large tonsils.
  6. RLS/PLMD: ~2%.
  7. Post-infectious fatigue: only relevant after a recent infection.
  8. Narcolepsy: roughly 1 in 2,000–5,000.
  9. Kleine–Levin syndrome: ~1 in 200,000–1,000,000.

  These categories overlap: more than half of adolescents with DSWPD also meet insomnia criteria (Q2).
- **The most useful single observation for this teen:** are sleep onset and morning refreshment normal when he can sleep on his own schedule (holidays, long weekends)?
  - If yes, insufficient sleep plus circadian delay is the leading explanation.
  - If no (he still lies awake, wakes repeatedly, or feels unrefreshed after long, unrestricted sleep), insomnia disorder or another sleep or medical problem needs a doctor's evaluation.

  This is inferred from the ICSD-3-TR "ad-lib sleep improves DSWPD" criterion and the Arnesen data (Q2).
- Rare causes should be presented calmly, each with its distinctive features, so readers are neither alarmed nor falsely reassured.

### Gaps
- No single community study was found that ranks all causes of EDS head-to-head in 15–17-year-olds. The ranking above combines separate prevalence studies that used different methods.
- No Russian adolescent sleep-duration surveillance figures were found in this search.
- Prevalence of depression and anxiety in 15–17-year-old males was not retrieved.

## 2. Delayed sleep–wake phase disorder (DSWPD): prevalence, diagnosis, vs normal "night owl" and vs insomnia

### Takeaway
DSWPD is the clinical extreme of the normal adolescent shift towards later sleep timing. It affects ~3% of 16–18-year-olds (published range 0.4–16% depending on definition). It is diagnosed when a habitual delay causes impairment for ≥3 months, usually >2 h later than required times, and is documented by 7–14 days of sleep logs or actigraphy. A key feature: sleep is normal when the person is allowed to sleep at the preferred time. The distinction matters for three reasons: over half of adolescents with DSWPD also meet insomnia criteria, the two need different approaches, and DSWPD independently predicts school absence.

### Cited Findings
- **Prevalence (Norway, n=10,220, ages 16–18, ICSD-R criteria):** 3.3% overall (girls 3.7%, **boys 2.7%**), rising with age (2.8% → 3.1% → 4.2% across 17-, 18- and 19-year-olds). [MODERATE] — [Sivertsen et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-13-1163)
- **Ranges reported:**
  - 1–16% — [Narala et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/mop.0000000000001322) and [Gradisar & Crowley, Curr Opin Psychiatry 2013](https://doi.org/10.1097/yco.0b013e328365a1d4)
  - 0.4–16% — [Pediatric circadian disorders review citing ICSD-3-TR (PMC10710539)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10710539/)
  - 3.3–4.6% in adolescents/young adults. UpToDate notes that inconsistent criteria and using "evening chronotype" as a proxy complicate comparisons, and that differing school start times may explain some regional variability — [UpToDate: DSWPD](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/delayed-sleep-wake-phase-disorder)
  - **Conflict:** estimates depend on definitions.
- **Diagnostic features (ICSD-3-TR, 2023):** [EXPERT/diagnostic criteria] — [PMC10710539](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10710539/)
  - symptoms present ≥3 months;
  - sleep logs, with actigraphy whenever possible, for ≥7 days (preferably 14) including weekends/holidays;
  - a significant delay of the main sleep period relative to required times, with functional consequences;
  - logs typically show sleep onset and offset >2 h later than socially acceptable times;
  - **when allowed to sleep ad libitum, sleep quality and duration improve, which distinguishes DSWPD from chronic insomnia or hypersomnolence disorders**;
  - polysomnography during the delayed sleep window is usually normal;
  - not better explained by another sleep, medical or mental disorder or by substances.
- **Thresholds for teens:** in adults, DSPD is suspected with a 3–6 h shift (sleep onset ~2–6 am). In children and adolescents, **delays of 2–3 h are considered consistent with a circadian rhythm disorder**, especially when inflexible school start times cut sleep duration. Much of the daytime harm comes through accumulated short sleep. Adolescent boys are more evening-oriented than girls. — [JAACAP review 2021](https://www.jaacap.org/article/S0890-8567(21)00059-9/fulltext)
- **"Night owl" vs disorder:** "Unlike unaffected 'night owls,' patients with DSWPD cannot conform to a sleep schedule that is compatible with personal, professional, or academic obligations." — [UpToDate](https://www.uptodate.com/contents/delayed-sleep-wake-phase-disorder)
- **Overlap with insomnia:**
  - "over half of adolescents with DSWPD also meet criteria for insomnia" — [PMC10710539](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10710539/)
  - In Norway, 53.8% of boys and 57.1% of girls with DSPS also met insomnia criteria — [Sivertsen 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-13-1163)
  - 88.4% of adolescents with survey-defined insomnia had circadian-delay symptoms. The "circadian-delay insomnia" group had more eveningness, shorter school-night and longer free-day sleep, more social jetlag, and more napping and daytime sleepiness. — [Arnesen et al. 2024/25](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07420528.2024.2444677)
- **Consequences:**
  - DSPS was linked to school non-attendance after adjustment for insomnia and depression: days absent, adjusted OR **3.22 for boys** (1.87 for girls). The DSPS group also had more depressive symptoms. — [Sivertsen 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/1471-2458-13-1163)
  - Clinical features include significant daytime sleepiness, anxiety and depressive symptoms, and potential school dropout — [Gradisar & Crowley 2013](https://doi.org/10.1097/yco.0b013e328365a1d4)
- **Mechanisms and associations:**
  - reduced build-up of homeostatic sleep pressure, a lengthened and delayed circadian rhythm, insensitivity to morning light, and heightened pre-sleep cognitive activity — [Gradisar & Crowley 2013](https://doi.org/10.1097/yco.0b013e328365a1d4)
  - associated with ADHD, autism, substance use, anxiety and depression — [Narala 2023](https://doi.org/10.1097/mop.0000000000001322)
  - youth with depression are about twice as likely to have delayed sleep onset; delayed sleep phase occurs in 62% of youth with bipolar and 30% with unipolar depression, versus 10% of controls — [JAACAP 2021](https://www.jaacap.org/article/S0890-8567(21)00059-9/fulltext)
- **Measuring phase:**
  - Sleep diaries predict melatonin onset (DLMO) within about 1 h in healthy people, but **not reliably in insomnia or circadian disorders**. If diaries are used to estimate phase, use unrestricted days (weekends/holidays) plus actigraphy. DLMO is highly sensitive and specific for DSWPD. — [Wu, Sleep Biol Rhythms 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979143/)
  - Mid-sleep on free days (from the MCTQ) correlates with DLMO — [PMC10710539](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10710539/)

### Inferences
- **For this teen:** if he can't fall asleep before roughly midnight–1 am on school nights but falls asleep easily later and wakes refreshed after long weekend sleep, the picture fits circadian delay (normal-to-DSWPD spectrum) plus insufficient sleep. If he lies awake or wakes repeatedly even on free days, or stays unrefreshed after long sleep, insomnia disorder or another cause becomes more likely and both may coexist.
- **Why the distinction matters (for the report):**
  - a phase delay will not resolve by "just going to bed earlier";
  - mislabelling DSWPD as insomnia (or as laziness) can lead to the wrong approach;
  - DSWPD carries a measurable risk of school absence;
  - co-existing insomnia, depression or ADHD each need attention.

  Management details belong to other researchers' sections.
- **Practical boundary between "night owl" and disorder:** the delay persists for ≥3 months, he cannot shift it despite real effort, and it causes impairment (late arrival or absence, dozing in class, falling grades).

### Gaps
- No validated, self-administered DSWPD screening questionnaire with an adolescent cutoff was found.
- Access to DLMO (salivary melatonin) testing outside specialist centres was not researched.
- No Russian prevalence data were found.

## 3. Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) in adolescents: prevalence, risk factors, symptoms, screening tools, snore-recording apps

### Takeaway
OSA is much less common than insufficient sleep. Pediatric prevalence is about 1–6%, and in one US cohort, moderate OSA (AHI ≥5) newly appeared in ~10% during the transition to adolescence. In adolescents it is more common in males and with obesity (an adult-like phenotype), plus enlarged tonsils. Snoring on ≥3 nights/week together with pauses, gasps, laboured breathing, mouth breathing, morning headaches, bedwetting or daytime sleepiness should prompt evaluation. Questionnaires (the parent-completed PSQ) and phone apps are screening aids at best: diagnosis requires a sleep study, and app validation exists only in adults.

### Cited Findings
**Guideline (AAP 2012; 350 articles reviewed)**
- Prevalence across level I–II studies: 1.2–5.7%. Symptoms: habitual snoring (often with pauses, snorts or gasps), disturbed sleep, daytime neurobehavioural problems. Risk factors: adenotonsillar hypertrophy, obesity, craniofacial anomalies, neuromuscular disorders. — [AAP, Pediatrics 2012](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-pdf/130/3/576/1088485/peds_2012-1671.pdf)
- **KAS 1 (Grade B):** clinicians should ask about snoring at routine visits. "Almost all children with OSAS snore, although caregivers frequently do not volunteer this information." "Occasional snoring … is less of a concern than snoring that occurs at least 3 times a week and is associated with any of the symptoms or signs." — [AAP 2012](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-pdf/130/3/576/1088485/peds_2012-1671.pdf)
- **KAS 2A:** if a child or adolescent snores regularly and has symptoms or signs, obtain polysomnography (**Evidence A**) or refer to a sleep specialist or otolaryngologist (Evidence D, Option).
- **KAS 2B (Grade C, Option):** if PSG is unavailable, alternatives include nocturnal video recording, nocturnal oximetry, daytime nap PSG or ambulatory PSG.
- History and examination have poor sensitivity and specificity, and **tonsil size cannot predict OSA** in an individual. — [AAP 2012](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/130/3/576/30284/Diagnosis-and-Management-of-Childhood-Obstructive)
- AAP guidelines "automatically expire 5 years after publication unless reaffirmed"; reaffirmation status was not verified here — [AAP 2012 PDF](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-pdf/130/3/576/1088485/peds_2012-1671.pdf)

**Adolescent epidemiology and risk factors**
- **Penn State Child Cohort** (population sample; 421 re-examined in adolescence at mean age 16.9): 100% of childhood AHI ≥5 had remitted. The **incidence of AHI ≥5 by adolescence was 10.6%**. Primary snoring prevalence in adolescence was 25.3%. Adolescent risk factors were **male sex, older age, obesity** and visceral fat. [MODERATE] — [Bixler et al., Eur Respir J 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7085278/)
- In the same cohort, childhood obesity predicted persistence of sleep-disordered breathing (OR 3.75) and weight loss predicted remission (OR 1.67) — [Frye et al., Pediatr Obes 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijpo.12461)
- **Korean review:**
  - OSA has a second prevalence peak in adolescence linked to weight gain, and adolescent boys are more affected
  - In 15–18-year-olds, snoring was 11.2% (boys 12.4% vs girls 8.5%) and OSA 0.9%
  - Obesity OR 4.5 across ages 2–18
  - **"Excessive daytime sleepiness is a typical symptom in adolescents with OSAS"**, whereas hyperactivity predominates in younger children
  - Morning headaches and enuresis occur; allergic-rhinitis nasal oedema may exacerbate OSA
  - [Pediatric OSA review (PMC3004499)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3004499/)
- **Scoping review/meta-analysis of PSG-diagnosed studies** (9 studies; n=3,482): male sex RR 1.15, obesity RR 1.42, tonsillar hypertrophy RR 1.61. Mouth breathing (RR 1.63), allergic rhinitis (RR 0.43) and smoke exposure (RR 1.34) were not significant. Listed symptoms: snoring, pauses, enuresis, nightmares, morning headaches, daytime sleepiness. *Note: accessed as a PDF copy on a third-party site; journal not verified.* — [Saporiti et al. 2025](https://waltersport.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Obstructive-Sleep-Apnoea-Associated-Factors-in-Children-and-Adolescents-Diagnosed-by-Polysomnography-Saporiti-et-al.-2025.pdf)
- **Phenotype:** in adolescents, obesity-associated "type 2" OSA predominates, though pharyngeal lymphoid tissue volume still matters (MRI study of 12–16-year-olds). Allergic rhinitis is "rarely responsible" on its own but can aggravate OSA. — [Amaddeo et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1051/odfen/2018045)
- **2025 review:**
  - Reported symptoms: snoring, mouth breathing, observed apnoeas, frequent awakenings, secondary enuresis.
  - Signs: allergic shiners, swollen nasal mucosa, high-arched palate, adenoidal facies, enlarged tonsils, micrognathia or midface hypoplasia.
  - Each BMI unit above the 50th percentile adds ~12% OSA risk.
  - [Jadhav et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11863174/)
- **Conflicting evidence:** allergic rhinitis is cited as an aggravating factor in reviews ([PMC3004499](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3004499/); [Amaddeo 2018](https://doi.org/10.1051/odfen/2018045)) but was not a significant risk factor in the PSG meta-analysis ([Saporiti 2025](https://waltersport.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Obstructive-Sleep-Apnoea-Associated-Factors-in-Children-and-Adolescents-Diagnosed-by-Polysomnography-Saporiti-et-al.-2025.pdf)).

**Pediatric Sleep Questionnaire, sleep-related breathing disorder scale (PSQ-SRBD; parent-completed)**
- **Format:** 22 yes/no items covering snoring frequency and loudness, observed apnoeas, breathing difficulty, sleepiness, inattention/hyperactivity and other features. Score = proportion of "yes" answers; **≥0.33 = positive**. — [Chervin et al., Arch Otolaryngol 2007](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaotolaryngology/fullarticle/484678)
- **Original validation** (ages 2–18; 54 children with SRBD vs 108 clinic children): sensitivity 0.81–0.85, specificity 0.87. Performance did not vary with age. — [Chervin et al., Sleep Med 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10733617/)
- **Against PSG** (n=105): sensitivity 78%, specificity 72%. — [Chervin 2007](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaotolaryngology/fullarticle/484678)
- **BTS 2023 guideline meta-analysis** (10 datasets; 824 subjects): pooled sensitivity 0.78, specificity 0.46. For AHI ≥5: sensitivity 0.84, specificity 0.37. Evidence rated low/very low. BTS: questionnaires "should not be considered for diagnosing … if mild SDB is suspected". [WEAK] — [BTS guideline online appendix](https://www.brit-thoracic.org.uk/document-library/guidelines/paediatric-sleep-disordered-breathing/online-appendix-1-guideline-paediatric-sleep-disorders-questionnaires/)
- **Severely obese adolescents** (Teen-LABS; n=45; mean age 16.7): sensitivity 86% but **specificity 38%** for obstructive AHI ≥5. — [Ishman et al., Obesity 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/oby.21623)
- **Systematic review of 15 scoring tools:** none accurate enough to diagnose. PSQ sensitivity 71–84%, specificity as low as 13%, AUC 0.57–0.69. — [Patel et al., Laryngoscope 2019](https://doi.org/10.1002/lary.28146)
- **Dutch validation 2025** (34 PSG-confirmed vs 110 controls): OSA sensitivity 0.92, specificity 0.74. — [Becking et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41313503/)
- **Conflict:** case-control designs give much better accuracy than clinical cohorts, which suggests spectrum bias.

**Snore-recording and phone apps**
- **Pediatric:** BTS found "not enough evidence to make a consideration on sleep video recording or sleep audio recording for diagnosing sleep disordered breathing in children" — [BTS appendix](https://www.brit-thoracic.org.uk/document-library/guidelines/paediatric-sleep-disordered-breathing/online-appendix-1-guideline-paediatric-sleep-disorders-questionnaires/). AAP lists nocturnal video only as an alternative test when PSG is unavailable (Grade C) — [AAP 2012](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/130/3/576/30284/Diagnosis-and-Management-of-Childhood-Obstructive)
- **[ADULT EVIDENCE] Meta-analysis** (11 studies, 1,644 participants): for AHI ≥15, smartphone methods showed pooled sensitivity 0.91 and specificity 0.88, but wide limits of agreement versus PSG. Methods using motion or oximetry outperformed sound alone. — [Kim et al., PLoS One 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9119483/)
- **[ADULT EVIDENCE] Popular apps** (SnoreLab, Anti Snore Solution, Sleep Cycle; n=60 with simultaneous PSG): they tracked snoring and recording time, but correlated only moderately with log AHI (|r| 0.34–0.49). The authors call for larger studies. — [Shiao et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11127649/)
- **[ADULT EVIDENCE] Dedicated screening apps:**
  - Snorefox M (n=150, non-symptomatic adults): sensitivity 0.91, specificity 0.83, PPV 0.63. The authors note that plain snore-recording software correlates with snoring but **not** with OSA severity. — [ESOSA study (PMC11355704)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11355704/)
  - A deep-learning home model (n=101): sensitivity ~91–93%, specificity 84–94%. — [Han et al., JAMA Otolaryngol 2023](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaotolaryngology/fullarticle/2812069)

### Inferences
- OSA is unlikely in a normal-weight 16-year-old boy who doesn't snore. It moves up the list with habitual loud snoring (≥3 nights/week), witnessed pauses or gasps, overweight/obesity, large tonsils, chronic nasal blockage or mouth breathing, morning headaches or new bedwetting.
- **Parents are the key observers:** a teen cannot report his own snoring or pauses. A short phone audio or video of a typical night can help the doctor, but cannot rule OSA in or out. A "normal" app or questionnaire result should not override clear symptoms.

### Gaps
- No validation of consumer snore apps in adolescents was found.
- No adolescent-specific PSQ cutoff exists.
- AAP reaffirmation or update status after 2012 was not verified.
- No Russian pediatric OSA guideline was reviewed.

## 4. Narcolepsy: age of onset, key symptoms, diagnostic delay

### Takeaway
Narcolepsy is rare (~20–60 per 100,000) but typically starts in the teens, with a peak around 14–16 years. The hallmark is daily irrepressible sleepiness or sleep attacks even after adequate sleep. It is often accompanied by cataplexy (brief emotion-triggered muscle weakness, e.g. with laughter), sleep paralysis, hallucinations at sleep onset or on waking, and fragmented night sleep. Diagnosis is commonly delayed by years (mean ~10–12 y in registries), and delays are longer when symptoms start in childhood. These features justify a specialist sleep evaluation rather than waiting.

### Cited Findings
- **Onset and prevalence:**
  - Onset usually between 10 and 30 years, peaking at 14–15. Global prevalence 20–50 per 100,000. Cataplexy in ~70% of affected children: brief weakness triggered by emotion, e.g. laughter. In children it can show as facial or eyelid weakness or tongue protrusion, and may be mislabelled as clumsiness, seizures or attention-seeking. [EXPERT review] — [Chung et al., Children 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9320719/)
  - In the European Narcolepsy Network (580 NT1 patients), **42.8% had sleepiness onset between ages 10 and 20** — [EU-NN analysis 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9166906/)
- **Diagnostic delay (registries):**
  - Nexus registry (US; n=1,024; 85% female): median onset 16 years; **mean onset-to-diagnosis 11.8 y**. With pediatric onset, the mean time to first consultation was 10.7 y vs 4.6 y for adult onset, and consultation to diagnosis took 4.5 y vs 2.2 y. 59% reported ≥1 misdiagnosis; 29% saw ≥5 physicians. [MODERATE; registry, self-report] — [Nexus Narcolepsy Registry, Sleep Med 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1389945721003439)
  - Symptoms prompting first consultation: sleepiness 91.8%, trouble concentrating 58.6%, poor school/work performance 40%, vivid dreams/hallucinations 34.6%, cataplexy-like episodes (knee buckling, jaw sagging, head bobbing) 27.7%.
  - EU-NN (NT1, 1990–2018): mean delay 9.7 y, median 5.3 y, with **no improvement over three decades**. Predictors of longer delay: younger age at onset (children and adolescents waited longer than adults), younger age at cataplexy onset, a longer gap between sleepiness and cataplexy onset, lower cataplexy frequency, and female sex. The authors note that childhood cataplexy is often unrecognised or misdiagnosed (e.g. as epilepsy or attention problems). — [EU-NN 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9166906/)
- **Conflicting pediatric-centre data:**
  - Scottish tertiary series (n=42): 51.3% diagnosed within 12 months of onset; range 3 months to 11 years — [Levy et al., J Child Neurol 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0883073819836548)
  - Systematic review: about 3 y in children vs 10–15 y in adults — [Nazir et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.14302/issn.2574-4518.jsdr-18-2071)
  - Likely explanation: pediatric clinic series capture children who were diagnosed, while adult registries capture people diagnosed late.
- **Pediatric presentation** (de novo cataplexy patients: 46 children vs 46 adults): children had more obesity (54% vs 17%), night eating, parasomnias, sleep talking and **"sleep drunkenness" (69% vs 24%)**, and higher ADHD scores, but less sleep paralysis (20% vs 55%). Sleepiness severity and cataplexy frequency were similar. — [Clinical comparison study (PMC7539846)](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7539846/)
- **Diagnostic criteria (ICSD-3-TR, 2023)** [EXPERT/diagnostic criteria]:
  - **NT1:** daily irrepressible need to sleep or daytime lapses into sleep, plus one of:
    - cataplexy and an MSLT mean sleep latency ≤8 min with ≥2 sleep-onset REM periods;
    - cataplexy and a SOREMP within 15 min on the nocturnal PSG (new in the TR);
    - CSF hypocretin-1 ≤110 pg/mL.

    — [AASM ICSD-3-TR supplemental material](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/ICSD-3-Text-Revision-Supplemental-Material.pdf)
  - **NT2:** daily sleepiness for ≥3 months; the same MSLT findings without cataplexy.
  - PSG must precede the MSLT, and **sleep logs, with actigraphy when possible, are required before testing** to exclude insufficient sleep and circadian problems. The 3-month duration requirement was dropped for NT1 when cataplexy or hypocretin deficiency is established. — [Barateau et al., Rev Neurol 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neurol.2023.08.001)
  - A nocturnal SOREMP is highly specific but only ~50–60% sensitive. Idiopathic hypersomnia (a differential) can also be diagnosed with total sleep time ≥660 min on 24-h recording. — [Narcolepsy & REM sleep review (PMC11911061)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11911061/)
- **Who observes what:** parents may not detect cataplexy as well as the child does, but they recognise sleepiness better. A history from the child, parents and teachers is advised. The PDSS and ESS-CHAD are useful questionnaires. — [Chung et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9320719/)

### Inferences
- **Distinguishing narcolepsy from ordinary teen sleepiness:** sleepiness persists even when a 1–2-week diary shows 8–10 h nightly; sleep attacks happen in stimulating situations (talking, eating, which correspond to ESS-CHAD items 6/8); knees, jaw or head go weak with laughter; vivid dream-like experiences or paralysis occur at sleep onset or on waking; weight gain may accompany the onset.
- Because of the long average delay, the report should advise a direct ask for a sleep-specialist referral when these features appear, rather than repeated reassurance.

### Gaps
- No Russia-specific narcolepsy epidemiology or referral-pathway data were found.
- The very low prevalence means positive-predictive value of any single symptom is low; no adolescent community data quantify this.

## 5. Restless legs syndrome (RLS) / periodic limb movements (PLMD) and iron (ferritin)

### Takeaway
RLS affects about 2% of 12–17-year-olds, about 1% with moderate-to-severe distressing symptoms. It is often familial. It presents as an urge to move the legs with unpleasant sensations at rest in the evening, relieved by movement, which delays sleep onset and causes night awakenings. PLMD is diagnosed only by PSG. Low iron stores (pediatric threshold ferritin <50 µg/L) aggravate RLS, so a doctor can check ferritin and iron studies. Testing and any treatment belong with the physician.

### Cited Findings
- **Peds REST** (UK/US internet panel; 10,523 families; NIH pediatric criteria):
  - definite RLS in **2.0% of 12–17-year-olds** (1.9% of 8–11); moderately or severely distressing symptoms ≥2×/week in **1.0%** of 12–17-year-olds;
  - no sex difference; ≥1 parent with RLS symptoms in >70% of families;
  - sleep disturbance 69.4% vs 39.6% in controls; "growing pains" history 80.6% vs 63.2%; 49.5% endorsed a negative effect on mood.

  [MODERATE] — [Picchietti et al., Pediatrics 2007](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2006-2767)
- **Caveat:** prevalence "may have been overestimated due to selection bias or lack of in-person validation" — [Dosman et al., Paediatr Child Health 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3381661/)
- **IRLSSG 2013 pediatric criteria:**
  - prevalence 2–4% of school-aged children and adolescents; 25–50% have moderate-severe symptoms; sleep disturbance in >85% of pediatric patients;
  - PLMS >5/h in 63–74% of children with RLS;
  - parental RLS in 71–80% (Peds REST);
  - 13–35% of people with ADHD meet RLS criteria;
  - relative iron deficiency is an aggravating factor, but ferritin <50 µg/L is common in the general pediatric population, so it is not a differentiating diagnostic feature.

  [EXPERT consensus] — [IRLSSG pediatric RLS criteria 2013](https://www.irlssg.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Ped-RLS-dx-crit-2013.pdf)
- **Clinical picture:**
  - RLS causes delayed sleep onset (>30 min) and night wakings.
  - PLMD diagnosis requires PSG (PLM index ≥5/h) plus clinical sleep disturbance not explained by sleep-disordered breathing or medication.
  - Below-normal ferritin was found in 33% of children with RLS.
  - Differentials include muscle overuse, Osgood–Schlatter disease, OSA and seizures.
  - [Dosman et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3381661/)
- Iron deficiency is implicated in both RLS and PLMS. Evidence points to ferritin <50 µg/L as the relevant pediatric threshold. — [Felt & Munzer 2017, review](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0037-1605342)
- **AASM 2024 guideline** (adult and pediatric) good-practice statement: in all patients with clinically significant RLS, "clinicians should regularly test serum iron studies including ferritin and transferrin saturation", ideally in the morning. Exacerbating factors to address first: alcohol, caffeine, antihistaminergic, serotonergic and antidopaminergic medications, and untreated OSA. Adult and pediatric iron thresholds differ (adult ≤75 ng/mL, children <50 ng/mL). [EXPERT; adult thresholds = ADULT EVIDENCE] — [Winkelman et al., J Clin Sleep Med 2025 (online 2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701286/)

### Inferences
- **Relevant questions for this teen (who has trouble falling asleep and night awakenings):**
  - Do his legs feel uncomfortable, or does he feel an urge to move them, when lying still in the evening?
  - Does walking relieve it?
  - Does a parent have similar symptoms?
  - Has anyone noticed repetitive leg kicking during sleep?

  If yes, he should raise it with the doctor, who can order iron studies. The report should not suggest self-supplementation (scope rule).
- Common sedating antihistamines (e.g. for allergies) can worsen RLS. This is a medication review question for the doctor, not a self-management step.

### Gaps
- Iron-deficiency prevalence specifically in 15–17-year-old males was not retrieved.
- "Restless sleep disorder", a newer pediatric entity, was not researched.

## 6. Mental health: insomnia ↔ depression/anxiety, suicide warning signs, ADHD and substance use

### Takeaway
Poor sleep and depression or anxiety are linked in both directions in adolescents. In meta-analysis, sleep predicts later internalising symptoms at about r = −0.20, and the reverse is r = −0.21. In mostly adult cohorts, insomnia roughly doubles to triples the odds of later depression or anxiety. Insomnia is the sleep complaint most strongly tied to suicidal ideation (prospective OR ≈ 1.8). "Sleeping too little or too much" is an official suicide warning sign, and any suicidal thinking needs immediate help. ADHD (sleep problems in up to ~70%) and substance use (alcohol, nicotine, caffeine, cannabis) are frequent, partly bidirectional contributors.

### Cited Findings
**Sleep ↔ depression/anxiety**
- **Adolescent meta-analysis** (63 longitudinal studies reviewed; 42 meta-analysed; search to Jan 2023):
  - longer sleep, better quality and fewer insomnia symptoms predicted fewer internalising symptoms later (r = −0.20), and internalising symptoms predicted worse later sleep (r = −0.21);
  - externalising symptoms: r = −0.15 and −0.17;
  - heterogeneity was high, and associations were stronger with subjective sleep measures.

  [MODERATE] — [Bacaro et al., Int J Clin Health Psychol 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijchp.2023.100424)
- **[ADULT/MIXED EVIDENCE] Meta-analysis** (13 general-population cohorts; ≥12-month follow-up): baseline insomnia predicted onset of depression (OR 2.83), anxiety (OR 3.23), alcohol abuse (OR 1.35) and psychosis (OR 1.28). — [Hertenstein et al., Sleep Med Rev 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30537570/)
- **Conflicting finding:** in a Quebec cohort (1,689 children; 1,113 adolescents), disturbed sleep and depression were bidirectional between ages 10 and 12, but **cross-lagged effects were non-significant after age 13**. — [Marino et al., JAMA Netw Open 2022](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2795493)
- Older systematic review (9 studies, mixed ages): insomnia was bidirectionally related to anxiety and depression, and anxiety predicted excessive daytime sleepiness. — [Alvaro et al., Sleep 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3669059/)

**Sleep and suicidality**
- **Adolescent meta-analysis** (37,536 cross-sectional; 9,295 prospective):
  - sleep disturbance was associated with suicidal ideation (OR 2.35), plans (OR 1.58) and attempts (OR 1.92);
  - prospectively it predicted ideation (OR 1.79, 95% CI 1.36–2.36) but not attempts (OR 1.98, CI 0.62–6.29);
  - **insomnia complaints carried higher risk than other sleep complaints**, and depression did not moderate the association.

  [MODERATE] — [Liu et al., Sleep 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30843072/)

**Warning signs and immediate help**
- **SAMHSA:**
  - youth warning signs include hopelessness, severe emotional pain, more physical complaints (fatigue, headaches, stomach aches), social withdrawal, **"changes in sleep (increased or decreased)"**, out-of-character anger and agitation;
  - the general list includes "sleeping too little or too much";
  - "Intervene immediately if you notice these signs."

  — [SAMHSA Warning Signs of Suicide (updated 2026)](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/suicidal-behavior/warning-signs)
- **Johns Hopkins:**
  - any young person expressing suicidal thoughts "should not be left alone and should be assessed right away";
  - call the provider right away if the child "can't sleep or eat for 3 days in a row", hears voices or sees things others don't, or feels out of control;
  - in the US, call or text 988.

  — [Johns Hopkins Medicine: Teen Suicide](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/conditions-and-diseases/teen-suicide)
- **Mayo Clinic:** warning signs include changed eating or sleeping patterns. If the teen is in danger now, call emergency services or a crisis line. — [Mayo Clinic 2026](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/tween-and-teen-health/in-depth/teen-suicide/art-20044308)
- **Harvard Mindscape:** seek help when feelings or behaviours (including "sleeping much more or less than usual") interfere with school, family or friends, or "last for a few weeks or more". Get immediate help for suicidal thoughts. — [Harvard Health Mindscape](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mindscape/for-young-people/getting-help/reasons-someone-might-need-help)
- **Russia:** the All-Russian children's helpline **8-800-2000-122** (short number **124**) is free, anonymous and 24/7, for children, teens and parents. — [Office of the Presidential Commissioner for Children's Rights, 2025](https://deti.gov.ru/Press-Centr/region-news/20277); [2026](https://deti.gov.ru/Press-Centr/region-news/22348)

**ADHD**
- Sleep problems occur in 25–55% of children with ADHD. A chart review found sleep problems in 74% of adolescents with ADHD (mainly initial insomnia, night waking, non-restorative sleep). ADHD symptoms raise the odds of insomnia, delayed sleep phase, sleep-disordered breathing and RLS. The effect of stimulant medication on adolescent sleep was unclear (mixed findings). — [Lunsford-Avery et al., systematic review 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5401800/)
- **2023 meta-analysis:** adolescents with ADHD reported more daytime sleepiness and sleep disturbance than peers, with no differences on objective measures. Up to 72% of adolescents with ADHD have sleep problems. — [Marten et al., JCPP Advances 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcv2.12151)
- Evening preference predicted sleep problems and daytime sleepiness in 80 adolescents with ADHD aged 13–17, independent of covariates and sleep duration — [Becker et al. (PMC6944746)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6944746/)
- 73–78% of patients with ADHD report difficulty falling asleep or late sleep onset — [JAACAP 2021](https://www.jaacap.org/article/S0890-8567(21)00059-9/fulltext)
- OSA, RLS and delayed sleep phase can "mimic" ADHD symptoms. — [Owens, J Can Acad Child Adolesc Psychiatry 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2687494/)

**Substance use**
- See meta-analysis ORs in Q1 (alcohol 1.77, smoking 1.66, coffee 1.58). Alcohol and smoking were also linked to insomnia (OR 1.82, 1.75) and to sleep-related breathing disorders (OR 2.29, 2.30). — [PMC12438825](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12438825/)
- **YRBS 2017** (n=14,638): alcohol (AOR 1.42) and marijuana (AOR 1.19) use were associated with <8 h sleep. The marijuana association was **stronger in males** and younger students. — [Kwon et al., J Sch Nurs 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1059840519901161)
- **Spanish 15–18-year-olds** (n=1,121):
  - 8.6% used alcohol or cannabis as a sleep aid in the past month, rising from 6.3% to 9.5% over 6 months;
  - poor sleep predicted cannabis self-medication (OR 1.9), which in turn predicted worse sleep quality 6 months later (OR 2.2) and problematic use (OR 1.9), a bidirectional link.
  - **Conflict:** the meta-analytic association for marijuana and sleep disturbance was non-significant.

  — [Sancho-Domingo et al., Addict Behav 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.addbeh.2025.108304)
- **California cohort** (n=3,265; ages 16–22; six annual waves): worsening sleep (later bedtimes, more trouble sleeping) was associated with increasing likelihood of alcohol and cannabis use. — [Longitudinal cohort (PMC8561242)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8561242/)

### Inferences
- Any teen with chronic insomnia or daytime sleepiness should be asked directly about mood, worry, hopelessness and self-harm. Sleep complaints can be the first visible sign of depression.
- Parents should know that new or marked sleep change plus withdrawal or hopelessness is a warning-sign cluster.
- Using alcohol or cannabis "to sleep" is a red flag in its own right: it predicts worse sleep and problematic use.
- Not every inattentive, sleepy teen has ADHD; sleep disorders can mimic it, and ADHD raises the risk of circadian delay, RLS and OSA.

### Gaps
- Data on adolescent e-cigarette use and sleep were not retrieved; only the title of Riehm et al. 2019 was seen.
- No 2024–2026 adolescent meta-analysis on the insomnia → depression direction specifically was found.
- Crisis numbers for countries other than the US and Russia were not checked.

## 7. Other medical contributors (anemia/iron, thyroid, post-viral fatigue, pain, asthma/allergy, medications) and Kleine–Levin syndrome

### Takeaway
Medical causes are uncommon but worth checking when sleepiness or fatigue persists despite enough sleep, or comes with other symptoms. Candidates include iron deficiency or anemia, hypothyroidism, coeliac disease, post-infectious fatigue, uncontrolled allergic rhinitis, asthma or eczema, and sedating medicines. After infectious mononucleosis, 13% of adolescents met chronic fatigue syndrome criteria at 6 months, falling to 4% at 24 months, and all persistent cases in that cohort were girls. NICE: suspect ME/CFS when debilitating fatigue, post-exertional malaise, **unrefreshing sleep** and cognitive difficulties persist ≥4 weeks in a young person, and refer to a paediatrician. Kleine–Levin syndrome is a very rare (1–5 per million), mostly adolescent-male, recurrent hypersomnia with multi-day episodes.

### Cited Findings
**Post-infectious fatigue and ME/CFS**
- **Prospective cohort** (n=301; ages 12–18; monospot-positive infectious mononucleosis):
  - CFS in **13% at 6 months, 7% at 12 months and 4% at 24 months**; most recovered over time;
  - 24% were "not fully recovered" at the 6-month telephone screen;
  - all 13 cases at 24 months were female; **no males met criteria at 12 or 24 months**;
  - steroid use during acute IM did not affect risk;
  - CFS rates after IM were about 20 times the general adolescent rate (0.2%).

  [MODERATE] — [Katz et al., Pediatrics 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2756827/)
- **NICE NG206 (2021), 1.2.2:**
  - suspect ME/CFS if all Box 2 symptoms have persisted "for a minimum of 6 weeks in adults and **4 weeks in children and young people**", with significantly reduced functioning, not explained by another condition;
  - **Box 2** requires all of: debilitating fatigue worsened by activity and not relieved by rest; post-exertional malaise; **"unrefreshing sleep or sleep disturbance (or both)"**, e.g. feeling exhausted, flu-like and stiff on waking, broken or shallow sleep, altered sleep pattern or hypersomnia; and cognitive difficulties ("brain fog");
  - **1.2.7:** refer children and young people to a paediatrician;
  - diagnosis requires symptoms for 3 months;
  - the recommendations were based on pre-COVID evidence and are not assumed to apply to post-COVID syndrome.

  [EXPERT guideline] — [NICE NG206 PDF](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206/resources/myalgic-encephalomyelitis-or-encephalopathychronic-fatigue-syndrome-diagnosis-and-management-pdf-66143718094021); Box 2 verbatim at [ME Association reproduction of NICE 1.2](https://meassociation.org.uk/nice-guidelines/items/suspecting-me-cfs/)
- **NICE 1.2.3 tests to exclude other diagnoses** when ME/CFS is suspected: urinalysis, full blood count, urea and electrolytes, liver function, **thyroid function**, ESR or plasma viscosity, CRP, calcium and phosphate, HbA1c, **serum ferritin**, **coeliac screening**, creatine kinase. Optional, by clinical judgement: vitamin D, B12, folate, infection serology, 9 am cortisol. — [ME Association reproduction of NICE 1.2](https://meassociation.org.uk/nice-guidelines/items/suspecting-me-cfs/)

**General medical differential and medications**
- **Fatigue mechanisms and causes:** anemia, hormone deficiencies (hypothyroidism), coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, rheumatological disease, malignancy, diabetes, Addison's disease. "Worsening school performance may be among the first signs of impaired thyroid function." — [De Nardi et al., Front Pediatr 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9755349/)
- **Systemic red flags:** fever, night sweats, weight loss, growth impairment, musculoskeletal pain, joint limitation. — [De Nardi et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9755349/)
- **Uncontrolled allergic rhinitis, asthma and atopic dermatitis** disturb sleep. **Drug-related sedation:** benzodiazepines, antiepileptics, neuroleptics, some antihypertensives (clonidine), and antihistamines — sedation is "quite common" even with second-generation cetirizine. — [De Nardi et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9755349/)
- **Somatic causes of adolescent EDS** listed in the review: asthma, eczema, cystic fibrosis, GERD, epilepsy. Recurrent hypersomnia: depression, Kleine–Levin syndrome, menstrual-associated. Iron status if RLS/PLMS is suspected; thyroid test if OSA with macroglossia. — [EDS review (PMC7384526)](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7384526/)
- **Pediatric hospital pre-referral bloods for tiredness:** full blood count, iron studies, coeliac serology, TSH, urea/electrolytes/creatinine, liver function, random glucose, B12/folate, **EBV serology**. Refer to sleep medicine if difficulties continue; to ENT if snoring or apnoeas occur with large tonsils. [EXPERT; this guideline also lists treatments, excluded here by scope] — [Perth Children's Hospital](https://www.pch.health.wa.gov.au/For-health-professionals/Referrals-to-PCH/Prereferral-guidelines/Tiredness)
- **[ADULT EVIDENCE]** In primary-care fatigue, blood tests have low yield and change management in ~5% of patients, so targeted testing is preferred over blanket panels. The same review cites a figure that 1 in 3 adolescents report fatigue ≥4 times a week. — [Ho & Zheng, Singapore Med J 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9815175/)
- **Pain** is listed among causes of adolescent insomnia, alongside chronic disease, puberty, media and caffeine use, and mental health (secondary citation of a review). — [Bibliometric analysis, Front Psychiatry 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2023.1246808/full)

**Kleine–Levin syndrome (rare)**
- **Systematic review of 186 cases** [WEAK; case series]:
  - 68% male; median onset 15 y, 81% in the second decade;
  - median 7 episodes of 10 days each, recurring every 3.5 months, over ~8 years;
  - triggers: infection 38.2%, head trauma 9%, alcohol 5.4%;
  - symptoms: hypersomnia 100%, cognitive change including derealisation 96%, eating disturbance 80%, hypersexuality 43%, depressed mood 48%.

  — [Arnulf et al., Brain 2005](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/128/12/2763/420465)
- Relapses every 1–12 months for a median of 14 years. Patients have normal sleep, cognition and mood between episodes, and imaging and labs are unremarkable. — [Arnulf et al., Lancet Neurol 2012](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/laneur/article/PIIS1474-4422(12)70187-4/abstract)
- During episodes patients sleep **15–21 h/day**. The first episode is triggered by infection in 72%. — [French review 2008 (Europe PMC)](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/18653203)
- **475 case reports:** male:female 3.49:1; median onset 15. Atypical forms include recurrent insomnia or anorexia instead of hypersomnia and hyperphagia. — [Billiard & Jaussent, Sleep Med 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2024.06.003)
- **Prevalence and criteria:** 1–5 per million; 70–90% male; mean onset 16. ICSD-3: ≥2 episodes of 2 days to 5 weeks, recurring more than once a year and at least every 18 months, normal between episodes, with cognitive dysfunction, altered perception, eating disorder or disinhibition during episodes. — [Qasrawi & BaHammam 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9793345/)

### Inferences
- **Questions for this teen with his parents:**
  - Any recent glandular fever or other infection?
  - Weight loss, fevers or night sweats?
  - Pallor or breathlessness?
  - Constant nasal blockage or allergy symptoms?
  - Asthma symptoms at night?
  - Chronic pain?
  - Any regular medicines, e.g. sedating antihistamines?
  - Does exhaustion get worse a day after exertion?
- Kleine–Levin syndrome matters only if sleepiness comes in distinct multi-day episodes with confusion or a dream-like state and returns to normal between episodes. That is a very different pattern from his daily complaint.
- For a male teen after mononucleosis, persistent post-infectious CFS was uncommon in the one prospective cohort. Prolonged fatigue still meets the NICE 4-week threshold for review.

### Gaps
- No adolescent-specific prevalence data were retrieved for hypothyroidism, anemia or iron deficiency (males) presenting as sleepiness.
- No dedicated adolescent study of chronic pain and sleep was retrieved.
- Long-COVID sleep and fatigue in adolescents was not covered (NICE NG206 explicitly excludes it).

## 8. Drowsy-driving risk in 16–17-year-olds

### Takeaway
About half of teen drivers report having driven drowsy in the past year. Short sleep (≤6–7 h) and evening chronotype raise that risk. In newly licensed young drivers, ≤6 h of sleep per night was linked to a 21% higher police-reported crash risk. Short sleepers' crashes clustered at night, and less weekend sleep was linked to run-off-road crashes. All-age data show crash risk climbing steeply below 7 h in the past 24 h, reaching ~4× at 4–5 h and ~11× at <4 h. Nodding off at the wheel is a red flag that needs prompt action.

### Cited Findings
- **DRIVE prospective cohort** (New South Wales; 20,822 newly licensed drivers aged 17–24, analysed n=19,327; ~2 y follow-up):
  - ≤6 h sleep per night vs >6 h: crash **RR 1.21** (95% CI 1.04–1.41);
  - less weekend sleep: run-off-road crashes RR 1.55;
  - crashes of short sleepers more likely between 20:00 and 06:00 (RR 1.66–1.86).

  [STRONG for young drivers] — [Martiniuk et al., JAMA Pediatr 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23689363/)
- **Virginia high-school drivers** (n=431; ages 15.5–18.7):
  - **47.6% reported drowsy driving** in the past year;
  - covariate-adjusted drowsy-driving prevalence was 13.9% higher (95% CI 3.0–24.9%) for <7 h vs ≥8 h school-night sleep, and 15.2% higher (95% CI 4.5–25.9%) for evening vs morning chronotype (MESC lower vs upper tertile).

  [MODERATE; cross-sectional] — [J Pediatr 2019](https://www.jpeds.com/article/S0022-3476(18)31408-2/abstract)
- **Italian licensed high-school students:** 40% reported sleepiness while driving; 24% had ≥1 crash (76% of them male). Crash predictors: male sex (OR 3.3), smoking (OR 3.2), **sleepiness at the wheel (OR 2.1)**, poor sleep (OR 1.9). Common countermeasures (music, opening windows) are ineffective. [WEAK–MODERATE] — [Pizza et al., J Clin Sleep Med 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2823274/)
- **US young drivers aged 14–22** (506 licensed): besides length of licensure, only driving alone while drowsy and current smoking were associated with ever having crashed. — [Accid Anal Prev 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18460353/)
- **YRBS 2007–2013** (n=50,370 high-school students): students sleeping ≤7 h were more likely to text while driving, drink and drive, ride with a drinking driver, and skip seatbelts or helmets. — [CDC MMWR 2016](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/65/wr/mm6513a1.htm)
- **School start time (ecological):** after a 1-hour later high-school start, crash rates of 17–18-year-old drivers in the county fell 16.5% while rising 7.8% in the rest of the state. Not proven causal. — [Danner & Phillips 2008](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/full/10.5664/jcsm.27345)
- **[ADULT/ALL-AGE EVIDENCE] AAA Foundation** (7,234 drivers in 4,571 investigated crashes; 06:00–23:59 only), compared with ≥7 h in the past 24 h:
  - crash rate 1.3× at 6–7 h, **1.9× at 5–6 h, 4.3× at 4–5 h, 11.5× at <4 h**;
  - sleeping 2–3 h less than usual: 3.0×;
  - the authors equate 4–5 h of sleep with roughly the risk at the 0.08 legal blood-alcohol limit.

  — [AAA Foundation 2016](https://aaafoundation.org/research/acute-sleep-deprivation-risk-motor-vehicle-crash-involvement/)
- **[ADULT/ALL-AGE EVIDENCE] Culpability analysis** (n=6,845): odds of being culpable rose from 1.3 (6 h) to **15.1 (<4 h)**. "Typically feeling drowsy upon waking" was associated with culpability (OR 2.2). — [Tefft, Sleep 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsy144)

### Inferences
- **For a 16-year-old:** if he drives, or rides a moped, e-scooter or bicycle in traffic, any episode of nodding off, drifting or "missing" stretches of road is a reason to stop driving while drowsy and to get prompt medical evaluation. This is especially true after ≤6 h of sleep or at night.
- Waking groggy every morning ("drowsy upon waking") was itself a crash correlate in all-age data, which matches the target teen's complaint.

### Gaps
- Local licensing rules were not verified, e.g. whether a 16-year-old in Russia may legally drive a car, moped or motorcycle. The report should adapt the driving section to local law (moped, e-scooter and bicycle riding may be the relevant exposure).
- No adolescent-specific dose–response data comparable to the AAA data were found.
- No Russian drowsy-driving data were found.

## 9. Validated self-report tools a teen can fill in (with cutoffs) and the 2-week sleep diary

### Takeaway
A practical self-check kit:
- **Insomnia Severity Index (ISI)** for insomnia: adolescent cutoff ≥9; adult bands 8/15/22.
- **ESS-CHAD** or **PDSS** for sleepiness: neither has a validated adolescent cutoff. ESS >10 is the adult convention; PDSS ≥15 has been proposed.
- **MESC** or **MCTQ** for chronotype and social jetlag: no universal cutoffs.
- **PSQ-SRBD**, completed by parents, for breathing: ≥0.33 = positive.
- A **2-week sleep diary** covering both school days and free days.

None of these is diagnostic. They structure the conversation with the doctor and show change over time.

### Cited Findings
**Insomnia Severity Index (ISI)**
- **Format:** 7 items scored 0–4; total 0–28; recall the "last month" in Morin's version or the past 2 weeks in some translations. Bands: **0–7 none, 8–14 subthreshold, 15–21 moderate clinical, 22–28 severe**.
- **[ADULT EVIDENCE] Adult cutoffs:**
  - ≥10 in the community: sensitivity 86.1%, specificity 87.7% — [Morin et al., Sleep 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079939/)
  - ≥11 in clinical samples — [Morin 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3079939/)
  - ≥14 in primary care: sensitivity 82.4%, specificity 82.1% — [Gagnon et al., J Am Board Fam Med 2013](https://www.jabfm.org/content/26/6/701)
- **Adolescents (Hong Kong school survey; n=1,516; ages 12–19; interview-based reference):** α 0.83; 2-week test–retest 0.79; AUC 0.85; **optimal cutoff ≥9** (sensitivity 0.87, specificity 0.75). [MODERATE] — [Chung, Kan & Yeung, Sleep Med 2011](https://research.polyu.edu.hk/en/publications/assessing-insomnia-in-adolescents-comparison-of-insomnia-severity/)
- **Other adolescent validations:**
  - German version (1,475 adolescents): acceptable psychometrics, one-factor structure, invariant across sex — [Gerber et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4888604/)
  - Swedish teens (n=205; 2025): α 0.74; Swedish youth research uses the ≥9 adolescent cutoff — [Eken et al., Sömn och Hälsa 2025](https://doi.org/10.59526/soh.13.39202)
- **Caveat:** in epidemiological use the ISI and similar scales are sensitive but not specific, and can pick up circadian delay as "insomnia" — [Arnesen et al. 2024/25](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/07420528.2024.2444677)
- **Alternative:** the Adolescent Insomnia Questionnaire (α 0.91) has a preliminary cutoff of 15 (sensitivity 0.907, specificity 0.771, AUC 0.93) against an interview. [WEAK; single development study] — [AIQ development study (PMC8205502)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8205502/)

**Epworth Sleepiness Scale for Children and Adolescents (ESS-CHAD)**
- **Format:** 8 situations rated 0–3 for chance of dozing; total 0–24; one-month recall. Changes from the adult ESS: classroom as the public place, "sitting quietly by yourself after lunch" (no alcohol), and "sitting and eating a meal" instead of being stopped in traffic.
- **Rasch validation** in 297 Australian students aged 12–18: reliable and unidimensional. However, "further studies are needed … to establish external validity and accurate cut-off points". It cannot distinguish causes of sleepiness. **Adult convention: ESS >10 = EDS.** [MODERATE reliability; cutoff = ADULT EVIDENCE] — [Janssen et al., Sleep Med 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28449902/)
- **Recent Slovenian validation** (n=3,314; mean age 15.4): two factors fitted better.
  - "Passive" sleepiness (reading, TV, vehicle, afternoon rest, after lunch) is likely tied to modifiable sleep habits.
  - **"Concerning" sleepiness — dozing while talking or eating — may indicate pathological sleepiness** that needs evaluation.
  - It uses ">10" as the conventional EDS threshold.

  — [ESS-CHAD-SI validation (PMC12955840)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12955840/)
- **1-week version in pediatric narcolepsy** (n=100; mean age 11.9): α 0.75; ICC 0.76; untreated mean ≈16. — [Wang et al. 2021/22](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34920345/)
- **Conflict:** unidimensional (Australia) vs two-factor (Slovenia) structure.

**Pediatric Daytime Sleepiness Scale (PDSS)**
- **Format:** 8 items scored 0–4; total 0–32. Developed in 450 middle-schoolers aged 11–15 (α 0.80). Higher scores were linked to low achievement, absenteeism, short sleep and frequent illness. — [Drake et al., Sleep 2003](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/26.4.455)
- **No established cutoff:** across 26 studies (n=18,458), mean scores ranged from 6.7 to 25.7 — [Meyer et al. systematic review 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5606183/)
- **Proposed cutoffs:**
  - **≥15** (Brazil; n=1,132; ages 14–19), based on sleep quality as the reference; AUC only 0.66–0.71 — [Meyer et al., Chronobiol Int 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/07420528.2017.1400980)
  - **16/17** for narcolepsy screening in Taiwan (31 narcolepsy, 28 OSA, 34 controls): sensitivity 87.1%, specificity 74.3%; poor at separating OSA — [Yang et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20132530/)
  - [WEAK]

**Chronotype: Morningness–Eveningness Scale for Children (MESC) and Munich ChronoType Questionnaire (MCTQ)**
- **MESC:** 10 items; score 10–43; lower = more evening. Spanish norms (n=5,387; ages 10–16): 10th/90th percentiles = 20/32, 25th/75th = 23/28. These cut-points are validated only in Spanish samples. Actigraphy validation (n=138; ages 12–13): evening types had later activity, temperature and light rhythms and larger weekend delays. MESC correlated with social jetlag (r = −0.32). [MODERATE validity; WEAK for universal cutoffs] — [Puig-Navarro & Díaz-Morales 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12215289/); an older Spanish sample used 18/30 — [Díaz-Morales 2008](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/SJOP/article/download/SJOP0808120201A/28797)
- **MCTQ / μMCTQ:** asks sleep timing separately for school/work days and free days. Mid-sleep on free days estimates chronotype and **correlates with DLMO**. — [PMC10710539](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10710539/)
- **Social jetlag** = the gap between mid-sleep on free days and on school days. In early adolescents measured by actigraphy, median social jetlag was ~0.9 h. — [Project Viva cohort (PMC6749538)](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6749538/)
- **[ADULT EVIDENCE]** In adults aged 18–34, MCTQ mid-sleep correlated r = 0.73 with actigraphy — [cited in Puig-Navarro 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12215289/)
- No clinical MCTQ cutoff exists.

**Parent-completed breathing screen:** PSQ-SRBD (22 items; ≥0.33 positive; ages 2–18). Accuracy and limitations are covered in Q3. — [Chervin 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10733617/); [BTS appendix](https://www.brit-thoracic.org.uk/document-library/guidelines/paediatric-sleep-disordered-breathing/online-appendix-1-guideline-paediatric-sleep-disorders-questionnaires/)

**Sleep diary (1–2 weeks)**
- ICSD-3-TR requires sleep logs, with actigraphy whenever possible, for **≥7 days including weekends/holidays, preferably 14**, for circadian disorders — [PMC10710539](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10710539/)
- Logs, with actigraphy when possible, are also required before MSLT testing for narcolepsy — [Barateau et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neurol.2023.08.001)
- **What to record:** bedtime, sleep-onset time, sleep-onset latency, night awakenings, wake time, sleep duration and quality, naps, weekend catch-up sleep, daytime sleepiness. — [JAACAP 2021](https://www.jaacap.org/article/S0890-8567(21)00059-9/fulltext)
- Use unrestricted (free) days to judge natural timing — [Wu 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979143/)

**Structured self-check questions (BEARS)**
- BEARS covers five domains: **B**edtime problems, **E**xcessive daytime sleepiness, **A**wakenings, **R**egularity/duration, **S**noring. It has age-specific trigger questions for 2–18-year-olds; for adolescents most are self-directed, while snoring is asked of parents. In a pilot (n=195; ages 2–12), it significantly increased detection of sleep problems in primary care. Not a scored, validated diagnostic instrument. [WEAK/EXPERT] — [Owens & Dalzell, Sleep Med 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15680298/); [OHSU BEARS algorithm](https://www.ohsu.edu/sites/default/files/2019-02/BEARS%20-%20Sleep%20Screening%20Algorithm%2012.30.16.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Suggested self-check sequence for the report:**
  1. Keep a 14-day diary covering at least one weekend, ideally a holiday too.
  2. Fill in the ISI. ≥9 suggests clinically relevant insomnia in teens; ≥15 is moderate/severe by adult bands.
  3. Fill in the ESS-CHAD (or PDSS). Pay special attention to any "yes" for dozing while eating or talking.
  4. Fill in the MESC or MCTQ to see chronotype and social jetlag.
  5. Ask parents to complete the PSQ, plus a few nights of listening, or a short phone recording, for snoring and pauses.
  6. Bring all of it to the doctor.
- **Simple yes/no screen derived from the cited criteria (BEARS, ICSD-3-TR, AAP, NICE, SAMHSA):**
  - Do I take >30 min to fall asleep or wake for long periods at night, ≥3 nights/week?
  - Do I sleep fine and wake refreshed when I can sleep in on free days?
  - Do I doze in class, or while eating or talking?
  - Do parents hear loud snoring ≥3 nights/week, or pauses or gasps?
  - Do my legs feel uncomfortable at rest in the evening, and does moving help?
  - Do my knees or jaw go weak when I laugh?
  - Do I see or hear dream-like things, or feel paralysed, when falling asleep or waking?
  - Have I felt low, hopeless or uninterested for a few weeks?
  - Do I use alcohol, cannabis or nicotine to sleep?
  - Have I nodded off while driving or riding?
  - Did this start after an infection, and does exertion make me much worse the next day?

### Gaps
- No validated adolescent cutoff exists for the ESS-CHAD, and only weak proposals exist for the PDSS.
- Licensing and free availability of the ISI and ESS-CHAD (both copyrighted instruments) were not verified.
- No validated Russian-language versions of these scales were checked in this search.

## 10. Red-flag list with timelines, where to go, and what a doctor or sleep specialist typically does

### Takeaway
Seek help **immediately** for suicidal thoughts or psychotic symptoms. Seek help **within days** if he cannot sleep for 3 days in a row or has nodded off while driving. Seek help **within weeks** for loud habitual snoring with pauses, cataplexy-like episodes or sleep attacks, multi-day hypersomnia episodes, daily dozing despite adequate sleep opportunity, persistent low mood, or RLS-type leg symptoms. Seek review **at 4 weeks** for post-infectious exhaustion with unrefreshing sleep (NICE). Insomnia or circadian delay meets diagnostic chronicity at ≥3 nights/week for **≥3 months**. That is a diagnostic threshold, not a reason to wait if function is clearly impaired. Evaluation should be done together with parents, who observe night-time signs. Typical work-up:
- history plus a 1–2-week diary (± actigraphy);
- targeted blood tests;
- polysomnography for suspected OSA;
- PSG plus MSLT for suspected narcolepsy.

**Why parents should be involved**
- Caregivers often don't volunteer snoring, yet almost all children with OSA snore — [AAP 2012](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article-pdf/130/3/576/1088485/peds_2012-1671.pdf)
- Parents recognise a child's sleepiness better, while the child recognises cataplexy better — [Chung 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9320719/)
- NICE notes young people "may need their parents or carers … to help them" describe symptoms — [NICE NG206](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng206/resources/myalgic-encephalomyelitis-or-encephalopathychronic-fatigue-syndrome-diagnosis-and-management-pdf-66143718094021)
- The PSQ is parent-completed — [Chervin 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10733617/)

**Where to go**
- **First stop: GP or pediatrician.** For suspected OSA the next step is PSG, or referral to a sleep specialist or otolaryngologist — [AAP 2012](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/130/3/576/30284/Diagnosis-and-Management-of-Childhood-Obstructive)
- **Persistent tiredness:** after first-line bloods, refer to respiratory and sleep medicine; refer to ENT if OSA symptoms occur with large tonsils — [Perth Children's Hospital](https://www.pch.health.wa.gov.au/For-health-professionals/Referrals-to-PCH/Prereferral-guidelines/Tiredness)
- **Suspected ME/CFS:** paediatrician — [NICE NG206](https://meassociation.org.uk/nice-guidelines/items/suspecting-me-cfs/)
- **Narcolepsy:** a sleep specialist (64%) or neurologist (27%) usually made the diagnosis. Patients who saw a GP before diagnosis had a longer interval from first consultation to diagnosis (4.7 vs 2.8 y). Intervals were shorter when the first consultation was with a sleep specialist (1.7 y) or neurologist (2.3 y). — [Nexus 2021](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1389945721003439)
- **Suicidal thoughts:** child-focused mental-health professional — [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/tween-and-teen-health/in-depth/teen-suicide/art-20044308)

**What the doctor or specialist typically does (brief)**
1. **History:**
   - BEARS-type sleep history; review of medications and substances; mood screen — [Owens & Dalzell 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15680298/); [De Nardi 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9755349/)
   - Questionnaires — [EDS review](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7384526/)
2. **Sleep diary for 1–2 weeks ± actigraphy** (required by ICSD-3-TR for circadian disorders and before MSLT). Actigraphy is useful for insufficient sleep, insomnia and circadian disorders but **cannot detect PLMs or apnoeas** — [PMC10710539](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10710539/); [EDS review](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7384526/)
3. **Examination:** BMI, tonsils, nose and palate, facial profile — [PMC3004499](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3004499/); [Jadhav 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11863174/)
4. **Overnight polysomnography** (gold standard for OSA, with pediatric scoring rules); alternatives only if PSG is unavailable — [AAP 2012](https://publications.aap.org/pediatrics/article/130/3/576/30284/Diagnosis-and-Management-of-Childhood-Obstructive)
5. **PSG followed by a daytime MSLT** for suspected narcolepsy or hypersomnia: mean sleep latency ≤8 min plus ≥2 SOREMPs. Sometimes CSF hypocretin or HLA typing is added — [ICSD-3-TR](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/ICSD-3-Text-Revision-Supplemental-Material.pdf); [Chung 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9320719/)
6. **Circadian phase assessment** in specialised settings: salivary DLMO — [Wu 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9979143/); [JAACAP 2021](https://www.jaacap.org/article/S0890-8567(21)00059-9/fulltext)
7. **Targeted blood tests:** full blood count, ferritin and iron studies, TSH, coeliac serology, kidney and liver function, glucose/HbA1c, B12/folate, and EBV serology if post-infectious — [Perth Children's Hospital](https://www.pch.health.wa.gov.au/For-health-professionals/Referrals-to-PCH/Prereferral-guidelines/Tiredness); [NICE 1.2.3](https://meassociation.org.uk/nice-guidelines/items/suspecting-me-cfs/); [AASM RLS 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11701286/)
   - [ADULT EVIDENCE] blanket panels have low yield — [Ho & Zheng 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9815175/)

### Inferences
- **For this teen,** the most likely sequence is:
  1. a 2-week diary plus ISI and ESS-CHAD;
  2. a GP or pediatric visit with a parent, covering the BEARS domains, snoring, mood, substances and legs;
  3. targeted bloods only if indicated;
  4. a sleep-clinic referral if the diary shows adequate sleep opportunity yet persistent sleepiness, or if any narcolepsy, OSA or RLS red flag is present.
- The ≥3-month thresholds are diagnostic definitions, not advice to wait. No source found sets a mandatory waiting period, so marked impairment (falling asleep in class daily, school absence, drowsy driving) justifies earlier evaluation.

### Gaps
- No guideline was found that sets explicit adolescent-specific waiting times for insomnia or EDS evaluation. The timelines above combine diagnostic criteria (DSM-5/ICSD-3-TR), NICE NG206's 4-week threshold, AAP OSA screening, and health-system warning-sign guidance.
- Russian national pathways (e.g. pediatric sleep-centre referral, the emergency number 112) were not verified in this search, except for the children's helpline.
- An updated AAP pediatric OSA guideline after 2012 was not found and its reaffirmation status was not verified. The BTS 2023 pediatric SDB guideline appendix was reviewed only for questionnaires and audio/video recording.
