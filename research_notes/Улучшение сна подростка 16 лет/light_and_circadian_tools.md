# Light Exposure, Circadian Timing and Light-Related Tools for a 16-Year-Old

> **Reading guide for the report writer.**
>
> **Target person:** a 16-year-old male student with a fixed weekday wake time of about 06:30–07:30. His problems are slow sleep onset, night awakenings, and waking unrefreshed with daytime sleepiness.
>
> **Evidence labels:**
> - **[ADOLESCENT]**: data from people aged about 13–19.
> - **[YOUNG ADULT]**: ages 18–24.
> - **[ADULT]** / **[OLDER ADULT]**: adult-only evidence. Applying it to a 16-year-old is an extrapolation.
> - **[CONSENSUS]**: expert consensus.
> - **[MANUFACTURER SPEC]**: used for features and prices only, never for efficacy.
> - **[INDEPENDENT TEST]**: third-party light-meter tests.
>
> **Evidence grades:** strong / moderate / weak / expert opinion only.
>
> **Light metrics:**
> - *Photopic lux* is what an ordinary lux meter or phone app reads.
> - *Melanopic EDI (m-EDI)* is the circadian-weighted measure: the lux of daylight that would have the same effect on the eye's circadian (melanopsin) receptors.
> - *Melanopic DER (mDER)* = m-EDI ÷ photopic lux. It is about 0.41–0.55 for 2700–3000K LEDs and about 0.87–0.95 for 6500K LEDs (sources in Q3).
> - "At the eye" means vertical illuminance at eye level, in the direction of gaze.
>
> **Prices and scope:** prices are approximate USD figures from the cited pages. Availability varies by country and was not checked for Russia or the CIS. Supplements (including melatonin), medications and clinician-delivered therapy are out of scope and are not recommended here.

## 1. Morning light: how much, how early, how long; outdoor vs indoor; dark winter mornings

### Takeaway
Bright light soon after waking is the main light lever for shifting a teen's body clock earlier. In adolescent lab studies:
- about 2.5 h of ~6,000 lux on weekend mornings advanced melatonin onset by ~1 h;
- 90 min/day of ~6,000 lux plus gradually earlier sleep advanced it by ~2 h;
- blocking short-wavelength (blue-green) morning light delayed 13–14-year-olds by ~30 min;
- two late nights in evening room light cancelled the advance.

Outdoor daylight (about 1,000 to over 100,000 lux) dwarfs indoor light (about 300–500 lux). The practical rule is outdoor light — or a light box while it is still dark — within the first hour after waking, every day including weekends. The evidence is **moderate** for adolescent physiology and **weak/extrapolated** for everyday routines such as walking to school; no study of such routines was found.

### Cited Findings
*Phase-response curve: when light advances vs delays a teen's clock*
- [ADOLESCENT; lab phase-response curve; ages 14–17, late/post-pubertal] Design: ~5,000 lux given as four 20-min exposures per day for 3 days, on a dim (~20 lux)/dark ultradian schedule. Results relative to melatonin onset (DLMO):
  - largest delay −1.8 h at DLMO+1.9 h; large delays from DLMO−0.6 h to DLMO+4.3 h;
  - largest advance +1.5 h at DLMO+9.2 h; large advances from DLMO+7.3 h to DLMO+11.4 h;
  - crossover from delay to advance at DLMO+5.7 h;
  - a secondary advance peak of ~0.7 h at DLMO+18–20 h.
  — [Crowley & Eastman 2017, J Biol Rhythms 32:334–344](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5972384/)
- [ADOLESCENT; same study, re-expressed relative to the teens' fixed 9-h home sleep schedule]
  - Largest delays came from light centred on scheduled bedtime; large delays occurred from 2.7 h before to 2.6 h after bedtime.
  - The largest advance (~1.4 h) came from light centred ~0.9 h before scheduled wake time; large advances occurred from 2.8 h before to 1.5 h after wake.
  - The crossover was ~0.2 h before mid-sleep.
  - Afternoon light 5.6–8.1 h after waking still advanced 6 participants by more than 30 min.
  - The authors recommend timing light from average mid-sleep, and note (citing Burgess 2010) that sleep restriction can reduce light-induced shifts.
  — [Crowley & Eastman 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5972384/)

*Dose and duration in adolescents*
- [ADOLESCENT; randomized lab trial; n=37 (16 male), ages 14.7–18.0]
  - Participants: school-night sleep ≤7 h; bedtimes ≥23:00 on school nights and ≥24:00 on weekends; baseline school-day wake ~06:30; actigraphic sleep 4.8–5.5 h.
  - Weekend protocol: woken 1 h after weekend mid-sleep on Saturday and 1 h earlier on Sunday. Then one of three conditions: room light (~20 lux), 1.5 h of ~6,000 lux (3×30 min), or 2.5 h of ~6,000 lux (3×50 min).
  - DLMO advances: room light 0.6±0.4 h; 1.5 h 0.6±0.5 h; 2.5 h 1.0±0.4 h.
  - The 2.5-h group differed from room light (d=1.16) and from the 1.5-h group (d=0.96). A ≥1-h advance occurred in 54% of the 2.5-h group vs 8% (room light) and 18% (1.5 h).
  - Light fell ~7–11 h after DLMO.
  - The authors estimate sleep onset could move ≥30–36 min earlier, and advise combining light with earlier bedtimes.
  — [Misiunaite, Eastman & Crowley 2020, Front Neurosci](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2020.00099/full); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32116532/)
- [ADOLESCENT; lab; n=6]
  - All had 10-h sleep opportunities for 6 days.
  - For the next 2 nights, half went to bed 4.5 h later and slept 5.5 h (evening room light + sleep restriction); the other half kept 10-h opportunities.
  - Then everyone had gradually earlier sleep plus 90 min of ~6,000 lux after waking for 3 days.
  - The well-rested teens advanced ~2 h. The late-bedtime/evening-room-light teens did not shift, or delayed by 2–4 h.
  — [Crowley, Fournier & Eastman 2018, Chronobiol Int](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30084658/)

*Evidence that missing morning light delays teens*
- [ADOLESCENT; field study; n=11 eighth-graders aged 13–14, 9 male] Orange glasses blocking short-wavelength light (below ~525 nm), worn from waking until ~15:00 for 5 school days, delayed DLMO by ~30 min (21.15 → 21.66 h clock time; p=0.006). — [Figueiro & Rea 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3349218/)
- [ADOLESCENT; RCT sample, n=49 with delayed sleep phase] 85% of participants reported avoiding morning light. — [Gradisar et al. 2011, SLEEP](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3208844/)
- [ADOLESCENT; narrative review] Adolescents spend ~90% of their time indoors. In an adolescent field study (n=15), less morning light was associated with later sleep onset, sleep offset and rise time. — [Ricketts et al. 2022, Sleep Med Rev 64:101667](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10693907/)

*Outdoor vs indoor light levels (mostly horizontal illuminance; vertical light at the eye is lower)*
- Clear sky 50,000–100,000 lx; overcast 10,000–20,000 lx; office 300–500 lx. — [Münch & Bromundt 2012, table](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3553574/table/T1/?report=objectonly)
- Direct sunlight >100,000 lx; overcast ~1,000 lx; office ~500 lx; home living areas ~300 lx; twilight ~10 lx. — [Systematic review (PMC6814154)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6814154/)
- Overcast noon 6,000–20,000 lx; clear sky >100,000 lx; indoor lighting usually ≤500 lx. — [TRILUX](https://www.trilux.com/en/lighting-practice/indoor-lighting/general-requirements/light-and-non-visual-effects/daylight/)
- CIBSE-based reference values: sunlight 100,000 lx; full daylight 10,000; overcast day 1,000; very dark day 100; twilight 10. — [Sheffield, Science and Technology of Low Carbon Design](https://sheffield.pressbooks.pub/scienceandtechnologyoflowcarbondesign/chapter/artificial-lighting/)
- **Conflict:** "overcast" is ~1,000 lx in two sources and 6,000–20,000 lx in two others. The difference likely reflects cloud thickness, time of day and season. — [PMC6814154](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6814154/) vs [TRILUX](https://www.trilux.com/en/lighting-practice/indoor-lighting/general-requirements/light-and-non-visual-effects/daylight/) / [Münch & Bromundt](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3553574/table/T1/?report=objectonly)
- 10,000 lux is roughly the sun ~45 min after sunrise; a summer pavement café is ~120,000 lux. — [Dutch Foundation for Bipolar Disorders, light-therapy protocol](https://www.kenniscentrumbipolairestoornissen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Light-Therapy-in-Bipolar-Disorders-Protocol-Dutch-Foundation-for-Bipolar-Disorders.pdf)
- [CONSENSUS] Under 500-lux task lighting indoors, vertical melanopic EDI at the eye is typically below 200 lux. — [Brown et al. 2022, PLOS Biol](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pbio.3001571)

*Daylight availability in winter at Russian latitudes*
- At the December solstice:
  - Moscow: sunrise ~08:57–08:59, sunset ~15:57–15:58, about 7 h of daylight;
  - St Petersburg: sunrise 10:00, sunset 15:53 (5 h 53 min);
  - Arkhangelsk: under 4 h of daylight;
  - Murmansk: polar night.
  — [xras.ru](https://xras.ru/project_diary.html?post_id=3208); [Moscow Planetarium](https://planetarium-moscow.ru/blog/articles/21122025-goda-zimnee-solntsestoyanie)
- Moscow on 15 Oct 2026: sunrise 07:00, sunset 17:29. The latest sunrise of the year is around 29 Dec. — [timeanddate.com](https://www.timeanddate.com/sun/russia/moscow?month=12)

*Practical protocols tested in adolescents (details in Q6)*
- [GUIDELINE; based on one adolescent RCT]
  - Light after waking for ≥30 min (maximum 2 h), from sunlight or a ~1,000-lux lamp.
  - Start at the teen's natural wake time and move 30 min earlier per day until ~06:00.
  - Then keep a regular rise time of 06:30–07:30.
  - Always combined with behavioral treatment.
  — [AASM 2015 (Auger et al.), J Clin Sleep Med](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/full/10.5664/jcsm.5100)
- [ADOLESCENT RCT] 30–60 min of light immediately on rising, starting at natural wake time and moving 30 min earlier per day to 06:00, plus dim light for 2 h before bed. — [Richardson et al. 2018, Sleep Med](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2018.02.001)

### Inferences
- **Timing rule.** Light shifts the clock earlier when it falls after the teen's biological mid-sleep, and most strongly around his usual wake time (Crowley 2017).
  - A practical proxy for his biological timing is mid-sleep on free days (weekend nights without an alarm).
  - Example: weekend sleep of 01:30–10:30 gives a mid-sleep of 06:00. Light at 06:30–08:30 on school days then falls in the advance zone.
  - If he is very delayed (say weekend sleep of 03:00–12:00, mid-sleep 07:30), light at 06:30–07:15 may land before the crossover. It would then produce little advance or even a delay.
  - This is why clinical protocols start light at the natural wake time and move it 30 min earlier per day.
  - Mid-sleep is only a proxy: his real DLMO is unknown.
- **Dose.** The lab doses that reliably shifted teens were large: 2.5 h of ~6,000 lux, or 90 min/day for 3 days.
  - Real-world version: at least 30 min — ideally 60+ min in total within the first ~2 h after waking — of outdoor light.
  - Even overcast daylight is several-fold to tens-of-fold brighter than indoor light. Shortly after sunrise, outdoor light already approaches light-box levels.
  - Indoor room light is probably too weak. The 0.6-h advance in the room-light group of Misiunaite 2020 most likely reflects the earlier wake time itself.
- **How early.** Within the first 30–60 min after waking. This is consistent with the curve's advance peak around habitual wake time and with Mayo Clinic's within-the-first-hour advice for light boxes (Q6).
- **Daily routine.**
  - Open the curtains and switch on bright lights immediately on waking.
  - Eat breakfast at the brightest window.
  - Walk at least part of the way to school, without sunglasses. Figueiro & Rea's morning orange-glasses result suggests that filtering morning light is counterproductive; this is an extrapolation.
  - Spend school breaks outdoors.
- **Weekends.** Keep weekend wake times close to school days, and get outdoor light within the first hour after waking. Misiunaite's protocol (woken 1 h after weekend mid-sleep, then bright light) produced ~1 h advances over a single weekend. The sleep-schedule details belong to another researcher; the light side is that sleeping until noon skips the advancing window entirely.
- **Dark winter mornings.** From roughly November to February in central Russia, 06:30–08:00 is before sunrise (Moscow ~09:00 and St Petersburg ~10:00 at the solstice). The commute then gives only twilight or street-light levels (~10 lux). Substitutes:
  1. A 10,000-lux light box at breakfast for 20–30 min at its rated distance (Q6).
  2. Or light-emitting glasses (Q6).
  3. Plus as much outdoor time as possible at midday and right after school, while the sun is up. Afternoon light still advanced some teens in Crowley 2017, and it adds to the daytime light history (Q2).
  - A dawn simulator helps with waking but does not replace bright light after waking (Q7).
- **Combine with evenings.** Morning light only works if evenings are dim and bedtime moves earlier (Crowley 2018). Light and schedule changes have to be combined.

### Gaps
- No study tested an "outdoors within 30–60 min of waking" or "walk to school" protocol in adolescents. The durations above are extrapolated from lab doses and lux tables.
- The total N of the Crowley & Eastman 2017 phase-response-curve study was not extracted.
- No data were found on vertical (at-eye) illuminance during a dark winter commute at 55–60°N, or on the minimum outdoor time needed under heavy overcast.
- The teen's actual DLMO and mid-sleep are unknown; the free-day mid-sleep heuristic is an approximation.
- The conflict over overcast lux values (~1,000 vs 6,000–20,000 lx) was not resolved.
- No 2023–2026 adolescent trial of morning light was found.

## 2. Daytime light: does more daytime light reduce sensitivity to evening light and improve sleep?

### Takeaway
Probably yes. After brighter days, the same evening light suppresses melatonin less and shifts the clock less, in consistent small adult lab studies. Adolescents with more, and more regular, daytime light sleep earlier, longer and more regularly, but that evidence is observational only. Grades: **moderate** in adults, **weak** in adolescents. The consensus daytime target is ≥250 lux melanopic EDI at the eye, which in practice means time outdoors or right beside windows.

### Cited Findings
- [ADULT; within-subject; n=12]
  - Bright week: 4.3±0.4 h/day outdoors plus a light box. Dim week: goggles transmitting ~2% of light outdoors.
  - Test: 3 h of 500 lux at night. Melatonin suppression was 53% after the dim week vs 41% after the bright week (p<0.05).
  - The difference was pronounced in only 7 of 12 people.
  — [Hébert et al. 2002, J Pineal Res](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3925650/)
- [ADULT] After a prior light history of ~1 lux vs ~90 lux, a 90-lux night exposure suppressed melatonin 86.1% vs 51.3%. It delayed the clock by 1.67 h vs 1.03 h. — [Chang, Scheer & Czeisler 2011, J Physiol](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3060589/)
- [ADULT; n=8] After prior exposure of ~0.5 vs ~200 lux, 6.5 h at 200 lux suppressed melatonin 85.7% vs 71.2%. — [Smith et al. 2004, JCEM](https://doi.org/10.1210/jc.2003-032100)
- [ADULT; 12 men aged 19–23]
  - Daytime light from 09:00 to 12:00 at <10, 100, 300, 900 or 2,700 lx.
  - A later 300-lux night exposure suppressed melatonin after the <10, 100 and 300-lx days, but not after the 900 or 2,700-lx days.
  - A companion study found bluish-white daytime light prevented the suppression.
  — [Kozaki et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26141542/); [companion study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304394015303542)
- [ADOLESCENT; review of an adolescent study, n=15]
  - More light on the previous day predicted earlier sleep onset and longer sleep.
  - A later last exposure to >10 lux predicted later onset and less sleep.
  - Less afternoon/early-evening light went with later onset.
  - More late-evening light went with less total sleep.
  — [Ricketts et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10693907/)
- [ADOLESCENT; observational; n=75, 17.17±0.83 years, 54% male; actigraphy with light sensor over 2 weeks of school term and 2 weeks of vacation] More regular day-to-day light exposure (Light Regularity Index) was associated with more regular sleep, longer sleep and earlier sleep timing. — [Hand et al. 2023, Sleep 46(8):zsad001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10424172/)
- [ADULT; observational; UK Biobank n=86,772, mean age 62.4; wrist light sensor]
  - More daytime light was associated with lower risk of depression, PTSD, psychosis and self-harm.
  - More night light was associated with higher risk of depression, anxiety, PTSD, psychosis, bipolar disorder and self-harm.
  - Greater daytime light attenuated the association between night light and poorer mental health.
  - Median night light was 1.44 lux; mean daytime light was 1,380 lux.
  — [Burns et al. 2023, Nature Mental Health 1:853–862](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44220-023-00135-8)
- [OLDER ADULT; home crossover RCT; n=36, aged ≥60] Longer morning exposure to blue-enriched light improved rest-activity rhythm stability. More time above 2,500 lx went with earlier bedtimes. Evening light went with longer sleep latency and lower sleep efficiency. — [Barroggi Constantino et al. 2025, GeroScience](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-025-01506-y)
- [CONSENSUS; healthy adults] Daytime target ≥250 lux melanopic EDI at the eye, measured vertically at ~1.2 m, preferably from daylight. Circadian responses span roughly 1–1,000 lux m-EDI. — [Brown et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pbio.3001571)
- [REVIEW] Natural daylight exposure mitigates the effects of evening artificial light. — [Bauducco et al. 2024, Sleep Med Rev](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.101933)
- [CONSENSUS; National Sleep Foundation 2024 Delphi panel] Daytime light may moderate responses to evening light. — [Hartstein et al. 2024, Sleep Health](https://www.sleephealthjournal.org/article/S2352-7218(24)00090-1/fulltext)

### Inferences
- **Classroom light is probably not enough.** The protective effects in adults needed fairly bright days (≥900 lx for 3 h in the morning; ~4 h/day outdoors). Ordinary classrooms and homes (~300–500 lux horizontal, <200 lux m-EDI at the eye) are unlikely to count as a "bright day." The teen should build up outdoor time across the commute, breaks and after-school activity. A reasonable working target is at least 1–2 h/day outdoors; this is an inference, since no adolescent dose data exist.
- **What 250 lux m-EDI looks like (calculated from the mDER tables in Q3):**
  - ≈250 lux of daylight at the eye;
  - ≈265–290 lux of a 6500K LED;
  - ≈350–400 lux of 4000K;
  - ≈455–610 lux of 2700–3000K.
  - So bright, cool-white light (≥4000K) suits daytime home study.
- **Regularity.** A similar light pattern on school days and weekends may matter as well as the total amount (Hand 2023; association only).
- **Size of the effect.** The buffering effect is real but moderate and variable (41% vs 53% suppression in Hébert). A bright day helps, but it does not cancel out a bright evening.
- **Winter.** At 55–60°N in winter, daylight is limited to about 6–7 h around midday, so outdoor time at school breaks becomes the main daytime source.

### Gaps
- No adolescent trial was found that increased daytime light and then measured evening light sensitivity or sleep onset. The adolescent evidence is correlational.
- The dose (lux × hours) of daytime light needed to blunt evening sensitivity in teens is unknown.
- No measurements of melanopic light in Russian classrooms were retrieved.

## 3. Evening light: adolescent sensitivity, the Brown 2022 targets, warm vs cool, overhead vs low lamps

### Takeaway
Evening light suppresses melatonin and delays the body clock in a dose-dependent way, with very large differences between individuals. Extra sensitivity is clear in early and mid-puberty, while late/post-pubertal teens, like most 16-year-olds, look roughly adult-like: Crowley 2015 found reduced sensitivity in the older group, and Nagare 2019 found no significant adolescent–adult difference. The best-supported target is the Brown 2022 consensus: ≤10 lux melanopic EDI at the eye during the 3 h before bed, which means roughly ≤20 lux of warm 2700–3000K light, achieved with dim, warm, low lamps instead of bright white overhead light. Evidence is **strong–moderate** for effects on melatonin and clock timing, and **weak–moderate** that dimming changes real sleep onset by more than minutes to tens of minutes.

### Cited Findings
*Adolescent sensitivity*
- [ADOLESCENT; lab; 38 pre/mid-pubertal (9.1–14.7 years) vs 29 late/post-pubertal (11.5–15.9 years)]
  - Design: 1 h of 0.1, 15, 150 and 500 lux on consecutive nights.
  - Evening sessions (from 23:00, n=39), melatonin suppression, pre/mid vs late/post:
    - 15 lux: 9.2±20.5% vs −5.3±17.7%;
    - 150 lux: 26.0±17.7% vs 12.5±17.3%;
    - 500 lux: 36.9±11.4% vs 23.9±21.7%;
    - all p<.05.
  - At 60 min: 11.5 / 47.3 / 60.7% (pre/mid) vs −8.9 / 20.9 / 39.5% (late/post).
  - Early-morning sessions (03:00, n=28) did not differ between groups.
  - The authors note that 15 lux is ineffective in adults. They conclude that older adolescents' later timing more likely reflects greater evening light exposure than greater sensitivity.
  - They cite polling: 96% of adolescents use technology in the hour before bed, and 72% use phones.
  — [Crowley et al. 2015, JCEM](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4702443/)
- [ADOLESCENT vs ADULT; lab; 18 adolescents aged 13–18, 23 adults aged 24–55]
  - Design: night exposures of 40–1,000 lux at the cornea, 2700K vs 6500K, lasting 0.5–3 h.
  - Light level, spectrum and duration all had significant effects.
  - The hypothesis of greater adolescent sensitivity to short-wavelength light was **not** supported. Thresholds and half-saturation levels were consistently, but not significantly, lower in adolescents.
  - Mean suppression over the 3-h session: adolescents 18.9% (2700K) vs 24.6% (6500K); adults 17.9% vs 24.8%.
  - Suppression grew with duration: 10.9% at 0.5 h, 18.4% at 1 h, 20.8% at 1.5 h, 29.5% at 3 h.
  - At the two lowest stimulus levels (the low end of the 40–1,000-lux range), suppression was not significantly above 10% at any duration.
  - An earlier study from the same group found more suppression from 5600K than 2700K light (43% vs 29%) in adolescents only.
  — [Nagare et al. 2019, J Biol Rhythms](https://doi.org/10.1177/0748730419828056); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30803301/)
- [REVIEW] Children suppress melatonin more than their parents, and sensitivity declines across development. The late-pubertal phase-response curve resembles the adult one. — [Ricketts et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10693907/)
- [ADULT; n=55, aged 18–30]
  - Design: 5-h evening exposures from 10 to 2,000 lux.
  - The group ED50 (light level causing half-maximal suppression) was 24.6 lux, or 13.5 lux in the first hour. Individual ED50s ranged from 6 to 350 lux, a more than 50-fold spread.
  - 10, 30 and 50 lux delayed apparent melatonin onset by 22, 77 and 109 min.
  - The four most sensitive people had >50% suppression at 10 lux.
  - **Conflict:** this sensitivity is higher than Nagare 2019 found at its lowest levels (no significant >10% suppression). Methods differed (5-h vs ≤3-h exposures, different metrics).
  — [Phillips et al. 2019, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1901824116)

*Brown 2022 consensus targets*
- [CONSENSUS; written for healthy adults]
  - Evening, from at least 3 h before bedtime: ≤10 lux melanopic EDI at the eye, from a spectrum low in short wavelengths.
  - During sleep: ≤1 lux m-EDI (≤10 lux if vision is needed at night).
  - Daytime: ≥250 lux m-EDI.
  - All measured vertically at eye level (~1.2 m).
  - Typical evening home lighting is ~30 photopic lux.
  - Warm 2700–3000K LEDs with an mDER below 0.35 can meet the 10-lux m-EDI limit at ~30 lux.
  - About 50% of evening situations in homes already meet ≤10 lux m-EDI.
  — [Brown et al. 2022, PLOS Biol 20(3):e3001571](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pbio.3001571)

*Real homes and evening-lighting experiments*
- [ADULT; 59 homes measured]
  - Predicted evening melatonin suppression was ≥50% in 48% of homes, ≥20% in 73% and ≥80% in 15%.
  - In the average home, suppression could range from 0% to 87% depending on individual sensitivity.
  - Evening light above a person's own average went with more wakefulness in the 90 min after bedtime (β=0.89).
  - Homes lit by LED or fluorescent sources had ~2× the melanopic light of incandescent homes (M:P ratio 0.55 vs 0.44).
  — [Cain et al. 2020, Sci Rep 10:19110](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-75622-4)
- [ADULT; n=8] One week of maximised (~65 lux) vs minimised (~3 lux) home lighting during the 4 h before bed. DLMO was 1 h 03 min later after the bright week, in every participant. The dim condition used a single remote lamp and/or the TV. — [Burgess & Molina 2014, Chronobiol Int](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4053688/)
- [ADULT; randomized crossover; n=24]
  - 2 h of 5000K LED light at 160 lux before bed lengthened sleep latency by 10.6 min compared with 5000K fluorescent light.
  - Lowering the colour temperature made no significant difference.
  - Restricting devices for 2 h before bed shortened sleep latency by 6.1 min.
  — [Lu et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41243216/)
- [ADULT men; n=72]
  - Design: 4 h of evening display light at four brightness levels, each in a low-melanopic and a high-melanopic version.
  - Low-melanopic light reduced melatonin suppression, advanced melatonin onset by ~0.5 h, and shortened sleep latency (significant only at the 284 cd/m² level).
  - Across ~5–150 lux m-EDI, sleep latency rose ~50%, the melatonin area under the curve fell ~50%, and DLMO was ~1 h later.
  - Effects appeared even below 10 lux m-EDI.
  — [Schöllhorn et al. 2023, Commun Biol 6:228](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-023-04598-4)
- [ADOLESCENT; n=6] Two late nights in evening room light (combined with sleep restriction) blocked or reversed the advance produced by morning bright light. — [Crowley et al. 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30084658/)

### Inferences
- **Sensitivity.** Treat the 16-year-old as roughly adult-like in sensitivity, but possibly at the sensitive end. Individual thresholds vary 50-fold, and some people respond to ~10 lux.
- **What 10 lux m-EDI means in ordinary lux (calculated from the mDER tables):**
  - ≈21–24 lux of 2700K LED;
  - ≈18–22 lux of 3000K;
  - ≈14–16 lux of 4000K;
  - ≈10.5–11.5 lux of 6500K.
  - Typical ~30-lux evening lighting from standard warm LEDs therefore gives about 12–17 lux m-EDI, slightly over the limit.
- **Warm and dim, both.** Warm colour alone is not enough if the room stays bright: in Lu 2025, lowering the colour temperature at 160 lux made no significant difference, and in Nagare 2019 both spectra suppressed melatonin at higher levels.
- **Practical evening settings, starting ~3 h before planned lights-out:**
  - Switch off overhead/ceiling lights, especially cool-white ones.
  - Use 1–2 shaded lamps at ≤2700K (2200K or "candle" settings where available), placed low (below eye level) and to the side.
  - For homework, use a shaded warm desk lamp aimed at the page.
  - Aim for roughly ≤20 lux at the eye with warm light: the room should feel dim, and reading should need the task lamp.
  - In the last hour, use the dimmest settings.
  - Avoid a bright bathroom light during the bedtime routine.
- **Overhead vs low lamps.** No direct study was found. The target is vertical light at the eye, so ceiling lights that flood the walls and field of view probably add more light at eye level than a shaded low lamp. Burgess & Molina reached ~3 lux with a single remote lamp.
- **Expected benefit.** Dim evenings prevent a measurable clock delay (~1 h of DLMO in adults over a week) and protect the effect of morning light (Crowley 2018). The direct gain in sleep onset is probably modest, from minutes to tens of minutes.

### Gaps
- No adolescent field trial of dimming home lighting was found; Burgess & Molina (n=8) and Cain 2020 are adult studies.
- No study directly compared overhead lighting with low lamps.
- mDER values for specific consumer bulbs, especially 2200K and amber "sleep" modes, were not found. The conflict between Brown's <0.35 example and manufacturer tables is unresolved.
- Crowley 2015's late/post-pubertal group was aged 11.5–15.9, so direct data on 16–19-year-olds are thin.
- The brief's "Nagare 2017" could not be matched exactly. The closest matches are:
  - Nagare et al. 2018, Lighting Res Technol 51:530 (adolescent vs adult, duration and spectrum); its key result was retrieved only via the 2019 paper.
  - Nagare et al. 2019, J Biol Rhythms (above).
  - A 2017 SLEEP abstract on iPad Night Shift (Q4).
- The accuracy of smartphone lux-meter apps for checking evening light levels was not researched.

## 4. Screens as a light source: light vs content/arousal/displacement, Night Shift and filters, brightness, real melatonin suppression

### Takeaway
Screens emit enough light to suppress melatonin: about 20–40% after 1–2 h of tablet use by teens at home, and much less with dim, warm settings. Yet the measured effect of screen light on actual sleep onset is small, around 2–10 min. In a 2024 polysomnography study, adolescents' melatonin recovered and their sleep was unaffected when smartphone reading ended ~50 min before bed. Expert consensus holds that content, arousal and displacement of sleep time — not the light — are the main problems. Night Shift-type filters reduce suppression only modestly and did not improve sleep in an RCT, and lowering brightness matters at least as much as warming the colour. Evidence is **moderate** that the light component is small, and **moderate** that colour filters alone do not help sleep.

### Cited Findings
- [CONSENSUS; NSF 2024 Delphi panel]
  - Consensus that screen use and pre-sleep content impair children's and adolescents' sleep.
  - **No consensus** that screen light itself impairs sleep.
  - Blue-light filtering showed minimal improvements.
  - Reducing interactive screen use in the evening generally works.
  — [Hartstein et al. 2024, Sleep Health](https://www.sleephealthjournal.org/article/S2352-7218(24)00090-1/fulltext)
- [REVIEW] Effects of evening screen light on sleep latency are consistently small despite clear melatonin suppression:
  - +3.3 min for a bright vs dim tablet in adolescents (Heath 2014; the bright screen did raise alertness);
  - −1.9 min in van der Lely 2015 (adolescents);
  - +9.9 min for a light-emitting e-reader over 5 nights in adults (Chang 2015), with DLMO ~90 min later.
  - Other mechanisms (arousal, displacement, two-way effects between sleep and screen use) matter, and daylight exposure mitigates evening-light effects.
  — [Bauducco et al. 2024, Sleep Med Rev](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.101933); [full-text PDF](https://researchnow-admin.flinders.edu.au/ws/portalfiles/portal/116753136/Bauducco_Bidirectional_P2024.pdf)
- [REVIEW] Light intensity, more than spectrum, likely dominates real-world device effects. There is not yet clear evidence that dim device light causes the adolescent delay in sleep onset. — [Ricketts et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10693907/)
- [ADOLESCENT + ADULT; lab with polysomnography; 33 boys aged 15.42±0.97, 35 men aged ~21.5]
  - Design: 90 min of reading on a smartphone without a filter, with the built-in filter, or from a printed book. Room light was 4–5 lux, and reading ended ~50 min before bed.
  - Unfiltered smartphone reading reduced melatonin immediately afterwards in both age groups.
  - Adolescents' melatonin recovered by bedtime; adults' did not.
  - Adolescents' sleep and overnight memory consolidation were unaffected. Adults lost N3 (deep) sleep in the first quarter of the night.
  - The authors advise no phones in the last hour before bed.
  — [Höhn et al. 2024, Brain Commun 6(3):fcae173](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11154150/)
- [YOUNG ADULT; RCT; n=167, aged 18–24; 7 nights; actigraphy]
  - Groups: iPhone with Night Shift, iPhone without Night Shift, or no phone in the hour before bed.
  - No overall differences between groups.
  - Exploratory: among those sleeping more than 6.8 h, the no-phone group had better sleep efficiency (p=.014) and less wake after sleep onset (p=.013) than the Night Shift group.
  — [Duraccio et al. 2021, Sleep Health](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33867308/)
- [ADOLESCENT; home study; ~20 teens aged 15–17]
  - Using their own self-luminous devices in the evening suppressed melatonin by 22.8±6% after 1 h and 38.3±6% after 2 h, compared with an orange-glasses control.
  - Comparison data in the paper: young adults using iPads at maximum brightness had 7% suppression at 1 h and 23% at 2 h (Wood 2013).
  - 70-inch TVs did not suppress melatonin.
  — [Figueiro & Overington 2016, Lighting Res Technol 48:966](https://doi.org/10.1177/1477153515584979); [PDF](https://www.usailighting.com/stuff/contentmgr/files/1/1b77b519ee2eabd100549138fc41783a/misc/self_luminousdevices_figueiro.pdf); [Wood et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22850476/)
- [ADULT; lab; n=12]
  - 2 h of iPad use at maximum brightness. Melatonin suppression:
    - 41% in a blue-light (goggles) positive-control condition;
    - 10% with Night Shift "more warm";
    - 17% with Night Shift "less warm".
  - The two Night Shift settings did not differ significantly.
  - The authors conclude brightness must be reduced as well as the colour shifted.
  — [Nagare, Plitnick & Figueiro 2019, Lighting Res Technol 51:373](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561503/)
- [Pilot; n=6; SLEEP 2017 abstract]
  - Suppression after 1 h / 2 h: 35% / 52% under blue light; 16% / 20% with Night Shift "less warm"; 8% / 12% with "more warm".
  - Light reaching the cornea from the iPad was below 15 lux.
  — [Nagare et al. 2017 abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepj/zsx050.782)
- [ADULT men; n=72] Low-melanopic display light, compared with high-melanopic, reduced melatonin suppression, advanced melatonin onset by ~0.5 h, and shortened sleep latency (significant only at the brightest 284 cd/m² level). — [Schöllhorn et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-023-04598-4)
- [ADULT; randomized crossover; n=24] Restricting devices for 2 h before bed shortened sleep latency by 6.1 min. — [Lu et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41243216/)

### Inferences
- **Typical suppression.** Real-world screen use causes roughly 10–40% melatonin suppression after 1–2 h. It depends mainly on brightness, viewing distance and duration. Phones and tablets held close at high brightness are the main concern; a TV across the room matters much less.
- **Phones vs individual sensitivity.** A phone at normal brightness delivers about 15 lux or less to the cornea (Nagare abstract). That is near the adult group ED50 of ~25 lux (Phillips 2019), so it is enough to matter for sensitive individuals but not for everyone.
- **Screen settings, if the teen uses screens in the evening:**
  - brightness at the lowest comfortable level, set manually rather than on auto;
  - Night Shift / Night Light scheduled at maximum warmth;
  - dark mode;
  - a larger viewing distance (TV or laptop rather than a phone at 20–30 cm).
  - A colour filter alone is not enough.
- **Bigger gains elsewhere.** For his specific problems (falling asleep, night awakenings), the larger gain is likely from ending interactive screen use ~1 h before lights-out. That works through content, arousal and displacement, which the screen-behaviour researcher covers, more than through filters.
- **A 60-min buffer.** Höhn 2024 suggests a 16-year-old's melatonin recovers within ~50 min after screen light stops, so a 60-min screen-free buffer before lights-out is a reasonable light-based rule.

### Gaps
- The exact n in Figueiro & Overington 2016 is not confirmed (about 18–20).
- Höhn 2024's results for the built-in-filter condition were not extracted.
- Participant ages in the 2017 Nagare SLEEP abstract were not extracted.
- There is no adolescent RCT of Night Shift or other filters with sleep outcomes; Duraccio 2021 studied 18–24-year-olds.
- No adolescent data were found for modern OLED phones or front-lit e-ink readers.

## 5. Blue-blocking (amber/orange) glasses

### Takeaway
In healthy people, blue-blocking glasses have not been shown to improve sleep:
- a 2023 Cochrane review (17 RCTs, adults) found very-low-certainty, inconsistent evidence;
- a 2025 meta-analysis of three double-blind crossover RCTs found no significant change in sleep latency, total sleep, efficiency or time awake after sleep onset (WASO);
- the only adolescent trial (13 boys, 1 week) showed less melatonin suppression and lower alertness before bed, but no change in sleep.

Physiologically meaningful filtering needs dark orange or red wraparound lenses (filtering density mDFD ≥1), such as the ~$12 UVEX SCT-Orange. Clear "blue-light" lenses and pale amber lenses do almost nothing, and orange lenses worn in the morning delay the clock. Grades: **weak / not supported** for sleep outcomes; **moderate** for the physiological effect of dark-orange lenses.

### Cited Findings
- [ADOLESCENT; crossover; 13 healthy boys aged 15–17]
  - Design: 1 week each of blue-blocking vs clear lenses during evening LED-screen use, then a lab evening with LED screens.
  - Blue blockers reduced screen-induced melatonin suppression (higher melatonin from 90 to 5 min before sleep).
  - They also reduced vigilant attention and subjective alertness before bedtime.
  - No change in polysomnographic sleep or morning measures; DLMO did not differ.
  — [van der Lely et al. 2015, J Adolesc Health 56:113–119](https://www.jahonline.org/article/S1054-139X(14)00324-3/fulltext)
- [SYSTEMATIC REVIEW; Cochrane 2023; adults]
  - 17 RCTs, each with 5–156 participants; follow-up up to 5 weeks.
  - Sleep quality: very low certainty. Six RCTs (148 participants) were inconsistent — three found improvement and three found no difference.
  - Alertness: effect unknown (2 RCTs, 42 participants).
  - Adverse effects were infrequent: depressive symptoms, headache, discomfort.
  - Trials pooled very different lens types, including clear lenses.
  — [Singh et al. 2023, Cochrane CD013244.pub2](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD013244.pub2); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37593770/); [Cochrane news](https://www.cochrane.org/about-us/news/blue-light-filtering-spectacles-probably-make-no-difference-eye-strain-eye-health-or-sleep)
- [META-ANALYSIS; adults; 3 double-blind crossover RCTs, n=49 (insomnia n=14, athletes n=15, healthy n=20)]
  - All results non-significant:
    - sleep-onset latency −4.86 min (95% CI −20.23 to 10.52);
    - total sleep +8.75 min (−35.31 to 52.82);
    - sleep efficiency −0.61;
    - WASO −1.47.
  - The authors also cite an earlier meta-analysis (Shechter) reporting modest, mainly subjective gains, and Hester 2021, which suggested potential benefit in circadian misalignment.
  — [Luna-Rangel et al. 2025, Front Neurol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12668929/)
- [LAB TESTING + REVIEW of 26 commercial glasses; conflict of interest: co-authors from f.lux Software]
  - Filtering density (mDFD) ranged from 0.01 to 3.43. Only mDFD ≥1 justifies a "blue-blocking" label.
  - UVEX tints: SCT-Orange 1.41; grey 1.04; amber 0.28.
  - Dark-orange lenses — Noir ARG, LowBlueLights (1.55), UVEX SCT-Orange (1.41), Melatonin Shades (1.37), Chronoptic (1.35; best M/P ratio and colour rendering) and Night Swannies (1.34) — let through 1–12 lux m-EDI at 51–115 photopic lux.
  - Others: UVEX Espresso 1.15; Maui Jim grey 0.94; Amazon Basics 0.83; TheraSpecs 0.75; Noir ABL 0.66; Gamma Ray Computer 0.64; 3M Yellow 0.39; Axon FL41 0.28; GUNNAR PPK 0.18; BluTech 0.14.
  - Clear "blue-blocking" lenses (Felix Gray, RetinaShield, Gamma Ray Classic, TechShield, Crizal Avance/Prevencia) were insufficient even with dim phones.
  - 15 of 16 field studies used dark orange lenses (mDFD ≥1.34) in wraparound frames, in people aged 15–71. Each improved at least one sleep or circadian outcome.
  - Correct timing is critical, and misuse can be counterproductive.
  — [Glickman et al. 2025, TVST 14(7):25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12315928/)
- [LAB; 50 lenses tested] Orange tints offer the best balance for circadian use; red tints block the most. — [Mason et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34983271/)
- [ADOLESCENT] Orange glasses worn from waking until ~15:00 delayed DLMO by ~30 min. — [Figueiro & Rea 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3349218/)
- [CONSENSUS] Blue-light filtering showed minimal improvements for children's and adolescents' sleep. — [Hartstein et al. 2024 (NSF)](https://www.sleephealthjournal.org/article/S2352-7218(24)00090-1/fulltext)

### Inferences
- **For whom.** Not a first-line tool for a healthy teen. They may be worth trying if he cannot control evening light (a shared, brightly lit family room, a bright bathroom, late homework) and is an evening type with delayed timing. This niche is supported by physiology (van der Lely), not by proven sleep outcomes.
- **Lens specs that matter:**
  - a dark orange or red tint with mDFD ≥1, meaning roughly ≥90% less melanopic light (because mDFD is a log10 scale);
  - wraparound frames or side shields;
  - published transmission data or independent testing.
  - Avoid clear "blue-light" coatings, yellow or amber "computer" tints and FL-41 lenses (mDFD ≤0.83).
  - The ~$12 UVEX SCT-Orange tested about as well as $89–150 branded glasses (mDFD 1.41 vs 1.34 for Night Swannies).
- **How to use.** Put them on ~2–3 h before planned lights-out, matching Brown's 3-h window, and keep them on until lights-out. Never wear them in the morning or during the day, because that delays the clock (Figueiro & Rea). Glasses do nothing about arousal from screen content.
- **What to expect.** Less melatonin suppression and slightly lower alertness before bed. Any gain in sleep onset is likely small: about 5 min in pooled RCTs, with a confidence interval that includes zero.

### Gaps
- No adolescent RCT lasting more than 1 week with sleep-onset outcomes, and no trials in adolescents with delayed sleep timing.
- Details of the Shechter meta-analysis and the Hester 2021 review were not retrieved.
- Adolescent adherence to wearing orange glasses, and how socially acceptable teens find them, were not studied in the sources found.
- Prices for LowBlueLights, Chronoptic, Melatonin Shades and Noir ARG were not retrieved.
- Glickman 2025 has an industry conflict of interest (f.lux).
- The newest synthesis found is from 2025; no 2026 trials were found.

## 6. Light-therapy lamps (~10,000 lux) and light glasses for delayed sleep timing in adolescents

### Takeaway
The AASM gives only a **weak** recommendation, based on low-quality evidence from one non-blinded adolescent RCT. It covers bright light after waking, combined with behavioral measures, for children and adolescents with delayed sleep–wake phase disorder (DSWPD). That combined programme moved weekday sleep onset ~40 min earlier. However, the best device trial in teens (Re-Timer green vs red light, n=60) found no advantage for the active light — all groups improved — so much of the benefit may come from the gradually earlier wake schedule and dim evenings.

Lamps are generally safe: in healthy young adults, side effects were mild, transient and no more frequent than with dim red light. They do require screening for:
- a personal or family history of bipolar disorder or mania;
- eye disease;
- photosensitizing drugs.

Used in the evening, or too early relative to a very delayed clock, they shift the clock the wrong way.

### Cited Findings
*Guideline and trials*
- [GUIDELINE] AASM 2015 recommendation for children/adolescents with DSWPD: light therapy after waking, together with behavioral treatments.
  - Strength WEAK FOR; evidence quality LOW; benefits clearly outweigh harms.
  - Based on one non-blinded RCT (Gradisar 2011; n=40 analysed; ages 13–18).
  - Protocol: light ≥30 min (maximum 2 h) from sunlight or a ~1,000-lux lamp, starting at natural wake time and moving 30 min earlier per day to 06:00, then a regular rise time of 06:30–07:30.
  - Results: weekday sleep onset −42 min, weekday wake −23 min, weekend sleep onset −93.9 min.
  - For adults with DSWPD, light alone received no recommendation.
  — [Auger et al. 2015, J Clin Sleep Med 11(10):1199–1236](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/full/10.5664/jcsm.5100); [PDF](https://www.aasm.org/resources/clinicalguidelines/crswd-intrinsic.pdf)
- [ADOLESCENT RCT; n=49 with DSPD; 14.6±1.0 years; 53% male]
  - CBT plus bright-light therapy vs waitlist.
  - Improvements after treatment: d=0.65–1.24. At 6 months (n=15): d=0.24–1.53.
  - Still meeting DSPD criteria: 82% of the waitlist vs 13% of the treatment group.
  - The effects of individual components cannot be separated, and DLMO was not measured.
  — [Gradisar et al. 2011, SLEEP 34:1671–1680](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3208844/)
- [ADOLESCENT/YOUNG ADULT RCT; n=60; ages 13–24, mean 15.9]
  - Design: 3 weeks of Re-Timer green light (~507 nm) vs red light (~643 nm), each with or without morning activity. Light was used for 30–60 min immediately on rising, starting at natural wake time and moving 30 min earlier per day to 06:00, plus dim light for 2 h before bed.
  - No differences between groups. All groups improved: sleep timing d=0.30–0.46; sleep latency d=0.32; daytime functioning d=0.45–0.87. Gains held at 3 months.
  - 38% needed further treatment.
  - The authors note that prior trials used 10,000 lux at ~50 cm for 30–45 min.
  — [Richardson et al. 2018, Sleep Med 45:114–123](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleep.2018.02.001)
- [ADOLESCENT pilot] The Re-Timer research list includes a pilot in ten 13–15-year-olds (30 min each morning for 2 weeks); the outcomes were not retrieved. — [Re-Timer research list](https://www.retimer.com.au/research-retimer/)
- [ADULT; n=18 after sleep deprivation] Luminette light glasses (~2,000 lux) and a 10,000-lux light box produced similar gains in alertness. — [Comtet et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6889287/)
- [REVIEW 2021] Light-emitting glasses deliver about 500–2,000 lux. — [Chronobiology in Medicine 2021](https://chronobiologyinmedicine.org/journal/view.php?doi=10.33069%2Fcim.2021.0019)

*How to use, and safety*
- [CLINICAL GUIDANCE]
  - 10,000 lux with minimal UV, within the first hour of waking, for 20–30 min, at 41–61 cm, without looking directly into the lamp.
  - Lamps are not FDA-regulated.
  - Use caution with bipolar disorder and eye disease.
  — [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/seasonal-affective-disorder/in-depth/seasonal-affective-disorder-treatment/art-20048298)
- Intensity falls steeply with distance: 10,000 lux at 30 cm becomes ~2,500 lux at 60 cm. Evening use disrupts sleep. — [Dutch light-therapy protocol](https://www.kenniscentrumbipolairestoornissen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Light-Therapy-in-Bipolar-Disorders-Protocol-Dutch-Foundation-for-Bipolar-Disorders.pdf)
- [TASK-FORCE REVIEW; focused on bipolar disorder]
  - Side effects (headache, eye strain, nausea, over-activation, switch into hypomania) are generally mild and transient.
  - Contraindications: mania, mixed states, rapid cycling, retinal disease, and photosensitizing drugs (e.g., sulphonamides, chloroquine, St John's wort).
  - In the LUBI trial (bipolar depression), 2 of 34 patients switched into hypomania, and 20.6% had subsyndromal or full hypomania.
  - A meta-analysis and a 5-year follow-up found no eye harm.
  — [ISBD task force 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12291220/)
- [META-ANALYSIS of RCTs in bipolar depression] Mood switches occurred in 3.0% with light vs 1.6% of controls (OR 1.30, 95% CI 0.38–4.44). — [2020 meta-analysis](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7265610/)
- [YOUNG ADULT; healthy; single 30-min session] 10,000 lux vs dim red light: side-effect rates did not differ (eye strain 22% vs 23%; blurred vision 12% vs 13%). — [Botanov & Ilardi 2013, PLoS One](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0075893)
- Photosensitizing medicines to check for include fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines (including doxycycline), sulfonamides, antimalarials, some NSAIDs and others. Avoid lamps with retinal disease. Hypomania is rare. Clinician supervision is recommended when treating depression. — [Yale Winter Depression Research Clinic](https://medicine.yale.edu/psychiatry/research/clinics-and-programs/winter-depression/risks/)
- **Risk of the wrong direction:** in adolescents, bright light from ~2.7 h before to ~2.6 h after habitual bedtime produced large delays, and the crossover from delay to advance was ~0.2 h before mid-sleep. — [Crowley & Eastman 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5972384/)

### Inferences
- **Can a healthy 16-year-old use a lamp on his own?** Probably yes for morning use, after a one-time check with a parent for contraindications:
  - a personal or family history of bipolar disorder or mania;
  - current depression;
  - eye or retinal disease;
  - photosensitizing medicines such as doxycycline.
- **When professional evaluation is warranted:**
  - before use, if any of the contraindications above apply;
  - if delayed sleep timing persists and impairs school functioning despite ~3–4 weeks of consistent morning light, dim evenings and a regular schedule. The AASM recommendation applies to diagnosed DSWPD treated alongside behavioral therapy, and 38% of Richardson's sample needed further treatment.
- **How to use it:**
  - School days: 20–30 min at the rated 10,000-lux distance at breakfast, within the first hour after waking. The lamp goes to the side at eye level and is not stared at.
  - Sitting farther away cuts intensity about 4-fold (Dutch protocol; Lumie spec). Either keep to the rated distance or lengthen the session; the dose equivalence is not established.
  - Never use it within ~3 h of bedtime.
  - If he is very delayed, start at his natural weekend wake time and move earlier by ~30 min/day (the Gradisar/Richardson protocols).
  - Pair it with dim evenings (Q3) and earlier bedtimes; light without a bedtime change is undermined (Crowley 2018).
- **Choosing a lamp:**
  - Prioritise lux at a realistic distance (≥30–35 cm) and a large panel over a headline "10,000 lux" that often applies only at 12–20 cm.
  - Choose UV-filtered white light.
  - Rough price tiers: ~$40–70 (Verilux), ~$145–190 (Carex), $279–349 premium.
  - Wearable light glasses ($179–220) are convenient but lower-output, and have no teen-specific evidence of superiority.
- **What to expect.** As part of a package, weekday sleep onset ~30–45 min earlier over several weeks (Gradisar −42 min; Misiunaite's estimate ≥30–36 min). The lamp's own contribution is uncertain.

### Gaps
- No trial isolates the effect of a 10,000-lux lamp in adolescents, and there is no adolescent head-to-head comparison of a lamp with outdoor light.
- The results of the Re-Timer adolescent pilot were not retrieved.
- No independent measurements of consumer light-box output were found; only manufacturer specs.
- Adolescent-specific safety data are sparse; most safety evidence is from adults or people with bipolar disorder.
- The light source used in Gradisar 2011 (sunlight or a ~1,000-lux lamp) is as summarised by the AASM; it was not checked in the primary paper.

## 7. Dawn simulation (wake-up lights)

### Takeaway
Dawn simulators make waking easier and reduce sleep inertia and morning sleepiness in small adult trials and in one large but short child/adolescent study. They do not, however, shift circadian timing or change sleep timing, duration or quality: Giménez 2010 found no DLMO shift, and Tonetti 2015 in 15–20-year-olds found only an attention measure improved. Grades: **weak–moderate** for subjective ease of waking; **no evidence** for earlier sleep onset. The features that matter are enough light at pillow distance (studies used ~250 lux at the eye), a ~30-min ramp and flicker-free output.

### Cited Findings
- [ADULT; n=8 with difficulty waking; 30-min dawn]
  - Perceived sleep quality +1.16 points; higher alertness.
  - Arithmetic: 69.5 vs 66.9 additions. Reaction time: 0.81 vs 0.86 s.
  - Cycling time trial 4.7% faster (p=0.07).
  — [Thompson et al. 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24509892/)
- [ADULT; home study in winter; n≈23; 30-min dawn to 0 lux (control), 50 lux or 250 lux; 2 weeks per condition] Sleep inertia was shortened by ~20–25 min. There was no DLMO shift. — [Giménez et al. 2010, Chronobiol Int](https://pure.rug.nl/ws/files/6752157/2010ChronobiolIntGimenez.pdf)
- [ADULT; n=16] Less sleepiness and more activation after waking. No effect on performance, cortisol or core body temperature. — [van de Werken et al. 2010, J Sleep Res](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2869.2010.00828.x)
- [ADULT; n=17 under sleep restriction; dawn 0→250 lux] Better mood, well-being and cognition than with dim light or blue light; minimal circadian effect. — [Gabel et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23841684/)
- [CHILD/ADOLESCENT; n=103, ages 7–18; 1 week with a wake-up light vs 1 week as usual]
  - Participants woke earlier, felt more alert, got up more easily and were more alert in the second lesson.
  - Evening types benefited more.
  — [Fromm et al. 2011, Biol Rhythm Res](https://doi.org/10.1080/09291016.2010.525851)
- [ADOLESCENT; n=56, ages 15–20 (mean 17.7); 2 weeks per condition] Only the efficiency of the alerting attention network improved (45.97 vs 31.57 ms). Sleep timing, duration and quality were unchanged. — [Tonetti et al. 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25351789/)
- [ADOLESCENT with delayed sleep phase; uncontrolled; n=12] Less morning drowsiness (p=.016) and lower Epworth sleepiness scores (p=.013). Difficulty waking: p=.092 (not significant). — [Kang et al. 2007](http://koreascience.or.kr/article/ArticleFullRecord.jsp?cn=SGJSB5_2007_v46n6_545)
- [YOUNG ADULT; n=21 college students] A dawn ramp from 0 to 210 lux beat constant 40-lux light and no light on alertness, mood and reaction time. — [Zhu et al. 2020](https://jps.ecnu.edu.cn/EN/Y2020/V43/I3/526)
- [PILOT; 14 Swedish apartments with smart dusk/dawn lighting] Somewhat more consistent sleep schedules, calm waking and high acceptance. — [Aries et al. 2024, Lighting Res Technol](https://doi.org/10.1177/14771535241287075)
- [ADULT] Closed eyelids transmit about 5–9% of light. — [Mason et al. 2022, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2113290119)

### Inferences
- **What it helps with.** A dawn simulator should help with grogginess on waking (sleep inertia, "waking unrefreshed"), not with falling asleep earlier.
- **Features that matter:**
  1. Peak output of at least ~250 lux at the actual distance between face and lamp. Tested devices gave 275–395 lux at 45 cm, and moving to ~30 cm roughly doubles that.
  2. An adjustable ramp of about 30 min.
  3. Flicker-free dimming.
  4. Works offline, with no subscription or hub required.
  5. Optional: the light stays on at full brightness after the alarm.
- **Through closed eyelids** (~5–9% transmission), 250–400 lux at the pillow means roughly 12–36 lux reaches the eye — a gentle signal, not a phase-shifting dose.
- **Value.** The JALL ($35) measured nearly as bright as the $170–180 devices. The Philips HF3520 (~$100–150) and Lumie Shine 300 ($169) are better-known brands. Smart bulbs with sunrise routines (Q8) are an alternative but usually deliver less light to the face.
- **Season.** Most useful from October to March, when mornings are dark.
- **Still get bright light after waking** (window, outdoors or a lamp). A dawn simulator alone does not deliver a phase-shifting dose.

- Beyond Tonetti 2015 and Fromm 2011, there is no adolescent RCT with objective sleep-inertia or circadian outcomes. Fromm 2011 lasted one week, and its design details were not extracted.
- No device-vs-device clinical comparisons exist; the independent meter tests are single-sample consumer reviews.
- Long-term (whole-winter) effects on teens' school-day sleepiness are unknown.
- Giménez 2010's exact N (~23) and its results for each intensity were not re-verified.

## 8. Smart bulbs and tunable home lighting

### Takeaway
No adolescent trial has tested smart or tunable home lighting, so support is indirect:
- dim, warm evening light at home in adults delays the clock less (DLMO ~1 h earlier than with brighter light);
- homes lit with LEDs deliver ~2× the melanopic light of incandescent homes;
- small adult pilots of automated dim/warm evening lighting show feasibility and some benefits.

Grade: **expert opinion / indirect evidence**. Smart bulbs are best used as automation that makes "bright mornings, dim warm evenings, dark nights" effortless and consistent.

### Cited Findings
- [ADULT] Homes lit by LED or fluorescent sources had ~2× the melanopic light of incandescent homes (M:P ratio 0.55 vs 0.44). Evening light above a person's own average went with more wakefulness after bedtime. — [Cain et al. 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-75622-4)
- [ADULT; n=8] Dim (~3 lux) vs bright (~65 lux) home lighting during the 4 h before bed: DLMO was ~1 h earlier in the dim condition. — [Burgess & Molina 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4053688/)
- [ADULT; crossover; n=24] At 160 lux, lowering the colour temperature made no significant difference to sleep latency. A 5000K LED lengthened latency by 10.6 min compared with a 5000K fluorescent lamp. — [Lu et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41243216/)
- [ADULT; pilot RCT, abstract only; n=24 adults on SSRIs] 4 weeks of automatically dimmed, warm smart evening lighting reduced evening light by 21 lux, and depression improved (d=2.11). — [Fitton et al. 2024, SLEEP Advances abstract](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleepadvances/zpae070.069)
- [PILOT; 14 Swedish apartments] Smart dusk/dawn lighting brought somewhat more consistent schedules, calm waking and high acceptance. — [Aries et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/14771535241287075)
- [DEMENTIA pilots; low quality] One (n=11) found no sleep effect; another in Jordan (n=8) reported large gains. — [Tandfonline 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/24735132.2023.2183622); [Sci Rep 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-17351-0)
- [REVIEW] Tunable home lighting is described as a promising intervention for adolescents. — [Ricketts et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10693907/)

- **Suggested automation schedule** (times relative to his own wake time and bedtime):
  - On waking: bedroom and kitchen lights at 100%, 5000–6500K. Optionally, a 20–30-min sunrise ramp before the alarm.
  - Daytime home study: bright, ≥4000K.
  - From ~3 h before lights-out: switch automatically to the warmest setting (2200K if available, otherwise 2700K) at ~30–50% brightness, and use lamps rather than ceiling fixtures.
  - Final hour: the lowest level (e.g., 1–10%).
  - Night: lights off. If needed, a motion-triggered light at its lowest, warmest setting.
- **Bulb choice.** Choose bulbs that dim deeply (≤2%) and change brightness and colour temperature together. Wi-Fi bulbs (WiZ) need no hub. Three or four bulbs cost about $40–100.

- mDER values for smart bulbs at their warmest settings, and their minimum output in lux, were not found.
- Availability and pricing of Hue, WiZ and IKEA in Russia or the CIS, and of alternatives common there (e.g., Xiaomi/Yeelight, Yandex or Sber smart bulbs), were not researched.

## 9. Light during sleep: Mason 2022, standby LEDs, night lights, street light, blackout curtains and eye masks

### Takeaway
Light during sleep is physiologically active. In young adults, one night at 100 lux raised heart rate, lowered heart-rate variability (HRV), made sleep lighter and impaired next-morning insulin sensitivity. Adolescents living where outdoor night light is brighter go to bed ~29 min later and sleep ~11 min less, though that evidence is observational. The consensus target is ≤1 lux melanopic EDI at the eye during sleep. Eye masks are a cheap way to get close, but the evidence of benefit is weak: small, contested memory and alertness gains in adults (Greco et al. 2023, the study the brief calls "Greenfield 2023"). Grades: **moderate** for physiology (adult lab); **weak** for adolescents and for masks. No direct studies were found on standby LEDs, night lights or blackout curtains.

### Cited Findings
- [ADULT; lab; n=20 young adults; one night at 100 lux vs <3 lux]
  - Higher heart rate and lower HRV during the night; higher insulin resistance the next morning.
  - Proportionally more N2 sleep and less slow-wave and REM sleep.
  - Melatonin was unchanged.
  - Closed eyelids transmit about 5–9% of light.
  — [Mason et al. 2022, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2113290119)
- [CONSENSUS] During sleep: ≤1 lux m-EDI at the eye, or ≤10 lux m-EDI if vision is needed at night. — [Brown et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pbio.3001571)
- [ADOLESCENT; cross-sectional; n=10,123, ages 13–18; outdoor artificial light at night measured by satellite]
  - Highest vs lowest quartile of outdoor light: weeknight bedtime 29 min later (95% CI 15–43) and sleep 11 min shorter (2–19).
  - For each step-up in outdoor night light (one median absolute deviation), the odds of mood disorder were 1.07 and of anxiety disorder 1.10.
  — [Paksarian et al. 2020, JAMA Psychiatry](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2767698)
- [ADULT; observational; n=86,772] More night light (median 1.44 lux) was associated with higher risk of several psychiatric disorders. Daytime light attenuated these associations. — [Burns et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44220-023-00135-8)
- [ADULT; two experiments]
  - Experiment 1 (n=94, ages 18–35): 1 week each with a light-blocking eye mask vs a control mask.
    - Word-pair learning: 65.06 vs 63.87 (d=0.19).
    - Vigilance-test reaction time: 310 vs 316 ms (d=0.16).
  - Experiment 2 (n=35): the learning benefit was replicated (d=0.22) and linked to slow-wave sleep; sleep architecture did not differ.
  — [Greco et al. 2023, Sleep 46(3):zsac305](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/46/3/zsac305/6912219)
- [REANALYSIS / COMMENTARY] Word-pair benefit p=0.053; reaction time −6.2 ms (p=0.047). The practical benefit is questionable: 0–2 extra words out of 80, and 0.1–12 ms faster. — [Rhodes 2023, Sleep](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsad105)
- [ADOLESCENT; double-blind RCT; n=102, mean age 15.6]
  - 3-ms light flashes every 20 s during the last 2–3 h of sleep.
  - The flashes alone had no effect.
  - Combined with CBT, sleep onset was 50.1 min earlier and total sleep 43.3 min longer than with sham flashes plus CBT.
  - Conflict of interest: one author is an unpaid advisor to LumosTech.
  — [Kaplan et al. 2019, JAMA Netw Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2751894)
- [REVIEW] In adolescents, more late-evening light is associated with less total sleep. — [Ricketts et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10693907/)

### Inferences
- **Target.** ≤1 lux m-EDI is about ≤2 lux of warm-white light at the eye (about 1 lux of cool-white light), so the room should be close to dark. Practical steps:
  - blackout curtains or blinds if street lights, car headlights or early summer dawn reach the bed;
  - cover or turn away standby LEDs and charger lights, especially white or blue ones;
  - phone face-down and silent, or out of the room;
  - no TV or monitor left on;
  - a contoured eye mask if the room cannot be darkened (cheap, low risk, small benefit).
- **Night awakenings** (one of his complaints):
  - Do not switch on overhead lights or check the phone.
  - For bathroom trips, use a dim, motion-activated, warm/amber night light at floor level.
  - The consensus ceiling when vision is needed is ≤10 lux m-EDI, about ≤20 lux of warm light at the eye — ideally much less.
- **Season (inference).** In summer at 55–60°N dawn comes very early, so blackout matters most then; in winter, street light is the main intruder.
- **Early-morning light leakage.** Light during the last hours of sleep can affect the circadian clock (Kaplan 2019), so light leaking into the bedroom early in the morning could shift it. The direction depends on timing relative to the crossover (Q1). Light-flash sleep masks are not established self-help tools.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly measured the effect of standby LEDs, small night lights, street-light leakage or blackout curtains on sleep, in adolescents or adults.
- The eye-mask evidence is adult-only and contested; there is no adolescent trial.
- Paksarian 2020 is cross-sectional, so it cannot separate the effect of light from urban noise or lifestyle.
- Consumer availability and price of light-flash masks (LumosTech), and prices of eye masks and blackout curtains, were not researched.
- Typical night-time light levels in a teen's bedroom (lux at the pillow) were not found.
